The first half checks whether you understand the mechanics. The second half checks whether you know that ANN is a deliberate engineering trade-off with a measurable cost — rather than a limitation you were unaware of.\n</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nPart 1: How Vector Search Works — The Mechanics\n</h4>\n<div style=\"padding: 2px; border-radius: 8px; font-size: 14px\">\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>code</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">STEP BY STEP\n\n1. At INDEX time, every chunk was converted to a vector - a list\n   of, say, 1536 numbers - by an embedding model. Chunks with\n   similar meaning ended up close together in that space.\n\n2. At QUERY time, the user&#x27;s question goes through the SAME\n   embedding model and becomes a vector in the SAME space.\n\n3. Now the problem is purely geometric: find the stored vectors\n   CLOSEST to the query vector.\n\n4. &quot;Closest&quot; is defined by a similarity or distance metric -\n   usually cosine similarity, or dot product on normalised\n   vectors, which give identical rankings.\n\n5. Return the top k, with their chunk text and metadata.\n\nTHAT IS THE WHOLE IDEA. Semantic search is a nearest-neighbour\nproblem in a high-dimensional space. Everything else in this\ntopic is about doing step 3 FAST at scale.</code></pre></div>\n</div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.0rem\">\nOne detail that matters and is easy to get wrong: <strong>the query must be embedded with the same model that built the index.</strong> Two models produce vectors in unrelated coordinate systems, so mixing them yields numbers that look valid and mean nothing — and if the dimensions happen to match, there is no error at all.\n</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nPart 2: Exact Search, And Why It Stops Working\n</h4>\n<div style=\"padding: 2px; border-radius: 8px; font-size: 14px\">\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>code</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">EXACT (brute force / &quot;flat&quot;) SEARCH\n  Compare the query against EVERY vector, sort, take the top k.\n  Perfect recall - you always get the true nearest neighbours.\n\nTHE COST:\n  For each vector you compute a dot product over d dimensions.\n       work = n vectors x d dimensions\n\n  10,000 vectors  x 1536 dims  =  15 million operations   fine\n   1,000,000      x 1536       =  1.5 billion             slow\n  10,000,000      x 1536       =  15 billion PER QUERY    hopeless\n\nAt 10M vectors you are doing 15 billion multiply-adds for a\nsingle search, and you must scan the entire index for every user\nrequest. That is seconds of CPU, not milliseconds.\n\nExact search is completely fine up to roughly 100,000 vectors.\nDo not build an ANN index you do not need - it is real\ncomplexity, and &quot;flat&quot; is also your ground-truth baseline for\nmeasuring the ANN index you build later.</code></pre></div>\n</div>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nPart 3: Why Not Just Use A Spatial Index? (The Real Answer)\n</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.0rem\">\nThis is the part most candidates miss, and it is what makes the \"why approximate?\" answer complete. The obvious objection is: <em>databases solve nearest-neighbour problems with tree indexes all the time — why not here?</em>\n</p>\n<div style=\"padding: 2px; border-radius: 8px; font-size: 14px\">\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>code</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">IN 2D OR 3D, exact nearest-neighbour is easy. A k-d tree or an\nR-tree partitions space and lets you prune huge regions:\n&quot;everything in this quadrant is too far away, skip it.&quot;\n\nIN 1536 DIMENSIONS, THAT PRUNING STOPS WORKING.\n\nThis is the CURSE OF DIMENSIONALITY. As dimensions increase:\n  - Volume concentrates near the boundaries of any region, so a\n    query point is close to many partition edges at once.\n  - Distances between random points CONVERGE - everything ends\n    up roughly equally far from everything else, so the contrast\n    between &quot;near&quot; and &quot;far&quot; collapses.\n  - A tree cannot confidently prune any branch, so it ends up\n    visiting almost all of them.\n\nRESULT: a k-d tree in 1536 dimensions degenerates to a full\nscan, plus tree overhead. It is WORSE than brute force.\n\nSO: exact nearest-neighbour in high dimensions has no efficient\ndata structure. That is a mathematical fact, not an engineering\ngap. The ONLY way to get sub-linear search is to give up the\nguarantee of exactness.\n\nTHAT is why vector search is approximate. Not because engineers\nwere lazy - because exactness at this dimensionality is\nprovably expensive.</code></pre></div>\n</div>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nPart 4: What \"Approximate\" Actually Costs You\n</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.0rem\">\nANN returns <em>almost always</em> the right neighbours. Insisting on exact nearest neighbours over approximate\nvectors is FALSE PRECISION.\n\nYou are not choosing between &quot;correct&quot; and &quot;incorrect&quot;. You are\nchoosing between two approximations, one of which is 1000x\nfaster.\n\nWHAT YOU MUST DO: measure it. Build a flat index over a sample,\ntreat it as ground truth, compute recall@k. Latency spikes, or the engine falls back to brute force over the filtered subset. Always test filtered queries at production selectivity.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>The ANN search is usually not your bottleneck.</strong> In a typical RAG request, vector search is 20–80ms while generation is 1–4 seconds. Optimising the index when generation dominates is the wrong target — measure per stage first.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Recall drifts as the index grows.</strong> An HNSW graph grown incrementally over a year is not the graph you would build from scratch, and a value of efSearch tuned at one million vectors may be wrong at ten million. Re-measure periodically against a flat baseline.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>The metric must match the embedding model.</strong> Configure cosine unless the model card says inner product. If vectors are normalised, cosine, dot product and Euclidean all give identical rankings — so the choice becomes about speed and score interpretation.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Deleted vectors leave tombstones.</strong> Many stores mark rather than remove, so index size does not drop immediately and traversal efficiency degrades as tombstones accumulate. Schedule compaction.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nHow This Comes Up In Interviews\n</h4>\n<div style=\"padding: 2px; border-radius: 8px; font-size: 14px\">\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>code</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">THE QUESTION\n  &quot;How does vector search find the nearest chunks, and why is it\n   approximate rather than exact?&quot;\n\nWHAT THE INTERVIEWER IS TESTING\n  Half one: do you understand the mechanics, or is the vector\n  database a black box to you?\n  Half two - the important half: do you know ANN is a DELIBERATE\n  trade-off with a MEASURABLE cost, or did you not realise your\n  search was approximate at all?\n\nWEAK ANSWER\n  &quot;It converts the query to a vector and finds similar vectors\n   using cosine similarity.
