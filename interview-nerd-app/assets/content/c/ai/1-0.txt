If you retrieve 10 and rerank 10, you have gained almost nothing — the reranker can only reorder what it was given, and the whole point is to promote something from rank 18. Retrieve 50, rerank 50, keep 5.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Reranking after truncation.</strong> Same failure in a different place: if you truncate to the top five before reranking, you have thrown away exactly the candidates the reranker existed to rescue.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Ignoring diminishing returns.</strong> Recall gains from more candidates flatten sharply — going from 50 to 200 usually adds very little quality and multiplies latency fourfold. Sweep it rather than assuming more is better.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Not using the relevance floor.</strong> If you always pass your top five regardless of score, you have discarded the calibration benefit and your system can never abstain.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Chunk length effects.</strong> Cross-encoders have their own input limits, and a very long chunk may be truncated before scoring — so the reranker judges only its first part. Check the model's limit against your chunk size.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Forgetting deduplication.</strong> Reranking does not remove near-duplicates; three copies of the same paragraph can all score highly and consume your top three slots.
