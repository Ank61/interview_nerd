How do you decide what goes into the prompt?\"</strong>\n</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">\nThis is the last stage before generation, and it is the one people skip. Retrieval and reranking get all the attention; the actual assembly of the prompt is usually a <code>join</code> on the top-k array. But this stage decides what the model can see, in what order, and with what framing — and it is where several distinct quality problems get fixed or created.\n</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">\nThe naive answer to the question is \"take the top 8 by score\". That is not wrong, but it is incomplete in five specific ways, and the interviewer is listening for whether you know them.\n</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nStep 0: Work Out What \"8\" Actually Is\n</h4>\n<div style=\"padding: 2px; border-radius: 8px; font-size: 14px\">\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>code</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">The number is not fixed. You can easily fill your top 8 with four distinct pieces of information. Deduplicate: merge adjacent overlapping chunks by character range, and detect near-duplicate text across documents with shingling, keeping one and recording the other sources so you can still cite them all.\n</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Relevance alone gives you no diversity :</span>\nEight chunks all from section 4.2 of one document are eight views of the same fact. For a comparative question — \"how do our three regional policies differ?\" — that is a failure. <strong>Maximal Marginal Relevance (MMR)</strong> is the standard fix: iteratively select the chunk that maximises relevance <em>minus</em> a penalty for similarity to what you have already chosen. A lambda parameter trades relevance against diversity.\n</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Order matters because of lost-in-the-middle :</span>\nModels attend most reliably to the beginning and end of a long context and least reliably to the middle. So do not simply concatenate in rank order — that buries your second-best chunk in the worst position. The common pattern is a <strong>sandwich</strong>: best chunk first, worst chunks in the middle, second-best last, with the question at the very end.\n</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Bare text is ambiguous :</span>\nA chunk on its own does not say what document it came from, when it was written, or which section it belongs to. Attach that as visible metadata — title, section, date, and a citation ID — because it improves the answer, enables citations, and lets the model notice that one source is five years old.\n</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Retrieved text is untrusted :</span>\nIt came from a document someone else wrote, and it may contain instructions. It must be fenced in delimiters and declared as data in the system prompt, and it must be escaped so it cannot close its own fence.\n</li>\n</ol>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nThe Counter-Intuitive Part: Fewer Chunks Is Often Better\n</h4>\n<div style=\"padding: 2px; border-radius: 8px; font-size: 14px\">\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>code</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">The instinct is: I have room for 8, so use 8.
