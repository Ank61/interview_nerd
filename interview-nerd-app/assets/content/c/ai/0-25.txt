{"title":"Chunking Strategy","tags":["high-frequency","must-know","indexing"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">\n<strong>Chunking is splitting your documents into the pieces you will actually retrieve.</strong> A chunk is the unit of retrieval — when a user asks a question, chunks are what come back and what gets put in the prompt. So chunking decides what it is even <em>possible</em> for your system to find, which makes it one of the highest-leverage decisions in RAG and one of the most commonly asked about.\n</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nWhy Chunk At All? Four Independent Reasons\n</h4>\n<ol style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style-type: number\">\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Embedding models have input limits :</span>\nMany cap at 512 tokens, some at 8,191. A 50-page document simply cannot be embedded whole — and if you send more than the limit, most implementations <em>silently truncate</em>, so the tail is embedded as if it did not exist and becomes permanently unretrievable.\n</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Retrieval precision :</span>\nOne vector per 50-page document averages everything in it into a single point. The document is about twelve topics; the vector is about none of them clearly. Smaller pieces mean each vector represents one idea, so search can distinguish them.\n</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Context budget :</span>\nYou cannot put whole documents in the prompt. You want the relevant paragraph, not the surrounding forty pages.\n</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Cost and latency :</span>\nFewer, tighter tokens in the prompt means a cheaper and faster request.\n</li>\n</ol>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nThe Strategies, From Worst To Best\n</h4>\n<div style=\"padding: 2px; border-radius: 8px; font-size: 14px\">\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>code</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">1. Which is most enterprise\n   content.\n   WHY IT WINS: the author already decided where one idea ends\n   and the next begins. Respect that instead of guessing.\n\n5. SEMANTIC CHUNKING\n   Embed each sentence, measure similarity between consecutive\n   sentences, and split where similarity DROPS - the point where\n   the topic changes.\n   USE: unstructured prose with no headings, e.g. transcripts.\n   COST: an embedding call per sentence at index time, which is\n   real at scale. You get accurate matching without giving the model a fragment. This resolves the central tension of the whole topic and is one of the highest-value patterns available.\n</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Sentence-window retrieval :</span>\nA variation: index single sentences, and on retrieval expand to include the N sentences either side. Very precise matching, natural context. Works well for dense reference material.\n</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Hierarchical / multi-level indexing :</span>\nIndex at several granularities — document summaries, section summaries, and leaf chunks. Search the summaries first to identify the right document or section, then search within it. Useful for very large corpora and for questions that are about a document as a whole.\n</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Contextual retrieval :</span>\nBefore embedding, prepend a short LLM-generated sentence explaining how this chunk fits into its document. A chunk reading \"this is not permitted under any circumstances\" becomes retrievable once it is prefixed with \"This section of the 2026 Travel Policy concerns first-class flight bookings.\" This directly fixes the biggest weakness of chunking — that chunks are ambiguous in isolation — and is one of the strongest recent techniques. The cost is an LLM call per chunk at index time.\n</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Proposition / atomic-fact chunking :</span>\nUse an LLM to rewrite passages into standalone factual statements, each self-contained. Extremely precise retrieval, but expensive to build and it loses narrative flow, so it suits fact-lookup corpora rather than explanatory documents.\n</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\">\n<span style=\"font-weight: 600; font-size: 1.1rem\">Late chunking :</span>\nEmbed the whole document with a long-context embedding model first, so every token's representation is informed by the full document, then pool the token embeddings into chunk vectors. Chunks keep document-wide context without a per-chunk LLM call. Newer, and dependent on the embedding model supporting it.\n</li>\n</ol>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nThe Central Problem: Chunks Are Ambiguous In Isolation\n</h4>\n<div style=\"padding: 2px; border-radius: 8px; font-size: 14px\">\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>code</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">A chunk extracted from the middle of a policy document:\n\n  &quot;This is not permitted under any circumstances, and any\n   expense claimed in breach of this rule will be rejected.&quot;\n\nQUESTIONS THIS CHUNK CANNOT ANSWER:\n  What is not permitted?     &lt;- the subject was in a heading\n  Which policy is this?      &lt;- the title was 20 pages earlier\n  Does it still apply?       &lt;- no date, no version\n  Who does it apply to?      &lt;- scope was in an earlier section\n\nAND IT WILL NEVER BE RETRIEVED, because a user asking &quot;can I\nbook first class?&quot; produces a query embedding that has almost\nnothing in common with this text.\n\nTHE FIXES, in increasing order of cost:\n  1. Hold everything else constant and try three or four chunking approaches. This is a genuine experiment, and the winner is frequently not the one you expected.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Read your chunks.</strong> Sample fifty at random and read them cold. Ask: could I answer a question from this alone? If most chunks are ambiguous out of context, your problem is chunking, not embeddings.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Track boundary failures.</strong> For failing questions, check whether the answer straddles two chunks. A high rate means your boundaries or overlap are wrong.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nOperational Details People Forget\n</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-top: 1.0rem; margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style-type: disc\">\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Stable chunk IDs.</strong> Derive them from document ID plus index or a content hash, so re-indexing does not orphan citations and you can delete precisely.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Version your chunking.</strong> Store a chunkingVersion on every chunk, so when you change strategy you know what is stale and can migrate incrementally.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Changing chunking means re-indexing.</strong> There is no in-place migration — new boundaries mean new text means new embeddings. Blue-green it.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Never exceed the embedding model's limit.</strong> Validate at index time and fail loudly, because the alternative is silent truncation.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Keep the character offsets</strong> of each chunk within its source document, so you can highlight the exact passage in the original when citing it.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nHow This Comes Up In Interviews\n</h4>\n<div style=\"padding: 2px; border-radius: 8px; font-size: 14px\">\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>code</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">QUESTIONS YOU WILL ACTUALLY BE ASKED\n  &quot;How do you chunk documents?&quot;\n  &quot;How does chunking change for contracts vs chat logs vs code?&quot;\n  &quot;What happens when an answer spans two chunks?&quot;\n  &quot;How do you know your chunking strategy is any good?&quot;\n  &quot;A chunk says &#x27;this is not permitted&#x27; - what&#x27;s the problem?&quot;\n\nWHAT THE INTERVIEWER IS TESTING\n  Whether you know there is more than one strategy, whether you\n  choose by CONTENT TYPE rather than applying one everywhere,\n  and whether you understand the isolation problem - that a\n  chunk stripped of its context is often unretrievable and\n  unusable even when it contains the answer.\n\nWEAK ANSWER\n  &quot;We split documents into 1000-character chunks with 200\n   characters of overlap.&quot;\n  Why it is weak: a fixed recipe with no reasoning.
