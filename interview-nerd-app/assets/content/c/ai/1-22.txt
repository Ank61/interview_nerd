The second half has the word <strong>measurably</strong> in it, and that is the whole test. The honest answer is: <strong>sometimes, and much less often than people assume.</strong>\n</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">\nA reflection step is simple. After the model produces an answer, something looks at that answer, says what is wrong with it, and the model gets a chance to fix it. The interesting question is what does the looking.\n</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nThree Kinds, From Weakest To Strongest\n</h4>\n<div style=\"padding: 2px; border-radius: 8px; font-size: 14px\">\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>code</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">1. The model has already committed to the\n   answer, and its own reasoning is sitting right there in\n   the context supporting it. The most common critique you\n   get back is &quot;this looks correct&quot;.\n\n2. A SEPARATE CRITIC - fresh context, different job.\n   A second call that never saw the work being produced,\n   only the finished thing, and is told to find problems.\n   BETTER, because it is not attached to the answer and\n   cannot see the reasoning that produced it.\n\n3. The answer is there, and so is every step of reasoning that led to it, all of which made sense at the time. Asking it to find fault with that is asking it to disagree with a page of its own arguments. It usually does not.\n</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.0rem\">\nThere is also a real risk of it making things <strong>worse</strong>. A correct answer gets \"improved\" into a wrong one, because you asked for a change and it produced one. Research on models correcting themselves without any outside feedback has found exactly this. The practical guard is a rule: <strong>only revise if the critique names a specific problem.</strong> \"This could be clearer\" is not a reason to rewrite.\n</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nWhat Makes A Critic Step Actually Useful\n</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-top: 1.0rem; margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style-type: disc\">\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>A fresh context.</strong> It sees the output, not the working. This is most of the benefit.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>A specific checklist, not \"review this\".</strong> A vague brief produces vague comments. \"Does every number in this answer appear in the sources? Does it answer all three parts of the question? Are there any claims with no citation?\" produces findings you can act on.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Told to find problems, not to review.</strong> The wording genuinely changes what you get back.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Structured output.</strong> A list of problems with a location and a severity, plus an explicit \"no problems found\" option. Free text gets you polite hedging.</li>\n<li style=\"margin-top: 0.5rem\"><strong>Different angles if the work can fail in different ways.</strong> One checking facts, one checking completeness, one checking tone — better than three generic reviewers.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600; letter-spacing: -0.025em\">\nWhat It Costs, And When To Stop\n</h4>\n<div style=\"padding: 2px; border-radius: 8px; font-size: 14px\">\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>code</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">COST: one critique plus one revision roughly TRIPLES your\ntokens and your latency for that answer. Run your test set with and without, and record three things: quality, cost, and latency.
