{"title":"ECS vs EKS vs Fargate vs Lambda — Choosing How to Run Your Code","tags":["ECS","EKS","Lambda","Architecture"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">This comparison is asked in almost every AWS interview, and most weak answers fail at the first step: they line up ECS, EKS and Fargate as three competing products. They are not. Getting the categories right is most of the battle, and it is the part that makes the rest of the answer sound effortless.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The clarification that prevents most of the confusion</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An <strong>orchestrator</strong> is the system that decides what runs where: it places containers onto capacity, restarts them when they die, replaces them during a deployment, and keeps the number of copies you asked for. <strong>Amazon ECS</strong> (Elastic Container Service) and <strong>Amazon EKS</strong> (Elastic Kubernetes Service) are both orchestrators.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>capacity option</strong> — sometimes called a launch type — is where the containers physically run. <strong>EC2</strong> means virtual servers in your own account that you can see, size, log into and patch. <strong>AWS Fargate</strong> is <strong>serverless</strong> capacity: AWS owns and patches the machines, you never see them, and you pay per container task or pod for the CPU and memory it requests.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">So Fargate is not a competitor to ECS or EKS — it is something either of them can run on. Then answer the two real questions in order: <em>which orchestrator?</em> and <em>which capacity?</em></p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Decision one: ECS or EKS?</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Amazon ECS</strong> is AWS's own orchestrator. Its concepts are few: a <strong>task definition</strong> (the blueprint for your containers), a <strong>task</strong> (a running instance of that blueprint) and a <strong>service</strong> (keeps N tasks running behind a load balancer). A competent engineer can learn it in a day. It integrates natively with IAM roles per task, Application Load Balancers, CloudWatch, Secrets Manager and Systems Manager Parameter Store, without add-ons. There is <strong>no charge for the ECS control plane</strong> — you pay only for the compute — and there is no cluster version for you to upgrade, which removes an entire category of recurring work.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Amazon EKS</strong> runs upstream <strong>Kubernetes</strong>, the open-source container orchestrator that has become the industry standard. You get the standard API, so the same manifests, Helm charts and tools work on any Kubernetes anywhere — your laptop, another cloud, an on-premises data centre. The ecosystem is enormous: Argo CD for GitOps, Istio or Linkerd for service mesh, Prometheus for metrics, Karpenter for node provisioning, operators for databases and more. Your team's skills transfer between employers, which matters for hiring and retention.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The cost of EKS is real and should never be glossed over. There is a <strong>per-cluster hourly charge</strong> for the managed control plane. Kubernetes has a large vocabulary — pods, deployments, services, ingresses, ConfigMaps, RBAC, CRDs — and a genuine learning curve. Clusters need <strong>version upgrades</strong> on a regular cadence because each minor version has a limited support window, and upgrades can break add-ons and deprecated APIs. You will also spend real time wiring up things ECS gives you for free, such as IAM roles for service accounts (IRSA) or EKS Pod Identity, and an ingress controller.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The honest tiebreaker interviewers want to hear: <strong>team experience is a legitimate deciding factor</strong>. A team of three who already know Kubernetes deeply may ship faster on EKS than on ECS. A team of three who do not, running six services on AWS only, will almost certainly go faster and spend less on ECS. Technology choice includes the humans operating it.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Decision two: Fargate or EC2?</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This choice is about who owns the machines, and it is independent of the orchestrator choice.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Fargate.</strong> No servers to provision, patch, scale or secure. You declare CPU and memory per task or pod and pay for that, per second, while it runs. Each task gets its own isolated micro-VM and its own network interface, which is strong isolation by default. Ideal for spiky traffic, small teams, environments that sit idle overnight, and any team without dedicated platform engineers. Trade-offs: a higher price per unit of CPU, a start-up delay of tens of seconds while capacity is provisioned and the image is pulled, no GPUs or special instance families, no <strong>daemon</strong> workloads (one agent per host, since you have no hosts), and bounded per-task resources and ephemeral storage.</li>\n<li><strong>EC2.</strong> Cheaper per unit of CPU, and much cheaper overall if you keep instances busy. <strong>Bin packing</strong> — fitting many containers onto each instance so little capacity is wasted — is what makes this work; at 70-80% sustained utilization EC2 usually wins decisively, and Savings Plans, Reserved Instances and Spot push it further. It is also the only way to get GPUs, ARM-based or high-memory instance families, per-host daemon agents, and full control over the kernel and host. The cost is that you now own capacity planning, autoscaling the fleet, AMI patching and node upgrades.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A useful mental model: <strong>Fargate converts money into time</strong>. You pay a premium per vCPU and get back the hours your team would spend on node operations. Whether that is a good trade depends on how many hours you have and how full your instances are. Many organisations run both — Fargate for small, bursty or strongly isolated services, EC2 for the dense, steady core.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Two more options outside the grid: Lambda and App Runner</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>AWS Lambda</strong> is <strong>event-driven</strong> compute: you upload a function, and AWS runs it in response to an event — an HTTP request through API Gateway, a message on SQS, a file landing in S3, a schedule. There is no orchestrator, no capacity and no scaling configuration; concurrency rises and falls automatically, and you are billed per request and per millisecond of execution, dropping to zero when nothing happens. It supports container images as a packaging format, which confuses people, but a Lambda function is still a function, not a long-running server.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Its boundaries are what make it unsuitable for some work. Each invocation has a <strong>maximum execution duration</strong> measured in minutes, not hours, so a long batch job does not fit. Deployment package size is bounded (larger for container images than zip files). The function is stateless between invocations with only a modest temporary directory. And there is <strong>cold start</strong> — the extra delay the first time a new execution environment must be created before your code runs, typically tens to hundreds of milliseconds, worse for large packages and heavyweight runtimes, and reducible with provisioned concurrency at a cost. For steady high-volume traffic, per-request pricing eventually exceeds the cost of always-on containers.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>AWS App Runner</strong> is the shortest path from a container image or a source repository to a running, load-balanced, HTTPS web service with a public URL. You point it at ECR or GitHub, set CPU and memory, and it builds, deploys, scales and gives you a TLS endpoint. There is nearly nothing to configure — and correspondingly little to control. It suits simple public HTTP services, prototypes and internal tools; it is not the place for complex multi-service architectures, background workers or fine-grained networking.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>A small team's first web API.</strong> Four engineers, one HTTP service, AWS only, nobody knows Kubernetes. <em>Recommendation: ECS on Fargate</em>, behind an Application Load Balancer. No control plane fee, no nodes, no cluster upgrades, and the whole mental model is a task definition and a service. App Runner is a defensible answer if the service is a single simple public web app and you want even less to configure — but you give up networking control.</li>\n<li><strong>A batch job that runs for two hours.</strong> Nightly data processing, heavy CPU. <em>Recommendation: an ECS task on Fargate triggered by EventBridge</em>, or EC2 Spot if the job is large and frequent enough to justify it. Lambda is ruled out immediately by the bounded execution duration — a two-hour run does not fit, and splitting it with Step Functions is only worth it if the work naturally chunks.</li>\n<li><strong>A machine-learning inference service needing GPUs.</strong> <em>Recommendation: EC2 capacity, ECS or EKS depending on the team.</em> Fargate has no GPU support and Lambda has none, so the capacity decision is made for you. EKS is a common pick here because the ML ecosystem (Kubeflow, Ray, GPU device plugins) is Kubernetes-native. Use scale-to-zero or Spot for expensive idle GPUs.</li>\n<li><strong>A hard multi-cloud or hybrid requirement.</strong> Regulatory or commercial reasons demand the ability to run in another cloud or on-premises. <em>Recommendation: EKS</em>, with standard Kubernetes manifests and Helm charts and careful avoidance of AWS-only add-ons in the critical path. This is the strongest single argument for EKS, and one of the few where ECS is genuinely the wrong answer.</li>\n<li><strong>A spiky, low-traffic internal tool.</strong> Used a few hours a day by staff. <em>Recommendation: Lambda behind API Gateway</em> if it fits the request/response model — it costs essentially nothing while idle. If it is an existing container image or needs long-lived connections, ECS on Fargate with a low task count, or App Runner with scale-to-zero, both work well. Paying for a 24/7 EC2 instance for a tool used at lunchtime is the mistake to avoid.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Common beginner mistakes across all of these</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Choosing Kubernetes for a three-service application.</strong> You inherit cluster upgrades, add-on compatibility and a large vocabulary to solve a problem ECS on Fargate solves in an afternoon. Choose EKS for a reason you can state out loud.</li>\n<li><strong>Treating Fargate as an orchestrator.</strong> Fargate has no scheduler, no service concept and no deployment logic. It is capacity that ECS or EKS drives.</li>\n<li><strong>Ignoring image size.</strong> A 2 GB image slows every scale-out, every deployment and every Lambda cold start. Use small base images, multi-stage builds, and a <code>.dockerignore</code>.</li>\n<li><strong>Putting secrets in environment variables.</strong> Plain environment variables in a task definition are visible to anyone with describe permissions and end up in version control. Reference Secrets Manager or SSM Parameter Store so the value is injected at runtime.</li>\n<li><strong>No health checks.</strong> Without them the platform cannot tell a wedged process from a working one, so broken containers keep serving traffic and deployments cannot roll back on failure.</li>\n<li><strong>One giant task definition.</strong> Packing five unrelated containers into one task means they scale together, deploy together and fail together. Separate services scale and release independently; a sidecar should genuinely support its main container.</li>\n<li><strong>Running stateful workloads without persistent storage.</strong> A database in a container whose data lives on ephemeral disk loses everything on restart. Use EFS or EBS deliberately — or, far more often, use RDS and let AWS run the database.</li>\n<li><strong>Forgetting that containers are ephemeral.</strong> Anything stored in the container — uploaded files, session state, logs — disappears. Files go to S3, sessions to ElastiCache or a database, logs to CloudWatch.</li>\n<li><strong>No resource requests or limits.</strong> Without them the scheduler cannot pack sensibly and one noisy container starves its neighbours.</li>\n<li><strong>Using the latest tag.</strong> It makes deployments unreproducible and rollbacks ambiguous. Tag with a version or commit SHA.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Common beginner misconceptions</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>\"Fargate is an alternative to ECS.\"</strong> It is a capacity option that ECS or EKS uses. The orchestrator and capacity decisions are separate.</li>\n<li><strong>\"EKS is just the better, more modern ECS.\"</strong> Neither is more modern; they solve the same problem with different trade-offs. EKS buys portability and ecosystem in exchange for complexity and a per-cluster charge.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Lambda supports container images, so it is a container platform.\"</strong> Container images are only a packaging format there. Lambda is still bounded-duration, event-driven functions.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Serverless is always cheaper.\"</strong> Serverless is cheaper when utilization is low or spiky. At steady high volume, well-packed EC2 with Savings Plans or Spot usually wins.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Kubernetes makes me cloud-agnostic.\"</strong> Your manifests port; your load balancers, IAM, storage classes, managed databases and CI pipelines mostly do not. Portability is real but partial.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Migrating from ECS to EKS later is a disaster.\"</strong> Your container images are the portable part and they carry over unchanged. You rewrite deployment configuration, which is work but not a rewrite of the application.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Picking the orchestrator is the important decision.\"</strong> Image hygiene, health checks, secrets handling, resource sizing and logging affect your reliability far more than ECS versus EKS.</li>\n</ul>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"First, these are two separate decisions.
