{"title":"Event Source Mapping Deep Dive — Batching, Filtering, and Partial Batch Failures","tags":["event-source-mapping","batching","event-filtering","partial-batch-failure"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">An <strong>event source mapping (ESM)</strong> is a Lambda-managed resource that <strong>polls</strong> a source on your behalf and then invokes your function with a <strong>batch</strong> of records. This is the inverse of push-based invocation: with S3, SNS, or EventBridge the service calls Lambda's <code>Invoke</code> API and Lambda simply receives the event. With SQS, Kinesis, DynamoDB Streams, Amazon MQ, DocumentDB change streams, and Kafka (MSK or self-managed), <strong>Lambda owns the consumer</strong>. The poller fleet runs in Lambda's service account, reads from the source with the <strong>execution role of your function</strong>, assembles records into a batch, and performs a <strong>synchronous</strong> invocation. Everything interesting about the reliability of these integrations — batching economics, filtering, retry semantics, poison-pill handling — is configured on the ESM, not in your handler.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The practical consequence: many production problems that look like \"Lambda bugs\" are actually ESM misconfiguration. Duplicate processing, infinite retry storms, silently dropped records, and cost blowouts almost always trace back to a batch size, a missing <code>FunctionResponseTypes</code> setting, or an absent filter.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The polling loop and the invocation contract</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Conceptually the ESM runs this Third, <strong>filtering happens in the poller</strong>, before your function exists, so filtered-out records are free.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Batch size and batch window: the latency/efficiency dial</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><code>BatchSize</code> is the maximum number of records per invocation. <code>MaximumBatchingWindowInSeconds</code> (0–300) is how long the poller will wait to <em>fill</em> that batch before giving up and invoking anyway. If you process 1,000 records/second with <code>BatchSize: 1</code> you pay for 1,000 invocations/second. At <code>BatchSize: 100</code> you pay for 10. The cost of that is <strong>tail latency</strong>: with a batching window of 20 seconds, a record that arrives just after a batch dispatches waits up to 20 seconds before processing begins. That is fine for analytics aggregation and catastrophic for a user-visible notification pipeline.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Batch size interacts with your timeout.</strong> Lambda's timeout applies to the whole batch, not per record. If one record takes 400 ms and you set <code>BatchSize: 500</code>, you need a 200-second timeout, and a single slow downstream call can push the batch over the edge, failing all 500 records.</li>\n<li><strong>Batch size interacts with retries.</strong> Larger batches mean more records re-processed when something fails, which is exactly why partial batch failure reporting matters more as batch size grows.</li>\n<li><strong>Batch size interacts with visibility timeout (SQS).</strong> A bigger batch needs a longer function timeout, which needs a longer queue visibility timeout.</li>\n<li><strong>Batch window has a subtlety for streams.</strong> For Kinesis and DynamoDB Streams, if the shard is empty the poller will still wait out the window, which idles pollers but does not cost invocations.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Event filtering: doing nothing, efficiently</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Filter criteria</strong> are EventBridge-style content filter patterns evaluated by the ESM poller. Records that do not match are discarded before any invocation, so you pay nothing for them — no invocation, no duration, no log lines. You can attach up to <strong>5 filter patterns per ESM</strong> by default (raisable via a service quota request); patterns are ORed together, so a record matching any one of them is delivered. The supported comparators include exact match (an array of literal values), <code>exists</code>, <code>prefix</code>, <code>numeric</code> with range operators, <code>anything-but</code>, null and empty checks, and nested <code>and</code> / <code>or</code> composition.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The trap is that <strong>you must write the pattern against the wire shape of the record, not the business payload</strong>, and that shape differs per source:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>SQS.</strong> The message payload lives under <code>body</code>. If the body is valid JSON, Lambda parses it and you filter on nested fields: <code>{\"body\": {\"orderType\": [\"premium\"]}}</code>. If the body is not JSON, it is treated as a plain string and only string comparators (equality, prefix, anything-but) apply. You can also filter on <code>messageAttributes</code>.</li>\n<li><strong>DynamoDB Streams.</strong> Records carry DynamoDB's typed attribute-value encoding, so you must include the type descriptor: <code>{\"dynamodb\": {\"NewImage\": {\"status\": {\"S\": [\"SHIPPED\"]}}}}</code>. You will also frequently filter on <code>eventName</code> (<code>INSERT</code>, <code>MODIFY</code>, <code>REMOVE</code>) — filtering out <code>REMOVE</code> events alone often halves invocation volume on TTL-heavy tables. Note that numeric attributes arrive as <code>{\"N\": \"42\"}</code>, a <em>string</em>, so numeric range operators on them do not behave the way people expect; prefer string equality or restructure the item.</li>\n<li><strong>Kinesis.</strong> The record's <code>data</code> field is base64 on the wire, but Lambda base64-decodes and JSON-parses it for filtering purposes, so you can write <code>{\"data\": {\"eventType\": [\"click\"]}}</code> directly. Metadata fields such as <code>partitionKey</code> are also filterable.</li>\n<li><strong>Kafka.</strong> Filter on <code>value</code> (JSON-parsed) and on <code>key</code>/<code>headers</code>.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">One important asymmetry: for <strong>SQS</strong>, records filtered out are <strong>deleted from the queue</strong> by the poller — they do not accumulate or go to the DLQ. For <strong>streams</strong>, filtered records are simply skipped and the checkpoint advances past them. Either way, <em>filtering is destructive from the consumer's point of view</em>: if you filter wrongly you do not get a dead letter, you get silence. Always deploy a filter change behind a metric that counts what you expect to process.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Partial batch failures: the incident this prevents</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Default ESM semantics are <strong>all-or-nothing per batch</strong>. If your handler throws, the entire batch is considered failed. For SQS that means none of the messages are deleted and all of them become visible again after the visibility timeout. For streams it means the checkpoint does not advance and the <em>same batch</em> is re-delivered.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Now imagine a batch of 100 SQS messages where message #47 has a malformed field that makes your parser throw. Messages 1–46 were already processed and their side effects already committed. The batch fails, all 100 come back, 1–46 are processed <em>again</em>, and #47 throws again. This repeats until <code>maxReceiveCount</code> is exhausted for every message in the batch — so 99 perfectly good messages get dragged to the dead-letter queue alongside the one poison message, after being processed several times each. That is the classic production incident: duplicated side effects, a DLQ full of healthy messages, and a cost spike.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The fix is <strong>partial batch failure reporting</strong>. Set <code>FunctionResponseTypes: [\"ReportBatchItemFailures\"]</code> on the ESM (supported for SQS, Kinesis, DynamoDB Streams, and Kafka sources) and have your handler <strong>return</strong> — not throw — a response of the form:</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>json</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\"><i class=x>{</i> <i class=a>&quot;batchItemFailures&quot;</i><i class=x>:</i> <i class=x>[</i> <i class=x>{</i> <i class=a>&quot;itemIdentifier&quot;</i><i class=x>:</i> <i class=s>&quot;&lt;messageId or sequenceNumber&gt;&quot;</i> <i class=x>}</i> <i class=x>]</i> <i class=x>}</i></code></pre></div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Semantics differ meaningfully by source, and this distinction is a favourite interview question:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>SQS.</strong> <code>itemIdentifier</code> is the record's <code>messageId</code>. Lambda deletes every message <em>not</em> in the list and leaves the listed ones for redelivery. Order is irrelevant; this is true per-message granularity.</li>\n<li><strong>Kinesis / DynamoDB Streams.</strong> <code>itemIdentifier</code> is the record's <code>sequenceNumber</code>. Because streams preserve order, Lambda checkpoints at the <strong>lowest</strong> reported failed sequence number and retries <em>everything from that point onward</em>. Reporting record 3 of 100 as failed means records 3–100 are retried, not just record 3. Partial batch reporting on streams is therefore a checkpoint optimization, not a per-record retry mechanism.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The failure modes of the response contract itself are worth memorizing:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li>Returning <code>null</code>, an empty object, or <code>{\"batchItemFailures\": []}</code> means <strong>the whole batch succeeded</strong>.</li>\n<li><strong>Throwing</strong> still fails the whole batch, regardless of the setting. Your handler must catch per-record errors and accumulate identifiers.</li>\n<li>An <code>itemIdentifier</code> that is null, empty, or does not correspond to a message in the batch causes Lambda to treat the <strong>entire batch as a failure</strong> — a silent regression when someone refactors and reports an order ID instead of a message ID.</li>\n<li>Enabling <code>ReportBatchItemFailures</code> but never returning the field means every batch looks successful, so real errors vanish. Pair it with a custom metric on failure count.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">ESM-level error controls</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Beyond partial batch reporting, streams-type ESMs (Kinesis, DynamoDB Streams, and Kafka for some settings) expose controls that SQS ESMs do not, because SQS already has a queue-level redrive policy:</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; To actually recover the records you take the shard ID and sequence-number range from the metadata and re-read the stream — which only works while the data is still within retention (24 hours for DynamoDB Streams, 24 hours by default for Kinesis). Choosing an S3 on-failure destination for Kinesis, which does capture the failed records themselves, avoids that race.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">How ESM scaling works, by source</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>SQS standard.</strong> Lambda starts with a small number of concurrent pollers (five batches) and, while the queue stays backlogged, adds concurrent instances rapidly — up to 300 per minute — with a ceiling of 1,250 concurrent executions for the ESM. Use the ESM's <code>ScalingConfig.MaximumConcurrency</code> (2–1,000) to cap it without causing throttles.</li>\n<li><strong>SQS FIFO.</strong> Concurrency is bounded by the number of <em>active message group IDs</em>; a queue with three group IDs cannot exceed three concurrent invocations no matter what you configure.</li>\n<li><strong>Kinesis and DynamoDB Streams.</strong> One concurrent invocation per shard by default. <code>ParallelizationFactor</code> (1–10) multiplies that while preserving per-partition-key ordering. Maximum concurrency is therefore shards × parallelization factor — a hard architectural ceiling you change by resharding, not by raising a Lambda quota.</li>\n<li><strong>Kafka.</strong> Concurrency scales with partitions; Lambda adds consumers as lag grows, up to one per partition (and can exceed that with newer provisioned-mode configurations).</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Monitoring an ESM</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The ESM itself is mostly observed through source metrics plus Lambda metrics, and you need both sides to diagnose anything:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Source-side backlog.</strong> <code>ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage</code> and <code>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible</code> for SQS; <code>IteratorAge</code> (AWS/Lambda) and <code>GetRecords.IteratorAgeMilliseconds</code> (AWS/Kinesis) for streams. Backlog age is the true user-facing SLI — queue depth alone can be healthy churn.</li>\n<li><strong>Lambda-side.</strong> <code>Invocations</code>, <code>Errors</code>, <code>Throttles</code>, <code>Duration</code> p99, <code>ConcurrentExecutions</code>. A rising backlog <em>with</em> rising <code>Throttles</code> means concurrency starvation; a rising backlog with flat invocations means the poller is not scaling or the ESM is disabled.</li>\n<li><strong>ESM state.</strong> <code>aws lambda get-event-source-mapping</code> returns <code>State</code> and <code>StateTransitionReason</code>. An ESM can sit in <code>Disabled</code> or in a problem state (for example, because the execution role lost <code>kinesis:GetRecords</code> or the queue was deleted) and Lambda will emit <em>no</em> error metric — the function is simply never invoked. Alarm on \"invocations == 0 while backlog > 0\".</li>\n<li><strong>Custom.</strong> Emit an EMF metric for <code>batchItemFailures.length</code> and for per-record processing latency; the built-in metrics cannot see inside a batch.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Interview scenario: sizing and hardening an order pipeline</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An order-events SQS queue receives <strong>2,000 messages/second</strong> at peak. Each message is ~1.5 KB JSON. Only ~15% are <code>orderType: \"premium\"</code>, which is all this consumer cares about. Per-record work is a 25 ms DynamoDB write. The SLA is \"premium orders reflected within 30 seconds\".</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step 1 — filter first.</strong> Add filter criteria <code>{\"body\": {\"orderType\": [\"premium\"]}}</code>. Effective rate drops from 2,000/s to 300/s, and the 1,700/s we discard cost zero invocations and zero duration. That is roughly an 85% cut before any tuning.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step 2 — size the batch.</strong> Budget 10 seconds of the 30-second SLA for batching, leaving 20 for processing and slack. With <code>MaximumBatchingWindowInSeconds: 5</code> and <code>BatchSize: 100</code>, a full batch is 100 × 1.5 KB = 150 KB, comfortably under 6 MB. At 300 records/second we fill a 100-record batch in ~0.33 s, so the window rarely binds and we dispatch ~3 invocations/second instead of 300. Duration per batch: 100 × 25 ms = 2.5 s serially, or ~400 ms with a concurrency of 10 inside the handler. Set function timeout to 30 s (headroom for retries), which means queue <strong>visibility timeout must be at least 180 s</strong> using the 6× rule.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step 3 — protect against poison messages.</strong> With <code>BatchSize: 100</code>, one bad record without partial batch reporting re-processes 99 good ones on every retry. Enable <code>FunctionResponseTypes: [\"ReportBatchItemFailures\"]</code>, wrap per-record work in try/catch, and return the failing <code>messageId</code>s. Set the queue's redrive policy to <code>maxReceiveCount: 5</code> with a dedicated DLQ.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step 4 — bound concurrency.</strong> At ~3 batches/second and ~0.5 s duration, steady-state concurrency is ~2.
