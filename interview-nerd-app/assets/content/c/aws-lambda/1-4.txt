{"title":"Lambda Cost Optimization at Scale","tags":["cost-optimization","finops","billing","graviton"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Almost everyone can recite the headline Lambda price — requests plus GB-seconds. The senior skill is knowing that at scale <strong>the headline price is frequently not the biggest line item</strong>. CloudWatch Logs ingestion, NAT Gateway data processing, provisioned concurrency held through the night, and Step Functions state transitions routinely outgrow the compute charge, and a team that only tunes memory will optimise the wrong thing for months.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Full Cost Model</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\"><li><strong>Requests.</strong> $0.20 per million invocations, regardless of duration or memory.</li><li><strong>Duration in GB-seconds, billed per millisecond.</strong> In us-east-1, <strong>$0.0000166667 per GB-second on x86_64</strong> and <strong>$0.0000133334 on arm64</strong>. GB-seconds = configured memory in GB × billed duration. Note it is <em>configured</em> memory, not used — provisioning 3 GB and using 300 MB bills the full 3 GB.</li><li><strong>Provisioned concurrency.</strong> Two charges: a <strong>holding charge of about $0.0000041667 per GB-second for every second an environment is held</strong>, invoked or not, plus duration at a <strong>discounted $0.0000097222 per GB-second</strong>. The implication cuts both ways — idle provisioned concurrency is the fastest way to waste money, while heavily utilised provisioned concurrency can be <em>cheaper</em> than on-demand.</li><li><strong>Ephemeral storage above the free 512 MB.</strong> Configurable up to 10,240 MB at roughly $0.0000000309 per GB-second (x86, us-east-1). Small per invocation, real at billions of invocations, and pure waste if the <code>/tmp</code> space is never used.</li><li><strong>Data transfer.</strong> Standard EC2 data transfer rates apply to traffic leaving AWS or crossing Availability Zones. Cross-AZ chatter between a Lambda and a database in another AZ is a silent recurring charge.</li><li><strong>NAT Gateway for VPC functions.</strong> About <strong>$0.045 per hour per gateway plus $0.045 per GB processed</strong>. Three AZs is roughly $100/month before any data, and routing S3 traffic through NAT instead of a free gateway endpoint is a classic five-figure mistake.</li><li><strong>CloudWatch Logs.</strong> Roughly <strong>$0.50 per GB ingested</strong> plus about <strong>$0.03 per GB-month stored</strong> (the Infrequent Access log class is cheaper to ingest at about $0.25 per GB, with reduced query features). This is <strong>the most common hidden Lambda cost at scale</strong> — see below.</li><li><strong>X-Ray.</strong> Roughly <strong>$5.00 per million traces recorded</strong> and $0.50 per million retrieved or scanned, with a monthly free allowance. Tracing 100 percent of a billion-invocation function is a budget item, not a default.</li><li><strong>Step Functions.</strong> Standard workflows bill about <strong>$0.025 per 1,000 state transitions</strong>; Express workflows bill by request and duration instead and are far cheaper for high-volume short workflows. A chatty Standard workflow wrapping a cheap Lambda can cost more than the Lambda.</li></ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>The logs arithmetic that shocks people.</strong> A function invoked <strong>500 million times a month</strong> that logs a modest <strong>2 KB per invocation</strong> produces 1 TB of log data. Ingestion at $0.50 per GB is about <strong>$500/month</strong>, plus storage. Meanwhile, that same function at 256 MB and 100 ms costs 500M × 0.25 GB × 0.1 s × $0.0000166667 ≈ <strong>$208</strong> of duration plus $100 of requests. <strong>Logging costs more than compute.</strong> Add a debug-level JSON dump of each event and 2 KB becomes 20 KB, and the logs bill becomes $5,000 against $300 of Lambda.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Optimisation Levers, With Reasoning</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\"><li><strong>Right-size memory empirically — and stop assuming smaller is cheaper.</strong> Because CPU scales with memory (a full vCPU near <strong>1,769 MB</strong>) and you are billed on memory × duration, a CPU-bound function often costs the <em>same or less</em> at 1,769 MB as at 512 MB while running three times faster. Use <strong>AWS Lambda Power Tuning</strong> to find the curve rather than guessing. The only functions that genuinely get cheaper at low memory are IO-bound ones whose duration does not respond to CPU.</li><li><strong>Migrate to arm64/Graviton.</strong> About <strong>20 percent lower per GB-second</strong> and often faster, so up to 34 percent better price-performance. Usually a one-line change; verify native dependencies have arm64 builds and shift traffic with a weighted alias.</li><li><strong>Reduce duration.</strong> Duration is the multiplier on everything. <strong>Connection reuse and HTTP keep-alive</strong> in global scope removes TCP and TLS handshakes from every call; <strong>bundling and tree-shaking</strong> cuts init; <strong>parallelising independent IO</strong> collapses serial waits. A 40 percent duration cut is a 40 percent compute cut with no capacity trade-off.</li><li><strong>Batch in event source mappings.</strong> Requests are billed per invocation, so processing SQS messages one at a time at <code>BatchSize: 1</code> costs 10× the request charge of <code>BatchSize: 10</code> — and far more than that in per-invocation overhead and init amortisation. Raise <code>BatchSize</code> (up to 10,000 for SQS standard queues, subject to a 6 MB payload limit) and use <code>MaximumBatchingWindowInSeconds</code> to trade a little latency for far fewer invocations. Pair it with <strong>partial batch responses</strong> (<code>ReportBatchItemFailures</code>) so one poison message does not cause the whole batch to be reprocessed.</li><li><strong>Cut log volume.</strong> Use the runtime's <strong>log level control</strong> (Lambda advanced logging controls let you set the application and system log level without redeploying), emit <strong>structured JSON</strong> rather than verbose prose, <strong>sample</strong> debug logs, never log full event payloads in production, and set an explicit <strong>retention period</strong> — log groups default to <strong>Never Expire</strong>, which is how teams accumulate years of logs they will never read. 30 days is a sensible default; ship anything longer-lived to S3.</li><li><strong>Sample X-Ray</strong> rather than tracing everything, and turn tracing off on high-volume functions where you already have metrics.</li><li><strong>Stop synchronous Lambda-to-Lambda chaining.</strong> If function A invokes function B with <code>RequestResponse</code> and waits, you pay for <strong>the same wall-clock time twice</strong> — A sits idle burning GB-seconds while B works. Chain three deep and you pay three times. The fixes: merge the functions, invoke asynchronously with <code>Event</code> if the result is not needed, or orchestrate with <strong>Step Functions</strong> so the waiting happens in the state machine rather than inside billable compute.</li><li><strong>Never pay Lambda to sleep.</strong> Polling loops, <code>sleep</code> calls, and waiting on a long-running job are billed at full rate. Use a Step Functions <code>Wait</code> state, or the <strong>callback pattern with a task token</strong>, so nothing is running while you wait.</li><li><strong>Use VPC endpoints instead of NAT</strong> for AWS service traffic — gateway endpoints for S3 and DynamoDB are <strong>free</strong> — and drop VPC attachment altogether on functions that only call AWS APIs.</li><li><strong>Delete idle provisioned concurrency.</strong> Check <code>ProvisionedConcurrencyUtilization</code>; if it sits at 10 percent overnight, drive it with <strong>Application Auto Scaling scheduled scaling</strong> instead of a flat allocation.</li></ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">When Lambda Stops Being the Cheapest Option</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Lambda's economics are <strong>excellent for spiky, bursty, or low-duty-cycle workloads</strong> and <strong>poor for sustained, constant, high-throughput load</strong>. The reason is structural: Lambda charges for capacity at a premium rate in exchange for scaling to zero and requiring no capacity management. If you never scale to zero, you are paying the premium for nothing.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The mental model is <strong>utilisation</strong>. A function running at 100 concurrency, 24 hours a day, at 1,769 MB costs 100 × 1.728 GB × 2,592,000 s × $0.0000166667 ≈ <strong>$7,465/month</strong> plus requests. The equivalent steady compute — roughly 100 vCPUs and 173 GB of RAM — is maybe 12–25 Graviton instances, which on <strong>Fargate</strong> is a few thousand dollars and on <strong>EC2 with Savings Plans or Spot</strong> can be several hundred to low thousands. The crossover in practice sits around <strong>sustained utilisation above roughly 40–60 percent of a provisioned fleet</strong>, but you should compute it rather than quote it.</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto;
