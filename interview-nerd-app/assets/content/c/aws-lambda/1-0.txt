It is the number of execution environments running your code at the same instant. Every other concurrency behaviour in Lambda — reserved concurrency, provisioned concurrency, throttling, scaling — is a statement about that instantaneous count. The single most useful equation in a Lambda interview is <strong>concurrency ≈ requests per second × average duration in seconds</strong> (Little's Law applied to a request/response system). If you can derive, defend, and debug that number, you can answer almost every concurrency question asked at SDE-2/SDE-3 level.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Concurrency Formula, Worked Properly</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A function invoked <strong>100 times per second</strong> with an <strong>average duration of 200 ms</strong> needs 100 × 0.2 = <strong>20 concurrent execution environments</strong>. Halve the duration to 100 ms and you need 10. Raise traffic to 1,000 RPS at 200 ms and you need 200. The formula makes an important point explicit: <strong>duration is a concurrency multiplier</strong>. A performance regression that takes a function from 200 ms to 800 ms quadruples its concurrency footprint at identical traffic, which is why latency regressions so often surface first as throttles rather than as latency alarms.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two caveats that separate a senior answer from a junior one. First, the formula uses the <strong>mean</strong>, but traffic is not uniform — a Poisson arrival process with mean concurrency 20 will routinely peak well above 20, so you provision against a high percentile, not the mean. Second, for <strong>stream and queue based event source mappings</strong>, concurrency is not driven by request rate at all: for Kinesis and DynamoDB Streams it is driven by shard count (one concurrent invocation per shard, or per shard × parallelization factor, which can be 1–10), and for SQS it is driven by the poller's own scaling behaviour and the batch size.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Account Concurrency Limit</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Every AWS account has a <strong>concurrent executions quota per Region</strong>, defaulting to <strong>1,000</strong> and raisable through Service Quotas (accounts at scale routinely run tens of thousands). Three properties matter:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\"><li><strong>It is regional, not global.</strong> 1,000 in us-east-1 and 1,000 in eu-west-1 are separate pools.</li><li><strong>It is shared across every function in the account and Region.</strong> Your batch job, your API backend, and someone else's experiment all draw from the same 1,000. This is the root cause of the classic <em>noisy neighbour</em> incident: a runaway async function consumes the pool and your customer-facing API starts returning 429s even though that API's own traffic never changed.</li><li><strong>Provisioned concurrency counts against it too.</strong> It is not an extra allocation on top.</li></ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The relevant CloudWatch metrics are <code>ConcurrentExecutions</code> (per function and account-wide), <code>UnreservedConcurrentExecutions</code> (account-wide headroom in the shared pool), <code>ClaimedAccountConcurrency</code> (how much of the quota is committed once reservations are counted), and <code>ProvisionedConcurrencyUtilization</code>.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Reserved Concurrency — One Setting, Two Jobs</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Reserved concurrency is the setting candidates most often half-understand. It does <strong>two things simultaneously</strong>, and you cannot get one without the other:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\"><li><strong>It guarantees a floor.</strong> The reserved amount is carved out of the account pool exclusively for that function. No other function can consume it, so a critical function reserved at 200 can always reach 200 regardless of what else is misbehaving.</li><li><strong>It imposes a ceiling.</strong> The function can never exceed its reserved value. This is the protective use: capping a function at 20 so it cannot open 400 connections to an RDS instance, or cannot exceed a third-party API's rate limit.</li></ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The consequence people miss: <strong>reserved concurrency is subtracted from the unreserved pool available to every other function.</strong> Reserving 200 on one function in a 1,000-limit account leaves 800 for everything else combined. Reservations are therefore a zero-sum allocation exercise, and AWS enforces a guardrail — <strong>you cannot create a reservation that would leave fewer than 100 unreserved concurrency</strong> in the Region. Setting reserved concurrency to <strong>0</strong> is the documented kill switch: the function is throttled on every invocation, which is how you stop a runaway consumer without deleting the trigger or the code.</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; Invocations routed to those environments skip the Init phase entirely. It is configured on a <strong>published version or an alias</strong>, which forces a disciplined deployment model: you publish a version, point an alias at it, and attach provisioned concurrency to the alias.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The interaction rule: if a function has <strong>both</strong>, provisioned concurrency is allocated <strong>out of</strong> the reserved amount and can never exceed it. A function reserved at 100 with 60 provisioned has 60 warm environments and can burst to 100 total, with the 40 spillover invocations paying cold starts. If you configure provisioned concurrency without reserved concurrency, the provisioned units are still deducted from the account pool, and spillover competes for the unreserved remainder.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Cost model: you pay a <strong>provisioned-concurrency charge per GB-second for every second the environment is held</strong>, whether or not it is invoked, <em>plus</em> a duration charge at a <strong>discounted per-GB-second rate</strong> when it is invoked, plus the normal per-request charge. In us-east-1 that is roughly $0.0000041667 per GB-second provisioned and $0.0000097222 per GB-second of duration, versus $0.0000166667 per GB-second for standard on-demand duration. The break-even insight: at very high, sustained utilisation, provisioned concurrency can be <em>cheaper</em> than on-demand because the discounted duration rate more than pays for the holding charge; at low utilisation it is pure waste. Use <code>ProvisionedConcurrencyUtilization</code> to find out which regime you are in, and drive the allocation with <strong>Application Auto Scaling</strong> (target tracking on utilisation, or scheduled scaling for predictable diurnal peaks).</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Scaling Rate — The Modern Per-Function Model</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The historical model was account-wide: an initial burst quota (500–3,000 depending on Region) shared by every function, after which the account could add only 500 concurrency per minute.
