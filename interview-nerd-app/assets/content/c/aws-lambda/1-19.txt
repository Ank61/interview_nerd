{"title":"Event-Driven Architecture Patterns and Lambda Anti-Patterns","tags":["eventbridge","event-driven","anti-patterns","architecture"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Serverless pushes you toward <strong>event-driven architecture</strong> whether you planned for it or not: functions are triggered by events, they are short-lived, and they scale independently. That is a genuine strength — loose coupling, independent scaling, and the ability to add consumers without touching producers. It is also a genuine hazard, because event-driven systems fail in ways that synchronous systems do not: silently, asynchronously, and at 3am. The senior skill is knowing both the patterns worth reaching for and the shapes that predictably collapse at scale.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">EventBridge as the Backbone</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Amazon EventBridge</strong> is the serverless event bus, and it should be the default routing layer for anything that is genuinely an event (a fact about something that happened) rather than a command or a stream of records. The pieces:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Buses.</strong> The <code>default</code> bus receives AWS service events. Create <strong>custom buses</strong> per bounded context or per domain — this is how you keep your <code>OrderPlaced</code> events out of the same routing surface as CloudTrail noise, and it gives you a clean per-domain permissions and quota boundary.</li>\n<li><strong>Rules and content-based filtering.</strong> A rule's <strong>event pattern</strong> matches on the structure and content of the event, including nested fields, prefix matching, numeric ranges, <code>anything-but</code>, <code>exists</code>, and (in newer versions) wildcard matching. This matters because <strong>filtering happens in EventBridge, not in your function</strong> — a consumer that only cares about orders over 1000 dollars should express that in the rule, not invoke a Lambda a million times to discard 999,000 of them. Filtering at the broker is both a cost and a correctness control.</li>\n<li><strong>Archive and replay.</strong> You can archive all events matching a pattern for a retention period, then <strong>replay</strong> them to a rule. This is one of EventBridge's most undersold features: it turns \"we deployed a bug and dropped four hours of events\" from a data-loss incident into a replay. It is also how you backfill a newly added consumer with historical events.</li>\n<li><strong>Schema registry and discovery.</strong> EventBridge can discover schemas from events flowing on a bus and generate typed code bindings. The real value is having an explicit, versioned contract — schema drift is the single most common cause of \"the event was published but nothing happened.\"</li>\n<li><strong>Cross-account and cross-region routing.</strong> A bus has a resource policy, so account A can put events onto account B's bus, or a rule can target a bus in another account or region. This is the mechanism for a multi-account event backbone, and it is far cleaner than each team granting each other IAM roles.</li>\n<li><strong>Targets, DLQs, and retries.</strong> Each target has its own retry policy (<code>MaximumRetryAttempts</code>, <code>MaximumEventAgeInSeconds</code>) and its own <strong>dead-letter queue</strong>. A missing target DLQ is how events vanish; always configure one. Also watch the <code>FailedInvocations</code> and <code>InvocationsSentToDlq</code> metrics.</li>\n<li><strong>Pipes and Scheduler.</strong> <strong>EventBridge Pipes</strong> does point-to-point source-to-target with optional filtering and enrichment, which deletes a whole category of \"Lambda that reads from a queue and writes to something else\" glue. <strong>EventBridge Scheduler</strong> replaces cron rules with one-time and recurring schedules at much higher scale, including per-item schedules (a reminder for each of a million users).</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; Interposing an SQS queue per consumer gives each consumer its own buffer, its own retry budget, its own DLQ, and its own scaling profile — one slow consumer no longer affects the others. In practice a mature system uses both: EventBridge for cross-domain routing, with an EventBridge rule targeting an SQS queue in front of a high-volume consumer to get the buffering benefit.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Transactional Outbox and Change Data Capture</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Here is a bug that ships constantly: a handler writes an order to DynamoDB and then publishes <code>OrderPlaced</code> to EventBridge. These are two separate systems, so there is no transaction. If the write succeeds and the publish fails, the order exists but nobody is told. If you publish first and the write fails, consumers act on an order that does not exist. Retrying the whole handler produces duplicate writes or duplicate events. This is the <strong>dual-write problem</strong>, and it cannot be fixed with better error handling — it is structural.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The <strong>transactional outbox</strong> solves it by making the write and the event-record a single atomic operation. In DynamoDB the elegant form is <strong>DynamoDB Streams as change data capture</strong>: write only to the table, and let the stream be the event source. The stream is guaranteed to reflect exactly what was committed, in per-partition-key order, so there is no window where the data and the event disagree. A small \"publisher\" Lambda consumes the stream and publishes domain events to EventBridge.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">BAD (dual write <i class=o>-</i> <i class=k>no</i> atomicity)\n  handler <i class=c>--&gt; DynamoDB.PutItem      (succeeds)</i>\n          <i class=c>--&gt; EventBridge.PutEvents (fails)   ==&gt; order exists, nobody notified</i>\n\nGOOD (outbox via streams <i class=o>-</i> <i class=k>one</i> <i class=k>atomic</i> write)\n  handler <i class=c>--&gt; DynamoDB.PutItem  (the ONLY write)</i>\n                   <i class=o>|</i>\n                   v\n            DynamoDB Stream  (NEW_AND_OLD_IMAGES, ordered <i class=k>per</i> <i class=k>partition</i> key)\n                   <i class=o>|</i>\n                   v\n            [stream<i class=o>-</i>publisher]  <i class=c>--&gt; EventBridge PutEvents</i>\n                   <i class=o>|</i>\n                   <i class=o>+</i><i class=c>-- at-least-once: consumers MUST be idempotent</i></code></pre></div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Points to raise when describing this: delivery becomes <strong>at-least-once</strong>, so downstream consumers need idempotency (an idempotency key, a conditional write, or Powertools Idempotency). Ordering is guaranteed <strong>only within a partition key</strong>, which is usually the right granularity — events for one order are ordered, events across orders are not. The stream retains records for 24 hours, so a publisher outage longer than that loses events, which is why <code>IteratorAge</code> on that publisher is a paging alarm. And if you are using a relational database instead, the classic outbox table plus a poller (or DMS/Debezium-style CDC) does the same job.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Claim Check for Large Payloads</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Every messaging service has a payload limit: SQS and SNS 256 KB, EventBridge 256 KB, Lambda synchronous request and response 6 MB, asynchronous invocation payload 256 KB. Large messages — an uploaded document, a full customer record with images, a batch export — do not fit. The <strong>claim check</strong> pattern puts the payload in S3 and passes only a pointer (bucket, key, version ID, plus a content hash and metadata) through the messaging layer.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Details that matter: include the S3 <strong>version ID</strong> so the consumer reads exactly the object the producer wrote, even if the key is overwritten; put enough metadata in the message (size, content type, a few routing fields) that consumers can filter without fetching; set an S3 <strong>lifecycle policy</strong> to expire claim-check objects so you do not pay to store payloads forever; and be explicit about who deletes the object, since a consumer that deletes after processing breaks a second consumer that has not read it yet. The Amazon SQS Extended Client Library implements this automatically for Java, and the pattern is trivial to hand-roll elsewhere.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Sagas and Compensation</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Distributed transactions do not exist across DynamoDB, a payment provider, and a warehouse API. The <strong>saga</strong> pattern replaces atomicity with a sequence of local transactions, each with a defined <strong>compensating action</strong>: if step four fails, run the compensations for steps three, two, and one in reverse. Reserve inventory; compensate by releasing it. Charge the card; compensate by refunding. Book the courier; compensate by cancelling.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two implementations: <strong>orchestrated</strong> (a Step Functions state machine with <code>Catch</code> blocks routing to compensation states — visible, debuggable, the default choice) and <strong>choreographed</strong> (each service listens for failure events and compensates itself — more decoupled, much harder to reason about). The subtleties worth naming: compensations must be <strong>idempotent</strong>, because the compensation itself can be retried; some actions are <strong>not compensable</strong> (you cannot un-send an email), so order the saga to put irreversible steps last; and there are intermediate states visible to users where inventory is reserved but payment has not cleared, so the UI and the data model must acknowledge \"pending\" as a real state rather than pretending the operation is atomic.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Anti-Pattern: The Lambda Monolith (and Its Opposite)</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The <strong>Lambda monolith</strong> or \"Lambdalith\" is one function fronting every route, with an internal router dispatching on path and method. It is attractive because it feels like a normal web app. The costs: the deployment package contains every dependency for every route, so cold start is the worst-case across all of them; the execution role must be the <strong>union</strong> of every route's permissions, destroying least privilege; any deploy risks every endpoint; and you cannot tune memory, timeout, or reserved concurrency per route, so a heavyweight report endpoint and a trivial health check get identical settings. Per-function metrics become meaningless because everything aggregates.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">But the opposite is also an anti-pattern. <strong>Over-decomposition</strong> into hundreds of trivial functions produces a deployment surface nobody can manage, dozens of extra cold starts in a single user journey, an explosion of IAM roles and log groups, and a distributed system where a simple refactor spans fifteen repositories. The sensible middle is a function per <strong>coherent responsibility</strong> — typically one per resource-and-operation grouping, or a small handful per bounded context, using a lightweight framework (Powertools Event Handler, or a micro-router) <em>within</em> a function where routes genuinely share code and permissions. State the trade-off explicitly in an interview; claiming one function per route is universally correct is a weaker answer than explaining when a small Lambdalith is legitimate (an internal low-traffic admin API, or a team migrating incrementally).</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Anti-Pattern: Synchronous Lambda-to-Lambda and Chatty Chains</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Covered in depth under orchestration, but it belongs on any anti-pattern list: invoking a Lambda from a Lambda with <code>RequestResponse</code> means you pay for the same wall-clock time at every level of nesting, you hold idle concurrency slots, and each hop needs its own retry and timeout reasoning. Closely related is the <strong>chatty synchronous chain</strong>: A calls B calls C calls D, each adding latency and each multiplying failure probability. If every hop is 99.9% available, four hops is 99.6% — and the latency is the <em>sum</em> of the hops plus any cold starts along the way, so p99 is dramatically worse than any individual service's p99. The fixes are to collapse the chain (do the work in one function), make it asynchronous (queue between steps), or orchestrate it (Step Functions), depending on whether a caller actually needs a synchronous answer.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Anti-Pattern: Recursive Invocation Loops</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The canonical version: a function triggered by object creation in a bucket writes its output <em>to the same bucket</em>. Each write triggers another invocation, which writes again. Concurrency saturates in seconds and the bill is measured in thousands of dollars before anyone notices. The same shape occurs with a function that consumes an SQS queue and, on error, writes the message back to the same queue; or a DynamoDB stream consumer that updates the same table.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Lambda ships <strong>recursive loop detection</strong> as a safety net: it tracks invocation lineage via metadata and, after roughly 16 recursive invocations in a chain, stops the function and emits the <code>RecursiveInvocationsDropped</code> metric and a notification. It works for supported paths (Lambda to SQS to Lambda, Lambda to SNS to Lambda, and Lambda invoking itself via the SDK). You can configure it per function with <code>aws lambda put-function-recursion-config --recursive-loop Terminate|Allow</code>. <strong>Treat it as a circuit breaker, not a design.</strong> It does not cover every path (notably S3-triggered loops are not the same lineage mechanism), it triggers only after real invocations and real cost, and relying on it signals you have not thought about the topology. The actual design controls are: write outputs to a <em>different</em> bucket or a different prefix with a scoped S3 event notification filter, use a separate DLQ rather than re-queueing to the source, set <strong>reserved concurrency</strong> as a hard blast-radius cap on anything with a self-referential risk, and alarm on <code>ConcurrentExecutions</code>.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Anti-Pattern: Lambda for Sustained High-Throughput Load</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Lambda's pricing is excellent for spiky, bursty, or low-duty-cycle workloads, and it is genuinely expensive for a service that runs flat out 24/7. At constant high throughput, an ECS Fargate or EC2 service with autoscaling is often several times cheaper, because you are paying for reserved capacity you are actually using rather than per-invocation overhead. The crossover depends on request duration, memory, and utilisation, but the shape of the answer is: <strong>if your concurrency graph is a flat line rather than a spiky one, model the container cost</strong>. Related signals that you are past the boundary: you have added provisioned concurrency across the board (at which point you are paying for always-on capacity with Lambda's pricing model), your workload is long-running or CPU-saturated, or you need connection pooling and in-process caching that Lambda's isolation model makes awkward. The right answer is not dogma in either direction — it is that this is a cost-model question you should actually compute, and that a hybrid (Lambda for spiky async work, containers for the steady-state API) is common and correct.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Anti-Pattern: Treating /tmp or Globals as Durable State</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The execution environment is reused, which tempts people into treating it as a server. Two failure modes. First, <strong>correctness</strong>: there is no guarantee a subsequent invocation lands in the same environment, and environments are recycled without warning, so anything in <code>/tmp</code> or a module-level variable can vanish between invocations. A counter in a global is wrong the moment concurrency exceeds one, because each environment has its own copy. A file written to <code>/tmp</code> in invocation one is frequently absent in invocation two. Second, <strong>leakage</strong>: because environments <em>are</em> reused, state left behind is visible to the next invocation, which may belong to a different user or tenant — a cached authorisation decision or a partially written temp file becomes a cross-request data bug, and in a multi-tenant system a security incident.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The correct uses of warm state are narrow and worth naming precisely: <strong>initialised clients and connections</strong>, <strong>immutable configuration and secrets with a TTL</strong>, and <strong>read-only reference data</strong>. Everything mutable and request-scoped belongs in DynamoDB, ElastiCache, or the event payload. And remember <code>/tmp</code> is bounded (configurable from 512 MB up to 10 GB) and <em>persists</em> across invocations in the same environment, so a function that writes a file per invocation without cleaning up will eventually fail with no space left on device — a classic slow-burn production bug.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Anti-Pattern: Unbounded Concurrency Against a Relational Database</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Lambda scales to thousands of concurrent environments in seconds. A relational database accepts a few hundred connections. Each execution environment opens its own connection, and environments outlive invocations, so idle connections accumulate. The result is connection exhaustion, and because the database is usually shared, the blast radius extends to unrelated applications. Controls, in order: <strong>RDS Proxy</strong> to pool and multiplex connections across environments; <strong>reserved concurrency</strong> on the function as a hard ceiling that also protects the rest of the account; a queue in front so bursts are absorbed and drained at a controlled rate (with SQS <code>MaximumConcurrency</code> on the event source mapping); short connection timeouts and no long-lived transactions; and, at the architectural level, questioning whether a relational database is the right store for a workload whose access pattern is high-concurrency key-value.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Anti-Pattern: Assuming Ordering the Source Does Not Guarantee</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This one bites late and hurts. <strong>Standard SQS does not guarantee order</strong>, and it delivers at-least-once — a \"cancel\" can arrive before the \"create\" it cancels. <strong>SNS does not guarantee order</strong> to a given subscriber. <strong>EventBridge does not guarantee order</strong>. <strong>Kinesis and DynamoDB Streams guarantee order only within a shard or partition key</strong>, and a resharding event or a change of partition key breaks the assumption you built on. <strong>SQS FIFO</strong> guarantees order within a message group, at a throughput cost and with the consequence that a poison message blocks its whole group.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The engineering answer is to <strong>design for out-of-order delivery</strong> rather than hope: include a monotonic version or sequence number in the event and reject stale updates with a conditional write (<code>ConditionExpression</code> on the version attribute); make operations commutative where you can (set a value rather than increment it); use event timestamps plus a last-writer-wins rule where that is semantically acceptable; or, where ordering is genuinely required, choose a source that provides it at the granularity you need — FIFO with a message group ID per aggregate, or a stream partitioned by the aggregate's ID. State the requirement explicitly in the design; \"we assumed events arrive in order\" is the root cause line in a lot of postmortems.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Interview Scenario: What Breaks at 100x Scale</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">You are shown this proposed design for an e-commerce order pipeline, currently handling 100 orders per minute and expected to reach 10,000 per minute during a sale.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">API Gateway <i class=c>--&gt; [order-api]  (writes Aurora, then invokes payment-svc</i>\n                              synchronously <i class=k>with</i> RequestResponse, <i class=k>then</i>\n                              publishes <i class=k>to</i> SNS)\n                     <i class=o>|</i>\n                     v\n                   SNS topic &quot;orders&quot;\n                     <i class=o>|</i>\n      <i class=o>+</i><i class=c>--------------+--------------+----------------+</i>\n      v              v              v                v\n[email<i class=o>-</i>svc]    [inventory<i class=o>-</i>svc]  [analytics<i class=o>-</i>svc]  [warehouse<i class=o>-</i>svc]\n (SNS<i class=o>-</i><i class=o>&gt;</i>Lambda) (SNS<i class=o>-</i><i class=o>&gt;</i>Lambda)    (SNS<i class=o>-</i><i class=o>&gt;</i>Lambda)    (SNS<i class=o>-</i><i class=o>&gt;</i>Lambda,\n                                                  writes back <i class=k>to</i>\n                                                  the SAME topic)</code></pre></div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Finding 1 — dual write between Aurora and SNS (correctness, P0).</strong> The write and the publish are not atomic. At 100 orders per minute you rarely see the gap; at 10,000 per minute, with SNS occasionally throttling, you will routinely have orders in the database that no consumer ever heard about, and orders announced that were rolled back. Fix: a transactional outbox — write the order and an outbox row in one Aurora transaction and publish from the outbox, or move the write to DynamoDB and use <strong>DynamoDB Streams</strong> as the event source so the commit <em>is</em> the event.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Finding 2 — synchronous invocation of payment-svc (cost, latency, availability, P0).</strong> <code>order-api</code> blocks while payment runs, so you pay twice for the same seconds, you hold two concurrency slots per request, and the API's availability is now the product of both functions' availability. At 10,000 per minute with a 2-second payment call, that is a large amount of idle concurrency. Fix: either make payment asynchronous with the order entering a <code>PENDING_PAYMENT</code> state, or orchestrate the whole order lifecycle in Step Functions with retries and compensation, leaving <code>order-api</code> to do nothing but validate and accept.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Finding 3 — Lambda subscribed directly to SNS (durability, P0).</strong> SNS invokes Lambda asynchronously with a limited retry policy and <em>no buffering</em>. If <code>inventory-svc</code> is throttled or down for ten minutes during the sale, those messages are gone. Fix: SNS-to-<strong>SQS</strong>-to-Lambda, one queue per consumer, each with its own DLQ and its own <code>MaximumConcurrency</code>. Each consumer now has independent backpressure, batching, and replay from its DLQ.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Finding 4 — warehouse-svc publishes back to the same topic (recursive loop, P0).</strong> This is a loop waiting to happen; one mis-filtered message and concurrency saturates. Lambda's recursive-loop detection may catch the Lambda-SNS-Lambda path, but that is a circuit breaker firing after real cost, not a design. Fix: separate topics or a separate EventBridge bus for downstream events, plus <strong>reserved concurrency</strong> on every consumer as a blast-radius cap.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Finding 5 — Aurora connection exhaustion (availability, P1).</strong> At 10,000 orders per minute, <code>order-api</code> alone may hold many hundreds of warm environments, each with its own connection, and the consumers add more. Aurora will refuse connections and the failure will spread to anything else using that cluster. Fix: <strong>RDS Proxy</strong>, reserved concurrency ceilings, and a queue in front of write-heavy consumers so the write rate is decoupled from the arrival rate.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Finding 6 — no filtering at the broker (cost, P2).</strong> Every consumer receives every order event and discards what it does not need; <code>analytics-svc</code> probably wants all of them, but <code>warehouse-svc</code> likely only cares about physical goods. Fix: SNS subscription filter policies, or move to EventBridge rules with content-based patterns, so filtering happens before an invocation is billed.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Finding 7 — no idempotency and no ordering discipline (correctness, P1).</strong> SNS is at-least-once and unordered. At 100x volume, duplicate deliveries stop being theoretical and a cancellation can overtake a creation. Fix: idempotency keys with conditional writes in every consumer (Powertools Idempotency does this against DynamoDB), and a version number on the order with stale-update rejection.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Finding 8 — account concurrency is a shared fate (availability, P2).</strong> All these functions draw from one regional concurrency pool.
