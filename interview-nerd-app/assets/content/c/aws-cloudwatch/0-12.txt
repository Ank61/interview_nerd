AWS gives it to you automatically; you do not configure it, you cannot turn it off, and you are not billed for it.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>trail</strong> is something different. Industry reports routinely put the average time to detect an intrusion in the hundreds of days. A compliance auditor will ask for one year, three years, or seven years of records depending on the framework. Ninety days is simply not enough, and the day you need those records is exactly the day you cannot go back and create them. A trail only captures events from the moment it starts logging onward.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Multi-Region versus single-Region trails</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">AWS is divided into <strong>Regions</strong> — separate geographic installations such as us-east-1 (Northern Virginia) or eu-west-1 (Ireland). A single-Region trail records only activity that happened in the one Region where you created it. A <strong>multi-Region trail</strong> records activity in every Region, including Regions that AWS launches in the future, and delivers all of it into the same bucket.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Always choose multi-Region. The reason is a real and common attack pattern: an attacker who obtains credentials rarely works in the Region your team watches. They spin up mining instances in ap-south-1 or create an IAM user in a Region nobody has ever opened. With a single-Region trail, none of that appears in your records. The console wizard defaults to multi-Region, and the only realistic reason to override it is an unusual data-residency rule — and even then you would normally create per-Region trails so nothing is left uncovered.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A useful detail: a small number of AWS services are <strong>global</strong> rather than regional — IAM, AWS STS (the token service), Amazon CloudFront, Route 53. Their events are recorded as global service events and delivered by your multi-Region trail, so IAM changes show up even though IAM does not \"live\" in a Region.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Organization trails</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>AWS Organizations</strong> is the AWS feature for grouping many AWS accounts under one management (formerly \"master\") account — typically one account per team or per environment. An <strong>organization trail</strong> is a trail created in the management account (or in a delegated administrator account) that automatically captures activity from <em>every</em> member account, current and future, into one bucket.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>One place to look.</strong> Instead of hunting through twelve accounts, your security team queries one set of files.</li>\n<li><strong>Automatic coverage.</strong> A new account joining the organization is logged from day one, with nobody having to remember to configure it.</li>\n<li><strong>Member accounts cannot turn it off.</strong> Users in a member account can see the organization trail but cannot stop, delete, or modify it. This is the security property that matters: the person who compromises a workload account cannot silence the recording.</li>\n<li><strong>Prefix includes the organization ID.</strong> Files land under a path containing your organization identifier, then the member account ID, so it stays obvious which account each event came from.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Delivery to S3: bucket, prefix, and policy</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">CloudTrail batches events and writes them as gzip-compressed JSON files into your bucket on a predictable path. That permission lives in an <strong>S3 bucket policy</strong> — a JSON document attached to the bucket that says which callers may do what. The policy CloudTrail needs grants two things to the service principal <code>cloudtrail.amazonaws.com</code>: <code>s3:GetBucketAcl</code> on the bucket itself (so CloudTrail can check it can write) and <code>s3:PutObject</code> on the log path, conditioned so the bucket owner ends up owning the delivered objects. Modern policies also add an <code>aws:SourceArn</code> condition naming the exact trail, which prevents a stranger from pointing <em>their</em> trail at your bucket and running up your bill (the \"confused deputy\" problem). If you create the trail in the console and let it make the bucket, this policy is written for you.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Protecting the evidence</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Here is the practice that separates a real audit setup from a checkbox one: <strong>put the log bucket in a different AWS account from the workloads it is recording</strong>, and give almost nobody access to that account. If your logs sit in the same account as your application and that account is compromised, the attacker's first move is to delete the logs. Cross-account delivery means the credentials that let them wreck the workload give them nothing in the log archive account.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Versioning.</strong> An S3 bucket setting that keeps every previous copy of an object. If someone overwrites a log file, the original version is still there.</li>\n<li><strong>MFA delete.</strong> An extra bucket setting requiring a multi-factor authentication code from the account's root user to permanently delete a version. It makes quiet deletion effectively impossible.</li>\n<li><strong>S3 Object Lock.</strong> Write-once-read-many protection. In <em>compliance mode</em>, an object cannot be deleted or altered by anyone — not even the account root — until its retention period expires. This is what regulated organizations use to satisfy \"immutable storage\" requirements.</li>\n<li><strong>Lifecycle rules.</strong> Automatic rules that move older files to cheaper storage classes (S3 Glacier Instant Retrieval, Glacier Flexible Retrieval, Glacier Deep Archive) and optionally expire them after, say, seven years. Log volume grows forever otherwise, and Deep Archive can be roughly a twentieth of Standard's per-gigabyte price.</li>\n<li><strong>Block Public Access.</strong> Leave the bucket-level setting on. An accidentally public audit log is a breach in itself.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Encryption and access control</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">CloudTrail log files are always encrypted at rest — by default with S3-managed keys (SSE-S3), which requires no setup. For stronger control you can encrypt with <strong>AWS KMS</strong> (Key Management Service, the AWS service that creates and guards encryption keys). With a KMS key you decide in the <em>key policy</em> who is allowed to decrypt, which means reading the logs requires two separate permissions: S3 access to the file and KMS access to the key. Revoke the key permission and old logs become unreadable to that person immediately, without touching the bucket at all. On the read side, grant access through an IAM role that auditors assume, rather than by handing out long-lived access keys.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Also sending to CloudWatch Logs</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">S3 is excellent long-term storage but it is passive — nothing happens when a file lands. A trail can <em>additionally</em> stream the same events into <strong>CloudWatch Logs</strong>, the AWS service that holds streaming log lines and can react to them. You supply a log group (a named container for logs) and an IAM role that permits CloudTrail to write into it.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This is what unlocks near-real-time detection. In CloudWatch Logs you create a <strong>metric filter</strong> — a pattern that scans every arriving line and increments a number when it matches — and then an <strong>alarm</strong> on that number that notifies you. Classic examples: alert when the <strong>root user</strong> (the all-powerful original account login, which should essentially never be used) signs in; alert when a security group — a virtual firewall around your instances — is modified; alert when an IAM policy changes; alert on a burst of AccessDenied errors. Without this, nothing tells you about a dangerous change until a human goes looking.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Log file integrity validation</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An auditor will eventually ask a blunt question: \"how do you know nobody edited these logs before showing them to me?\" Enabling <strong>log file integrity validation</strong> on a trail is the answer. Events generally appear in Event history within about five minutes, and log files are typically delivered to S3 within roughly fifteen minutes of the API call, published several times an hour. Delivery to CloudWatch Logs is usually the faster of the two paths but is still minutes, not seconds. Plan accordingly: CloudTrail is an excellent audit and near-real-time detection tool, and a poor choice for anything that needs sub-second reaction.
