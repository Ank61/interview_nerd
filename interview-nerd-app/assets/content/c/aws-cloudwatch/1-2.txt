{"title":"Advanced Alarming — Anomaly Detection, Metric Math, and Alert Noise Reduction","tags":["anomaly-detection","composite-alarms","missing-data","alert-hygiene"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Most teams treat alarms as a checklist: pick a metric, pick a number, attach SNS, done. That produces on-call rotations where engineers acknowledge pages without reading them, which is strictly worse than having no alarms at all — it trains people to ignore the signal that matters. Advanced alarming is a systems-design discipline with three parts: choosing a signal that is actually a proxy for user pain, encoding the evaluation semantics so the alarm means what you think it means, and structuring the alarm graph so one failure produces one page rather than forty.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Why Static Thresholds Fail</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A static threshold encodes an assumption that the metric's normal range is constant. That assumption breaks in three predictable ways. <strong>Seasonality:</strong> a threshold set for weekday afternoon traffic fires every Saturday night, or conversely a threshold loose enough for the weekly peak is blind to a 3 a.m. outage. <strong>Growth:</strong> a raw error-count threshold of 100 errors per minute is reasonable at 10,000 requests per minute and absurd at 200,000 — the business grows and the alarm becomes a noise generator without anyone changing it. <strong>Heterogeneity:</strong> a single threshold across a fleet of different instance sizes or a set of tenants with wildly different volumes is wrong for most members. The cure for growth is a <em>ratio</em> (metric math); the cure for seasonality is <em>anomaly detection</em>; the cure for heterogeneity is per-dimension alarms generated from a SEARCH-driven template, or an alarm on a normalized quantity.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Anomaly Detection in Depth</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">CloudWatch anomaly detection trains a model on a metric's recent history (on the order of the past two weeks, requiring at least a few days of data before it produces a usable band) and continuously emits an <strong>expected band</strong> — an upper and lower bound the metric should stay within given the time of day and day of week. You then create an alarm using the <code>ANOMALY_DETECTION_BAND(m1, 2)</code> expression with a comparison operator of <code>GreaterThanUpperThreshold</code>, <code>LessThanLowerThreshold</code>, or <code>LessThanLowerOrGreaterThanUpperThreshold</code>. The second argument is the <strong>band width</strong> in standard-deviation-like units: smaller is tighter and noisier, larger is looser and slower to fire. Two is the default and a reasonable start; three is appropriate for a metric with genuinely high variance.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The model learns seasonality automatically, picking up daily and weekly cycles, so a metric that always drops at night produces a band that also drops at night rather than a flat line. That is its main advantage over a static threshold, and it is also why it is particularly good at the <em>lower</em> bound — detecting \"traffic is suspiciously low\", which static thresholds almost never catch and which is a classic symptom of an upstream failure or a broken deployment.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Its failure modes matter more than its features in an interview:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Insufficient history.</strong> A brand-new service or a newly published metric has nothing to train on. The band will be wide and useless, or the alarm will sit in INSUFFICIENT_DATA. Do not put anomaly detection on a service you launched yesterday.</li>\n<li><strong>Legitimate step changes.</strong> A deploy that doubles throughput, a marketing launch, a new large customer, or a scale-out all look exactly like anomalies. The model re-learns over days, so you get a stretch of false pages during the adjustment. For planned step changes, expect noise and consider temporarily suppressing the alarm.</li>\n<li><strong>Training on an incident.</strong> This is the subtle one. If the metric spent six hours at a catastrophic value last week, that period is in the training window and the model has learned that such values are normal-ish, widening the band and making the alarm less sensitive exactly where you need it. CloudWatch lets you <strong>exclude time ranges</strong> from training in the anomaly detector configuration, and the discipline is to add the incident window to the exclusions as part of every postmortem.</li>\n<li><strong>It does not know what is bad.</strong> Anomaly detection finds statistical unusualness, not user harm. A metric can be perfectly normal statistically while users suffer, and can be anomalous while nothing is wrong. Never let it be the only alarm on a user-facing path.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Metric Math Alarms: Alarm on the Right Quantity</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The single highest-leverage change most teams can make is alarming on <strong>rates and ratios instead of counts</strong>.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Error rate, not error count.</strong> <code>100 * errors / requests</code> is stable as traffic grows and correctly ignores a proportional increase in errors during a traffic spike — while still firing when a small absolute number of errors represents a large fraction of a quiet period.</li>\n<li><strong>Guard the denominator.</strong> At 3 a.m. with two requests, one error is a 50% error rate and a spurious page. Combine the ratio with a minimum-volume condition — either a composite alarm requiring both \"error rate high\" and \"request count above N\", or a single expression such as <code>IF(requests &gt; 100, 100*errors/requests, 0)</code>.</li>\n<li><strong>Handle sparsity with FILL.</strong> <code>FILL(errors, 0)</code> turns a quiet minute into a legitimate zero instead of a gap, and <code>FILL(requests, 1)</code> avoids division by zero. Without these, a low-traffic service oscillates into INSUFFICIENT_DATA and back, generating transitions that look like flapping.</li>\n<li><strong>SEARCH for dynamic fleets.</strong> <code>SUM(SEARCH('{Ns,Service,InstanceId} MetricName=\"Errors\"', 'Sum', 60))</code> covers every instance including ones launched after the alarm was created. The alarm expression must return exactly one time series, so the SEARCH must be wrapped in an aggregate. Choose the aggregate deliberately: <code>SUM</code> gives a fleet-level view that will not fire for one sick host; <code>MAX</code> fires if any single member is bad and is correspondingly noisier. For \"is any host broken\", per-host alarms generated from IaC are often better than a MAX rollup, because the alarm name tells you which host.</li>\n<li><strong>Composite quantities.</strong> Metric math also lets you alarm on things no single metric expresses: queue depth divided by processing rate to get an estimated drain time, or bytes divided by a provisioned limit to get a utilization percentage against a quota.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Composite Alarms and Noise Suppression</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>composite alarm</strong> evaluates a boolean rule over the states of other alarms — <code>ALARM(\"child-a\") AND ALARM(\"child-b\")</code>, with <code>OR</code>, <code>NOT</code>, and the state functions <code>ALARM()</code>, <code>OK()</code>, <code>INSUFFICIENT_DATA()</code>, plus the literals <code>TRUE</code> and <code>FALSE</code>. Composite alarms can reference other composite alarms, letting you build a small dependency graph. Three patterns matter:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Correlation to raise precision.</strong> Page only when high latency <em>and</em> an elevated error rate <em>and</em> healthy-host count below target coincide. Each child alone is noisy; the conjunction is almost always a real incident. The children keep their own actions removed (or pointed at a ticket queue) so only the composite pages.</li>\n<li><strong>Dependency suppression.</strong> When the database is alarming, twelve services that depend on it will also alarm. Express the leaf alarms as <code>ALARM(\"api-errors\") AND NOT ALARM(\"database-down\")</code> so the downstream noise disappears while the real upstream cause pages once. This is the single most effective structural fix for a noisy rotation.</li>\n<li><strong>Actions suppressor.</strong> A composite alarm can nominate another alarm as its <strong>actions suppressor</strong>, with a <em>wait period</em> and an <em>extension period</em>. While the suppressor alarm is in ALARM, the composite still changes state but takes no actions. This is the clean mechanism for maintenance windows and planned deploys: an alarm that goes ALARM when a deployment is in progress (driven by a metric your pipeline publishes) suppresses the composite's pages for the duration plus the extension period, without anyone disabling alarms by hand and forgetting to re-enable them.</li>\n</ul>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">                   [ deploy<i class=o>-</i><i class=k>in</i><i class=o>-</i>progress ]  <i class=o>&lt;</i><i class=c>-- actions suppressor</i>\n                              <i class=o>|</i>\ncheckout<i class=o>-</i>page<i class=o>-</i>composite  (PAGES <i class=k>on</i><i class=o>-</i><i class=k>call</i>)\n     rule: (ALARM(&quot;checkout-error-rate&quot;) <i class=k>OR</i> ALARM(&quot;checkout-p99-latency&quot;))\n           <i class=k>AND</i> <i class=k>NOT</i> ALARM(&quot;rds-primary-unhealthy&quot;)\n           <i class=k>AND</i> <i class=k>NOT</i> ALARM(&quot;dependency-auth-service-down&quot;)\n        <i class=o>|</i>                    <i class=o>|</i>                       <i class=o>|</i>\n[error<i class=o>-</i>rate]         [p99<i class=o>-</i>latency]           [rds<i class=o>-</i><i class=k>primary</i><i class=o>-</i>unhealthy]\n(<i class=k>no</i> action)          (<i class=k>no</i> action)             (PAGES DB <i class=k>on</i><i class=o>-</i><i class=k>call</i> once)</code></pre></div>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Mechanics That Decide Whether an Alarm Is Right</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Evaluation periods vs. datapoints to alarm.</strong> These are the M-of-N controls. <em>Evaluation periods</em> (N) is how many of the most recent periods CloudWatch looks at; <em>datapoints to alarm</em> (M) is how many of those must breach for the alarm to fire. Setting 3 of 5 tolerates transient spikes while still firing quickly on a sustained problem; setting 1 of 1 is maximally fast and maximally noisy. The trade-off to articulate is detection latency versus false positives: M of N with M &lt; N smooths noise but, once in ALARM, also delays the return to OK, because it takes N non-breaching periods to clear.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Treat missing data.</strong> Exactly four options, and the difference is a common interview probe:</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; Two consequences follow. First, an alarm can evaluate against data older than you expect. Second, and more surprising in practice, <strong>late-arriving data can change an already-made decision</strong>: many AWS services publish with a delay, so a period CloudWatch evaluated as missing can later be filled in, and a subsequent evaluation that now sees that datapoint may flip the alarm. This is the usual explanation for an alarm that fires and clears seconds later, or that seems to react to a period you thought had already been decided. The mitigations are to use a period at least as long as the metric's publishing interval, to prefer M-of-N over 1-of-1 on delayed metrics, and to accept that alarms on sparse or delayed metrics are inherently less crisp.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Actions on every transition.</strong> An alarm has three action lists: <code>AlarmActions</code>, <code>OKActions</code> and <code>InsufficientDataActions</code>. OK actions are how you get auto-resolution in the incident tool so someone does not investigate an already-recovered problem — worth wiring up, but be careful that a flapping alarm then generates paired page/resolve spam. INSUFFICIENT_DATA actions are usually a mistake to page on: the common cause is a resource that was legitimately deleted or a metric that stopped publishing during a deploy. The better pattern is to route INSUFFICIENT_DATA to a ticket or a dashboard, and if silence genuinely is failure, express that with <code>treat missing data = breaching</code> so it becomes a real ALARM with a real reason instead.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Alert Hygiene as an Engineering Practice</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Page on symptoms, not causes.</strong> Users do not care that CPU is at 90%; they care that checkout is failing or slow. Page on user-visible symptoms — error rate, latency, availability, successful order rate. Cause metrics (CPU, memory, connection pool, disk) belong on dashboards and in runbooks, where they help you diagnose the symptom you were paged for.</li>\n<li><strong>Three tiers, not one.</strong> <em>Page</em> for things requiring human action within minutes. <em>Ticket</em> for things requiring action within days (a disk trending toward full, a certificate expiring in three weeks). <em>Dashboard only</em> for everything else. Most organizations have one tier and it is \"page\".</li>\n<li><strong>Every page has a runbook and an action.</strong> If the responder's only possible action is \"wait and see\", it should not be a page. A page with no documented next step is a bug in the alerting system.</li>\n<li><strong>Measure the alerting system.</strong> Track pages per on-call shift, pages outside business hours, percentage of pages that were actionable, percentage auto-resolved before human action, and time-to-acknowledge. Review them in a recurring forum. A rotation above roughly two pages per shift is unsustainable, and a false-positive rate above a small fraction means people will start ignoring pages.</li>\n<li><strong>Delete alarms.</strong> An alarm that has fired a hundred times and never led to an action is a liability. Deleting it is a legitimate and under-used fix.</li>\n<li><strong>Manage alarms as code.</strong> Alarms created in the console drift, lack owners, and survive the service they monitored. IaC-defined alarms have review, ownership, and deletion when the service is decommissioned.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Production Scenario: Cutting a Rotation from 40 Pages a Week to 6</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A platform rotation receives about 40 pages per week; the on-call survey says roughly 70% are non-actionable. The intervention, in order of leverage:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>1. Measure first.</strong> Export three months of alarm history (EventBridge alarm state-change events into a log group, or the incident tool's own data) and rank alarms by page count and by actionability. Typically 5 alarms produce 60% of the volume.</li>\n<li><strong>2. Kill the cause-based pages.</strong> High CPU, high memory and low disk on autoscaled fleets move from page to dashboard; they were never actionable because autoscaling already handled them.</li>\n<li><strong>3. Convert counts to ratios.</strong> The raw 5xx-count alarm, which fired on every traffic peak after a year of growth, becomes an error-rate expression with a minimum-volume guard.</li>\n<li><strong>4. Add dependency suppression.</strong> A composite alarm structure so that when the shared database or the auth service is alarming, the twelve dependent-service alarms do not page. This alone typically removes a third of the volume, because one incident used to produce a dozen pages.</li>\n<li><strong>5. Fix the flappers.</strong> Alarms on sparse metrics move from 1-of-1 to 3-of-5 with appropriate FILL and an explicit treat-missing-data choice, removing the fire/clear pairs caused by late-arriving data.</li>\n<li><strong>6. Deploy suppression.</strong> An actions-suppressor alarm driven by a deployment-in-progress metric, with an extension period, so the routine restart blip during every deploy stops paging.</li>\n<li><strong>7. Add what was missing.</strong> Noise reduction must not reduce coverage, so add symptom-level alarms that did not exist: end-to-end availability from a Synthetics canary, and a low-side anomaly-detection alarm on successful order rate, which catches whole classes of silent failure that no cause-based alarm ever would.</li>\n<li><strong>8. Institutionalize.</strong> Weekly page-volume review, a rule that every new page-tier alarm ships with a runbook link in its description, and a quarterly cull of alarms that never led to action.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The key sentence for an interview: the goal is not fewer alarms, it is a higher ratio of actionable pages — and step 7 is what proves you reduced noise rather than coverage.</p>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"I treat alarming as a design problem.
