list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Sources.</strong> Four telemetry types with different economics. <strong>Metrics</strong> are cheap, aggregated and pre-aggregated — they answer \"is it broken and how badly\". <strong>Logs</strong> are expensive and high-cardinality — they answer \"why\". <strong>Traces</strong> are sampled and relational — they answer \"where in the call graph\". <strong>Audit events</strong> (CloudTrail, Config) are a separate class with different retention and immutability requirements and different consumers. Conflating audit with operational telemetry is a design smell: they have different owners, different retention, and different tamper-resistance needs.</li>\n<li><strong>Collection.</strong> The <strong>CloudWatch agent</strong> for EC2 host metrics and logs; <strong>ADOT</strong> (the AWS Distro for OpenTelemetry) or a vanilla OpenTelemetry collector as the general-purpose pipeline for metrics, traces and increasingly logs; <strong>Fluent Bit</strong> for container log routing (via the ECS FireLens integration or as a DaemonSet on EKS); <strong>Embedded Metric Format</strong> to emit metrics from a log line without a separate <code>PutMetricData</code> call, which is the right pattern in Lambda; and native service integrations where they exist, because a metric AWS publishes for free is always cheaper than one you collect. The strategic choice here is to put an <strong>OpenTelemetry collector in the path even if the backend is CloudWatch</strong>, because it makes the backend a configuration decision rather than a rewrite.</li>\n<li><strong>Routing and processing.</strong> <strong>CloudWatch Logs subscription filters</strong> stream matching log events to Lambda, Firehose or Kinesis in near real time. <strong>Firehose</strong> is the workhorse for fan-out to S3, OpenSearch or a third-party platform, with a transformation Lambda that is the correct place to <strong>redact secrets and personal data</strong> before they land anywhere durable. <strong>CloudWatch Metric Streams</strong> continuously push metrics to Firehose, which is how you get near-real-time metrics into Grafana Cloud, Datadog or your own store without polling the API and paying per <code>GetMetricData</code> call. <strong>EventBridge</strong> routes state changes and audit events into automation.</li>\n<li><strong>Storage tiers.</strong> Hot in CloudWatch for fast alarms and recent debugging, warm in OpenSearch or CloudTrail Lake for rich search and SQL over weeks to months, cold in S3 with Athena for the long compliance tail. The design principle is that <strong>query latency requirements, not data age, determine the tier</strong> — and that moving data down the tiers should be automatic, not a project.</li>\n<li><strong>Consumption.</strong> Dashboards for humans, alarms for paging, SLO and error-budget reporting for prioritisation, detections for security, and compliance evidence for auditors. Each consumer has a different latency requirement and that is what justifies the tiering.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Build versus buy, honestly</h4>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; margin-top: 0.75rem\">\n<table style=\"width: 100%; border-collapse: collapse; font-size: 0.92rem\">\n<thead><tr style=\"text-align: left\" data-box=\"1\">\n<th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Option</th>\n<th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Cost model</th>\n<th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Coverage</th>\n<th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Operational burden</th>\n<th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Choose it when</th>\n</tr></thead>\n<tbody>\n<tr>\n<td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>CloudWatch native</strong></td>\n<td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Per metric, per GB logs ingested and stored, per alarm, per dashboard, per query scanned. First, <strong>correlation quality</strong> is the real differentiator between tiers, not feature count: the value of a platform is how fast it takes you from \"an alarm fired\" to \"this deployment, this dependency, this query\". CloudWatch's correlation across metrics, logs and traces has improved substantially but remains weaker than the best commercial platforms; that gap is what you are actually paying for. Second, <strong>the cost comparison is usually done wrong</strong> — teams compare the vendor invoice to the CloudWatch invoice, and ignore the engineer-months of building dashboards, maintaining collectors, running OpenSearch clusters and writing correlation glue. Compare total cost of ownership over three years including people, and the answer often flips. Third, <strong>OpenTelemetry is the portability layer</strong> and it changes the shape of the decision: if applications emit OTLP to a collector, the backend becomes a collector configuration change. That does not make migration free — dashboards, alert rules and runbooks are the real switching cost, and they do not port — but it converts the biggest lock-in risk, re-instrumenting every service, into a manageable one. Instrumenting with OTel while sending to CloudWatch today is an entirely defensible position and the one I would usually recommend.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Governance: what keeps the platform useful at scale</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Naming and tagging standards.</strong> Every log group, metric namespace, alarm, dashboard and trace attribute carries <code>service</code>, <code>environment</code>, <code>team</code> and <code>criticality</code>. Without this you cannot attribute cost, cannot route an alarm to the right on-call, and cannot filter a dashboard to one service. Enforce it with tag policies and a Config rule, and reject non-conforming resources in CI rather than remediating later.</li>\n<li><strong>Ownership of alarms and dashboards.</strong> Every alarm has a named owning team, a runbook link in its description, and a defined severity. Alarms with no owner are how you get a pager that everybody ignores. Review alarms that have never fired (probably wrong) and alarms that fire constantly (definitely wrong) on a schedule.</li>\n<li><strong>Retention and data classification.</strong> Set retention deliberately per log group — days for debug logs, weeks for application logs, months for access logs, years only for audit and compliance data. The CloudWatch default of never expiring is the single most common source of silent spend. Classify data so the policy is mechanical: operational, sensitive, regulated.</li>\n<li><strong>Keeping secrets and personal data out of logs.</strong> This is a design problem, not a discipline problem. Structured logging with an explicit allow-list of fields beats trying to remember not to log a request body. Redact in the Firehose transformation Lambda before data lands durably. Run <strong>CloudWatch Logs data protection policies</strong>, which detect and mask sensitive-data patterns in log groups, and scan with Macie where data reaches S3. Remember the asymmetry: a secret logged once is a secret rotated, and a log line containing personal data inherits every retention and deletion obligation that data carries — which is why it must never get into a seven-year archive.</li>\n<li><strong>Cost attribution.</strong> Tag telemetry resources by team and publish per-team observability spend on a dashboard that the teams see. Visibility alone changes behaviour more reliably than any policy; teams that can see they are spending more on logs than on compute will fix it themselves. Set anomaly detection on the observability spend line.</li>\n<li><strong>Configuration as code.</strong> Alarms, dashboards, log groups with retention, metric filters, detections and collector configuration all live in version control and deploy through the same pipeline as application code. A dashboard clicked together in the console is undiscoverable, unreviewable and gone when someone tidies up.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The maturity path teams actually walk</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Level 1 — service metrics and alarms.</strong> The metrics AWS gives you free (ALB 5xx and latency, RDS CPU and connections, SQS queue depth and age, Lambda errors and throttles), with a small number of alarms on user-visible symptoms. Cheap, fast, and it catches most outright outages. Most teams stop here too long.</li>\n<li><strong>Level 2 — structured logging and dashboards.</strong> JSON logs with consistent fields, correlation IDs threaded through, per-service dashboards, Logs Insights queries saved as runbook steps, and metric filters turning log patterns into alarmable metrics. This is where debugging stops being archaeology.</li>\n<li><strong>Level 3 — tracing and SLOs.</strong> Distributed tracing across service boundaries, and the cultural shift from \"is the CPU high\" to \"are we meeting the availability and latency objective, and how much error budget is left\". SLOs are what make alerting sane, because they let you page on user-visible symptoms and let everything else be a ticket.</li>\n<li><strong>Level 4 — automated remediation and detection engineering.</strong> EventBridge-driven auto-remediation for known failure modes, anomaly detection where static thresholds fail, security detections with owners and tests, and observability data feeding capacity and cost decisions rather than only incidents.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The advice worth giving with the ladder: <strong>do not skip levels</strong>. Tracing without structured logging and correlation IDs produces traces you cannot join to anything. SLOs without reliable service metrics produce numbers nobody believes. Auto-remediation without good detections automates the wrong action confidently.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Who owns observability?</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The two failure modes are symmetric. A <strong>fully centralised</strong> observability team becomes a ticket queue: they own dashboards for services they do not understand, they are paged for alarms they cannot action, and product teams treat monitoring as someone else's job. A <strong>fully decentralised</strong> model produces twenty incompatible logging formats, no shared correlation ID, duplicated spend, and no way to answer a question that crosses service boundaries.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The answer that works is a <strong>platform team providing a paved road</strong>. The platform team owns the shared infrastructure (collectors, pipelines, storage tiers, the audit and detection platform), the standards (naming, tagging, log schema, retention classes), the libraries and modules that make the right thing the easy thing (an instrumented service template, a Terraform module that creates a log group with retention and standard alarms), and the cross-cutting visibility that no single team can build. <strong>Product teams own their own telemetry, their own dashboards, their own alarms and their own on-call</strong> — because the people who can act on an alert must be the people who receive it. The platform team's success metric is adoption of the paved road, not tickets closed; if teams are routing around it, the road is not paved well enough. Security detections are the one area that stays centralised, for the same reason audit data does: the people being monitored should not own the monitoring.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Worked design 1: regulated fintech, 40 services, AWS-only</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Constraints.</strong> 40 microservices on EKS and Lambda, single cloud, strict change control, seven-year audit retention, a six-person platform team, and an auditor who asks for evidence quarterly. Cost matters but compliance failure matters more.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Design.</strong> Collection with ADOT as a DaemonSet on EKS and the ADOT Lambda layer, applications instrumented with OpenTelemetry SDKs emitting OTLP — so the backend stays replaceable. Metrics and traces to CloudWatch and X-Ray; container logs via Fluent Bit to CloudWatch Logs with per-log-group retention set by data class. Subscription filters on the sensitive log groups feed a Firehose with a redaction transform to S3 for the long tail, and CloudWatch Logs data protection policies mask sensitive patterns at source. Audit is a separate plane: an organization CloudTrail to a log archive account with Object Lock compliance mode for the full seven years, plus a CloudTrail Lake event data store holding management events and regulated-bucket data events for 18 months as the investigative surface, federated to Athena so audit data joins the resource-ownership table. GuardDuty, Security Hub and Access Analyzer enabled organization-wide from the security account.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Why not a third-party platform?</strong> Single cloud removes the main advantage, the data-residency and processor-approval overhead of exporting regulated telemetry is real, and six people can run this. <strong>Why OpenTelemetry anyway?</strong> Because the decision above is right today and may not be in three years, and OTel makes that a collector change rather than a re-instrumentation programme. <strong>The evidence story</strong> — which is what the auditor actually buys — is: organization trail with log file validation, scheduled <code>validate-logs</code> output archived, SCPs preventing tampering, Object Lock, and Security Hub standard scores over time.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Worked design 2: hybrid retailer, AWS plus on-premises, seasonal peak</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Constraints.</strong> Core commerce on AWS, warehouse and point-of-sale systems on-premises, a legacy datacentre that will exist for years, 10x traffic at seasonal peak, 200 engineers across many teams, and a hard requirement to see a customer journey end to end across both estates.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Design.</strong> Here the cross-estate requirement dominates and a <strong>third-party platform</strong> is the right call — CloudWatch cannot see the datacentre, and building the correlation layer yourself is a multi-year project that is not this company's business. Instrument everything with <strong>OpenTelemetry</strong> and run collectors in both estates, so the vendor relationship stays negotiable. Feed AWS-native metrics into the platform with <strong>CloudWatch Metric Streams to Firehose</strong> rather than API polling, which is both cheaper and lower-latency. Keep CloudWatch as the hot tier for AWS alarms and as the fallback when the vendor has an incident — a dependency you cannot alarm on during a vendor outage is a real risk at peak.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>The governance is the hard part, not the architecture.</strong> With 200 engineers and per-GB, per-host and per-custom-metric pricing, cost control is a standing function: sampling policy for traces (head sampling for volume, tail sampling to keep the errors and slow requests that matter), an explicit cardinality budget with a review for any new high-cardinality dimension, ingest quotas per team, and per-team spend published where teams can see it. Retention tiering is what makes peak affordable: days of full-fidelity data in the platform, everything to S3 for the long tail with Athena for retrospective analysis. Plan the peak explicitly — observability load scales with traffic, so a 10x traffic event is a 10x telemetry bill unless sampling adapts, and the platform itself must be load-tested before peak, because losing observability during the highest-revenue week is its own incident. Audit stays on AWS-native rails regardless: organization CloudTrail to a log archive account, with a filtered subset forwarded to the platform for correlation rather than the whole stream.</p>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"I design it in five layers: sources, collection, routing, tiered storage, and consumption.
