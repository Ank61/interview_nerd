Senior interviews probe this because it is where engineering judgment, statistics, and organizational politics intersect: the technical part is easy and the part that fails in practice is enforcement. The gap between SLO and SLA is your margin to fix a problem before money and trust are at stake; the gap between actual performance and the SLO is the error budget you deliberately spend on shipping features. An SLO of 100% is not ambitious, it is incoherent — it forbids all deployments and all risk, and it makes the error budget zero so the entire framework degenerates.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The error budget is the conceptual payload. It reframes reliability from a moral question (\"we should never break things\") into an engineering resource with a quantity. Below budget, you are free to ship aggressively — unused budget is arguably wasted velocity. Exhausted budget triggers a pre-agreed consequence: freeze risky changes, redirect the team to reliability work, and require an explicit override at a named level of authority to ship anyway. The nines are worth internalizing because the difference between them is enormous: 99% is about 7.2 hours of monthly budget, 99.9% about 43 minutes, 99.95% about 21 minutes, and 99.99% about 4.3 minutes — which is less than most human response times, meaning four nines is an architecture statement (automatic failover, no human in the loop) rather than an effort statement.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Choosing SLIs That Mean Something</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A good SLI is measured at the boundary the user actually touches, is a ratio of <em>good events</em> to <em>valid events</em>, and moves when users are unhappy. That last clause disqualifies most infrastructure metrics: CPU utilization, memory pressure, thread pool depth and connection counts are diagnostic inputs, not SLIs. Nobody has ever been unhappy about CPU.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Availability SLI.</strong> Successful requests divided by valid requests. The design work is in the definitions: a 5xx is clearly bad; a 4xx is usually the client's fault and should be excluded from <em>valid</em> events (otherwise a customer's buggy integration burns your budget); a 429 is arguably your fault if you under-provisioned. Write these rules down, because they are where SLO arguments actually happen.</li>\n<li><strong>Latency SLI.</strong> Better expressed as a <em>threshold ratio</em> than as a percentile: \"fraction of requests served in under 300 ms\" rather than \"p99 under 300 ms\". The ratio form composes into an error budget arithmetically, is aggregatable across time and resources, and avoids the percentile-averaging trap entirely. A slow request should also count as a failed request for the availability budget if it is slow enough that the user gave up.</li>\n<li><strong>Measure at the right edge.</strong> A load balancer metric misses DNS and client network problems; RUM or a Synthetics canary sees closer to the real user experience but also captures problems you cannot fix. Many organizations run both: a server-side SLI as the contractual-ish internal target and a client-side one as reality check.</li>\n<li><strong>Quality and freshness SLIs.</strong> For pipelines and async systems, availability and latency are the wrong shape. Use data freshness (fraction of the time the pipeline lag is under N minutes), completeness, or correctness.</li>\n<li><strong>Few of them.</strong> Two or three SLIs per user-facing journey. A service with fifteen SLOs has none, because nobody can act on fifteen budgets.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Computing the SLI with Metric Math</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">If your service emits <code>GoodRequests</code> and <code>ValidRequests</code> counters (or you can derive good as valid minus bad), the SLI is a one-line expression: <code>100 * FILL(good, 0) / FILL(valid, 1)</code>. For a latency-threshold SLI you need the application to emit the count of requests under the threshold, which is a single extra counter and vastly easier to reason about than reconstructing it from percentiles. The FILL calls matter for the same reason as in alarming: without them, quiet periods produce gaps that break the division and push the alarm into INSUFFICIENT_DATA. Error budget <em>remaining</em> can be expressed directly too: <code>100 * (1 - (badEvents / (validEvents * (1 - target))))</code>, which yields a percentage of budget left and makes a genuinely useful dashboard tile and a burn-down graph.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Burn Rate Alerting — The Core Idea</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Burn rate</strong> is how fast you are consuming the error budget relative to the rate that would exactly exhaust it over the SLO window. A burn rate of 1 means you will finish the budget precisely at the end of the window. A burn rate of 14.4 means you will consume the entire 30-day budget in about 50 hours. Numerically, burn rate is the observed error ratio divided by the budgeted error ratio: if your SLO is 99.9%, the budgeted error ratio is 0.001, so a measured 1.44% error rate is a burn rate of 14.4.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Why this beats a static threshold: a static error-rate page says \"errors are above 2%\", which conveys nothing about consequence. A burn-rate page says \"at this rate you will exhaust a month's reliability budget in two hours\", which is a statement about impact that automatically scales with the SLO's strictness and with traffic. It also solves the two failure modes of threshold alerting simultaneously — a brief spike that consumes a trivial slice of budget does not page, while a small but sustained degradation that a threshold would never catch does eventually page because it accumulates.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The standard construction is <strong>multi-window, multi-burn-rate</strong>. Each alert pairs a long window (which establishes that the problem is real and sustained) with a short window (which ensures the alert clears promptly once the problem stops, instead of hanging around for hours). Both windows must be breaching for the alert to fire.</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; margin-top: 0.75rem\">\n<table style=\"width: 100%; border-collapse: collapse; font-size: 0.92rem\">\n<thead><tr style=\"text-align: left\" data-box=\"1\"><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; The burn-rate expression itself is the error ratio over that window divided by the budgeted error ratio, computed by setting the metric's <code>Period</code> to the window length. Instrumentation is via <strong>OpenTelemetry</strong> — the AWS Distro for OpenTelemetry (ADOT) auto-instrumentation agent for Java, Python, Node.js and .NET, enabled as a Lambda layer, an EKS add-on with an operator that injects the agent into pods, or by configuration on ECS and EC2 — so you generally do not change application code. It supports both period-based SLIs (evaluating a metric per period) and request-based SLIs (a ratio of good to total requests), tracks attainment and error budget for you, and can create <strong>burn rate</strong> configurations with your chosen look-back windows, exposing burn rate as a metric you can alarm on.</li>\n<li><strong>Correlation.</strong> Because the same OTel pipeline produces traces, the SLO view links straight to X-Ray traces, to the relevant logs, and to the dependency that caused the breach — which is the real time-saver compared with a hand-built SLO dashboard.</li>\n<li><strong>Relationship to ServiceLens and X-Ray.</strong> X-Ray is the distributed tracing backend. ServiceLens was the earlier console experience that stitched X-Ray traces together with CloudWatch metrics and logs into a service map. Application Signals is the successor and superset: it keeps the map, adds standardized golden-signal metrics per service and operation, and adds native SLO and error-budget management on top. In an interview, describe X-Ray as the trace data plane, ServiceLens as the older correlation view, and Application Signals as the current application-performance and SLO layer that consumes both.</li>\n<li><strong>Trade-offs.</strong> It costs money per service and per trace/signal, it requires the OTel agent to be deployed, and its automatic SLIs are opinionated — a genuinely custom SLI (data freshness in a pipeline, order-completion rate) still needs the metric-math approach. Many teams run both: Application Signals for request-driven services, hand-built SLOs for everything else.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Review Practice, and Why SLOs Fail Organizationally</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The technical work is the easy half. An SLO that nobody enforces is decoration — a dashboard tile that turns red and changes no behaviour. The practices that make it real:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>A recurring error budget review.</strong> Weekly or per-sprint, with the product owner present, walking the burn-down chart and asking what consumed the budget and whether the SLO is still the right target.</li>\n<li><strong>A pre-agreed, written policy for exhaustion.</strong> Typically: freeze non-essential changes, prioritize reliability work, and require a named executive to override. The policy must be agreed <em>before</em> the budget is exhausted, because negotiating it during an incident always ends with \"we'll ship anyway\".</li>\n<li><strong>Revisit the target.</strong> If you never come close to consuming the budget, the SLO is too loose and it is giving you no signal — tighten it, or accept you could be shipping faster. If you blow it every month, either the target is unrealistic or there is a real reliability investment to make; pretending otherwise just teaches everyone to ignore it.</li>\n<li><strong>Single ownership.</strong> Each SLO has one owning team who can actually change the outcome. An SLO on a journey that crosses six teams with no owner produces finger-pointing, not reliability.</li>\n<li><strong>Exclude what you cannot control, honestly.</strong> Planned maintenance and client-side errors may be excluded, but every exclusion is a place where the number drifts away from user experience, so keep them few and documented.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Interview Scenario: SLIs, SLO and Burn-Rate Alerts for a Checkout API</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Checkout handles about 3 million requests a day. The design:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>SLIs.</strong> Two. <em>Availability</em> = requests not returning 5xx, divided by valid requests, where valid excludes 4xx caused by client input but <em>includes</em> 429 because throttling is our capacity decision. <em>Latency</em> = fraction of valid requests completed in under 500 ms, measured at the ALB so it includes our full server-side path. Written down and agreed in advance.</li>\n<li><strong>Alerts.</strong> Three, replacing eleven threshold alarms. <em>Fast burn</em>: 14.4x over 1 hour AND over 5 minutes, pages immediately, meaning about 2% of the month's budget is already gone and the trajectory is hours to exhaustion. <em>Medium burn</em>: 6x over 6 hours AND 30 minutes, pages in business hours. <em>Slow burn</em>: 3x over 1 day AND 6 hours, opens a ticket.
