{"title":"CloudWatch Alarms — States, Thresholds, Actions, and Composite Alarms","tags":["cloudwatch","alarms","sns","alerting"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Collecting metrics is only useful if something notices when they go wrong. Nobody watches a dashboard at 3am. A <strong>CloudWatch alarm</strong> is the piece that watches for you: a rule that continuously evaluates one metric (or a metric math expression) against a threshold you define, changes state when it crosses that line, and takes an action — emailing a human, adding servers, rebooting an instance. Alarms are where monitoring becomes operations, and configuring them badly produces either silence during an outage or so much noise that people mute the channel.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The three alarm states</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An alarm is always in exactly one of three states, and actions fire on <em>transitions</em> between them, not repeatedly while a state persists.</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; margin-top: 0.75rem\">\n<table style=\"width: 100%; border-collapse: collapse; font-size: 0.92rem\">\n<thead><tr style=\"text-align: left\" data-box=\"1\"><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">State</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">What it means</th></tr></thead>\n<tbody>\n<tr><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>OK</strong></td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">The metric is within the threshold. Data is arriving and it looks fine.</td></tr>\n<tr><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>ALARM</strong></td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">The metric has breached the threshold for as long as you said it must.</td></tr>\n<tr><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>INSUFFICIENT_DATA</strong></td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Not enough data points to decide. Not good, not bad — <em>unknown</em>.</td></tr>\n</tbody>\n</table>\n</div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">INSUFFICIENT_DATA deserves a paragraph of its own because beginners consistently misread it as a failure and go hunting for a broken system. It simply means CloudWatch could not form an opinion. Common, entirely benign causes: the alarm was created seconds ago and has no history yet; the metric only reports when something happens (a Lambda that had no errors publishes no Errors data points at all, so there is literally nothing to evaluate); the period is finer than the data (asking for 60 seconds against basic 5-minute EC2 monitoring); the resource is legitimately stopped. Occasionally it <em>is</em> meaningful — a host that has died stops publishing — which is exactly why the \"treat missing data\" setting below exists.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The anatomy of an alarm</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Every alarm is assembled from the same parts. Reading a real one is much easier once you can name them.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Metric (or expression).</strong> Which series to watch — namespace, metric name, and the exact dimensions. Getting the dimensions wrong is the most common cause of an alarm that never leaves INSUFFICIENT_DATA.</li>\n<li><strong>Statistic.</strong> The arithmetic applied within each bucket — Sum for counters, Average for gauges, p99 for latency.</li>\n<li><strong>Period.</strong> The size of each bucket, in seconds. The alarm evaluates once per period, so the period sets your minimum reaction time.</li>\n<li><strong>Threshold and comparison operator.</strong> The line and the direction: GreaterThanThreshold, LessThanThreshold, GreaterThanOrEqualToThreshold, and so on.</li>\n<li><strong>Evaluation periods (N) and datapoints to alarm (M).</strong> The \"M out of N\" rule, explained next.</li>\n<li><strong>Treat missing data.</strong> What to do about empty buckets.</li>\n<li><strong>Actions.</strong> What happens on transition into ALARM, back to OK, or into INSUFFICIENT_DATA.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">M out of N — why one bad data point should not page anyone</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Evaluation periods</strong> is how many of the most recent periods CloudWatch looks at (call it N). <strong>Datapoints to alarm</strong> is how many of those must be breaching for the alarm to fire (call it M). If you only set evaluation periods, M equals N and every one of them must breach.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The reason this exists: a single one-minute CPU spike to 95% during a log rotation is normal, and nobody should be woken for it. Setting N=5 and M=3 means \"in the last five minutes, at least three were bad\" — enough to distinguish a sustained problem from a blip, while still tolerating one clean minute in the middle of an ongoing incident (which a strict \"5 consecutive\" rule would reset). The cost is reaction time: with a 60-second period, 3 out of 5 typically fires three to five minutes in. There are four options, and picking the wrong one gives you either false pages or silent blindness.</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; margin-top: 0.75rem\">\n<table style=\"width: 100%; border-collapse: collapse; A Lambda <strong>Errors</strong> alarm left on the default sits in INSUFFICIENT_DATA whenever the function has no errors — which is most of the time — making the dashboard look permanently broken; <code>notBreaching</code> is correct there. Conversely, a <strong>heartbeat</strong> alarm on a metric your batch job publishes every five minutes must use <code>breaching</code>: if the job dies, the metric stops, and treating silence as \"fine\" means the job can be dead for weeks in total quiet.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Alarm actions — what actually happens</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An alarm on its own changes a colour on a screen. Actions make it matter. You can attach different actions to ALARM, to OK, and to INSUFFICIENT_DATA transitions.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Notify an SNS topic.</strong> <strong>Amazon SNS</strong> (Simple Notification Service) is a fan-out notification channel: you publish one message to a <strong>topic</strong> and SNS delivers a copy to every <strong>subscriber</strong>. Subscribers can be email addresses, SMS numbers, an HTTPS endpoint (this is how PagerDuty, Opsgenie and Slack integrations work), an SQS queue, or a Lambda function for automated remediation. This indirection is the point — you wire the alarm to the topic once, and change who gets woken up by editing subscriptions rather than alarms. Note that email subscriptions must be confirmed by clicking a link, and an unconfirmed subscription is a classic reason \"my alarm didn't email me\".</li>\n<li><strong>EC2 actions.</strong> An alarm on an EC2 metric can <strong>stop</strong>, <strong>terminate</strong>, <strong>reboot</strong> or <strong>recover</strong> the instance. Recover is the useful one: on an underlying hardware failure it migrates the instance to healthy hardware keeping the same instance id, private IP and EBS volumes. Reboot on a failed status check is a common self-healing pattern.</li>\n<li><strong>Auto Scaling actions.</strong> An alarm can trigger a scaling policy to add or remove instances — the classic \"CPU above 70% for 3 minutes, add two servers\" loop, and its scale-in counterpart.</li>\n<li><strong>Systems Manager actions.</strong> Alarms can create an OpsItem or an incident, feeding a ticketing or incident-management workflow rather than just an inbox.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Composite alarms — turning ten pages into one</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>composite alarm</strong> watches other alarms rather than a metric, combining them with a rule expression using <code>AND</code>, <code>OR</code> and <code>NOT</code> over functions like <code>ALARM(\"name\")</code> and <code>OK(\"name\")</code>. Only the composite needs to notify anyone; the children can stay silent.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Why this matters for your sanity: when a database falls over, twelve alarms fire at once — latency, error rate, connection count, queue depth, health checks on four services. That is twelve pages for one problem, and the on-call engineer spends the first five minutes silencing their phone instead of fixing anything. A composite such as <code>ALARM(\"api-high-latency\") AND ALARM(\"db-connections-exhausted\")</code> sends one meaningful page. The other classic use is suppression: <code>ALARM(\"api-errors\") AND NOT ALARM(\"deployment-in-progress\")</code> avoids paging for the expected blip during a deploy. Composite alarms also support a <strong>suppressor alarm</strong> for exactly this, and they cost more per alarm than a standard one — still far cheaper than alert fatigue.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Beyond static thresholds: metric math and anomaly detection alarms</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A fixed number is not always the right line. A <strong>metric math alarm</strong> evaluates an expression instead of a raw metric — most usefully an <strong>error rate</strong>, <code>(errors / requests) * 100 &gt; 5</code>, which is scale-independent and therefore correct at both 60 and 6,000,000 requests a minute, unlike \"more than 50 errors\". You build it by supplying <code>--metrics</code> with the source metrics (ReturnData false) plus an expression entry (ReturnData true).</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An <strong>anomaly detection alarm</strong> uses a learned band instead of a threshold. You create a detector, then an alarm with the comparison operator <code>LessThanLowerOrGreaterThanUpperThreshold</code> (or just the upper or lower half) pointing at <code>ANOMALY_DETECTION_BAND(m1, 2)</code>, where the number is roughly how many standard deviations wide the band is — larger means more tolerant. This is the right tool for metrics with a strong daily or weekly rhythm, like request volume, where no single static number is correct at both lunchtime and 4am.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Choosing thresholds and avoiding alert fatigue</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Alert fatigue</strong> is what happens when alarms fire often enough that people stop reading them — and it is how real outages get ignored. A few rules that prevent it:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Alarm on symptoms users feel, not on internal numbers.</strong> High CPU is not a problem if requests are still fast. Slow responses and failed checkouts are problems. Page on the second kind; graph the first kind for diagnosis.</li>\n<li><strong>Every alarm must be actionable.</strong> If the honest response at 3am is \"hmm, interesting\" then it should be a dashboard widget, not a page. Ask \"what would I do about this right now?\" before creating it.</li>\n<li><strong>Base thresholds on observed behaviour.</strong> Look at two weeks of the metric before picking a number. Round-number guesses like 80% are almost always either too sensitive or useless.</li>\n<li><strong>Use M-out-of-N rather than a hair trigger</strong>, and set treat-missing-data deliberately on every single alarm.</li>\n<li><strong>Separate severities.</strong> Route \"wake someone up\" and \"look at it tomorrow\" to different SNS topics with different subscribers.</li>\n<li><strong>Prune.</strong> Any alarm that has fired repeatedly without anyone acting is either mistuned or unnecessary. Fix it or delete it.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Testing an alarm before you rely on it</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An untested alarm is a guess. You can force a state transition with <code>aws cloudwatch set-alarm-state</code>, supplying the alarm name, the state value and a reason. This fires the real actions — the SNS message really goes out — so it is exactly how you verify that the topic is wired up, the email subscription was confirmed, and the PagerDuty rotation actually rings. The forced state is temporary: CloudWatch re-evaluates on the next period and puts the alarm back where the data says it belongs. Test every alarm you intend to depend on, and test the whole chain to the human, not just the alarm.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Common beginner misconceptions</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>\"INSUFFICIENT_DATA means something is broken.\"</strong> It means CloudWatch cannot form an opinion — often because the metric is sparse and nothing bad has happened, or the alarm is brand new, or the dimensions do not match anything. Fix it with the right treat-missing-data setting, not by panicking.</li>\n<li><strong>\"An alarm fixes things.\"</strong> An alarm changes state and triggers an action. Unless that action is an EC2 recovery, a scaling policy or a remediation Lambda, the fixing is still done by a human.</li>\n<li><strong>\"One data point over the line should page someone.\"</strong> That is how you train a team to ignore alerts. Use evaluation periods and datapoints-to-alarm so sustained problems page and blips do not.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Alarms work on logs directly.\"</strong> They do not. Alarms evaluate <strong>metrics</strong>. To alert on a log pattern you first create a <strong>metric filter</strong> on the log group, which turns matching lines into a metric, and then alarm on that metric.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Actions repeat while the alarm is in ALARM.\"</strong> Actions fire on <em>transitions</em>. An alarm that stays in ALARM for six hours notifies once. Re-notification and escalation are the job of a paging tool subscribed to the SNS topic.</li>\n<li><strong>\"I created the alarm, so I'll be emailed.\"</strong> Only if an SNS subscription exists and was <strong>confirmed</strong> by clicking the link in the confirmation email. Verify with set-alarm-state.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Alarming on a raw error count is fine.\"</strong> It is scale-dependent: 50 errors is nothing at 5 million requests and catastrophic at 60. Alarm on a <strong>rate</strong> via metric math.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Alarms are free.\"</strong> Ten are free under the free tier, then they are billed per alarm metric per month, with high-resolution and composite alarms priced higher.</li>\n</ul>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"An alarm watches a metric or a metric math expression and moves between three states: OK, ALARM, and INSUFFICIENT_DATA — and INSUFFICIENT_DATA just means CloudWatch can't form an opinion, usually because the metric is sparse, not that something's broken.
