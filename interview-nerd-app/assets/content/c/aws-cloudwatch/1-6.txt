{"title":"Cross-Account and Cross-Region Observability","tags":["oam","cross-account","multi-region","governance"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Every organization past a certain size ends up with the same structural tension. Security and blast-radius arguments push workloads into many AWS accounts — per team, per environment, per compliance boundary — and latency and resilience arguments push them into multiple Regions. Meanwhile the on-call engineer at 3am needs <strong>one place to look</strong>, and CloudWatch is fundamentally a per-account, per-Region service: metrics live in the account and Region that produced them, log groups are regional, alarms evaluate metrics in their own Region. Reconciling those two facts is the whole topic, and the modern answer is <strong>CloudWatch cross-account observability</strong>, backed by governance decisions about who owns alarms, who pays, and how an operator can tell which account a graph refers to.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">CloudWatch Cross-Account Observability and OAM</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The model has two roles. A <strong>monitoring account</strong> is where humans and dashboards live. <strong>Source accounts</strong> are where workloads run and telemetry is produced. The plumbing is <strong>Observability Access Manager (OAM)</strong>, which introduces two resources:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Sink.</strong> Created in the monitoring account, in a specific Region. It is the endpoint that source accounts attach to, and its resource policy controls <em>who may attach</em> — typically expressed as an organization or organizational-unit condition so that every account in the OU is permitted without enumerating account ids.</li>\n<li><strong>Link.</strong> Created in each source account, pointing at the sink's ARN, declaring which <strong>resource types</strong> that account shares and optionally a <strong>label template</strong> used to render the source account's name in the monitoring account's console.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The shareable resource types cover the main telemetry families: <strong>metrics</strong> (<code>AWS::CloudWatch::Metric</code>), <strong>logs</strong> (<code>AWS::Logs::LogGroup</code>), <strong>traces</strong> (<code>AWS::XRay::Trace</code>), <strong>Application Signals</strong> services (<code>AWS::ApplicationSignals::Service</code>) and Internet Monitor monitors. A link can also carry <strong>filters</strong> so a source account shares only part of its telemetry — for example only log groups matching a name pattern, or only metrics in specified namespaces. That matters both for cost control in the monitoring account and for data minimization: a regulated account can share operational namespaces while withholding log groups containing sensitive payloads.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The critical property is that <strong>OAM does not copy data</strong>. Telemetry stays in the source account, owned and paid for there; the monitoring account is granted read access and the CloudWatch console, Logs Insights, metric math, dashboards and alarms in the monitoring account can reach across the link. Practically this means a monitoring-account engineer can graph a metric from three accounts on one widget, run one Logs Insights query spanning log groups in several accounts, and follow an X-Ray trace across account boundaries — without a replication pipeline, without duplicate ingestion charges, and without the staleness and failure modes a copy pipeline brings. The scale limits are generous: a single sink accepts links from a very large number of source accounts, and a source account may link to a small number of monitoring accounts (on the order of five), which is what lets a central SRE monitoring account and a separate security monitoring account coexist.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" centralization pipelines give you a second copy</strong>. Copies cost a second ingestion, can lag or break, and must be secured — but they survive the deletion of the source account's data, which is exactly what a security team wants. Mature organizations run both: OAM for operations, a hardened logging account for audit and security.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cross-Region Realities</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Metrics and log groups are regional.</strong> A metric published in eu-west-1 does not exist in us-east-1. There is no built-in metric replication.</li>\n<li><strong>Dashboards are the friendly exception.</strong> A single CloudWatch dashboard can contain widgets that render metrics from multiple Regions and, with OAM, multiple accounts, so the \"one pane of glass\" requirement is genuinely satisfied at the visualization layer.</li>\n<li><strong>Alarms are regional and cannot watch another Region's metric.</strong> This is the constraint that trips people up. An alarm created in us-east-1 evaluates only metrics in us-east-1. The standard workarounds are: (1) <strong>create the alarm where the metric lives</strong> and route its SNS action cross-Region to a central topic or incident tool — simplest, most reliable, and the default recommendation; (2) <strong>replicate the metric</strong> into a central Region with a scheduled Lambda that reads with <code>GetMetricData</code> and writes with <code>PutMetricData</code>, accepting the added latency, cost and a new failure point, which is only worth it when you must do metric math <em>across</em> Regions; (3) <strong>use a cross-Region-native service</strong> for the signal — Route 53 health checks and CloudWatch Synthetics canaries observe endpoints from outside and publish where you choose.</li>\n<li><strong>OAM links are created per Region.</strong> A source account operating in three Regions creates a link in each, all pointing at the single sink in the monitoring account's Region. Forgetting a Region is the most common reason a team reports \"account X is missing from the dashboard\".</li>\n<li><strong>Some telemetry is global-ish and lands in one Region.</strong> CloudFront, WAF for CloudFront, Route 53 and certain IAM-adjacent and CloudTrail global-service events surface in us-east-1 regardless of where the workload runs, which usually makes us-east-1 the pragmatic monitoring Region.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Governance: Ownership, Permissions, Naming, Blast Radius</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Who owns alarms and paging.</strong> The durable rule is that <strong>alarms live with the workload and paging is centralized</strong>. The team that can fix the problem owns the threshold, because they are the ones who will tune it; the routing to PagerDuty or an incident tool is a platform concern so that escalation policy, deduplication and on-call schedules are consistent. Alarms defined centrally against other teams' services rot quickly — nobody tunes an alarm they did not write.</li>\n<li><strong>Permissions design for the monitoring account.</strong> The monitoring account is a read-heavy surface: grant CloudWatch, Logs, X-Ray and Application Signals read permissions broadly and write permissions narrowly. Remember that cross-account visibility is a <strong>data-exposure decision</strong> — anyone with Logs Insights access in the monitoring account can read shared log groups from every linked account, so use link filters to exclude sensitive log groups, apply data protection policies at source, and restrict the unmask permission. The sink policy should be scoped by organization or OU condition rather than a list of account ids so that new accounts join automatically and unknown accounts cannot attach.</li>\n<li><strong>Naming and tagging so a graph is self-describing.</strong> At 3am an operator must be able to tell, from the widget alone, which account, environment and Region a line refers to. That means an enforced naming convention for log groups and dashboards that encodes domain, service and environment; the OAM link <strong>label template</strong> configured to render a human account name rather than a twelve-digit number; mandatory tags for owner, environment, cost-centre and service on every resource; and dashboard widget titles that include the Region. Two identically-named services in dev and prod appearing on one dashboard with no distinguishing label is a genuine incident risk.</li>\n<li><strong>The blast-radius argument for a dedicated observability account.</strong> Observability is what you use to diagnose a failure, so it must not share a failure domain with what it observes. A separate monitoring account means a compromised or misconfigured workload account — an IAM change, a service control policy mistake, a runaway process consuming API quota, or an account-level incident — does not take out your ability to see. It also gives a clean home for cross-cutting costs and a natural permission boundary for read-everything access. The counterweight is one more account to run and the need for disciplined access control, since it aggregates visibility into everything.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cost Allocation Across Accounts</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Because OAM reads in place, <strong>the source account keeps paying for producing the telemetry</strong> — log ingestion and storage, custom metrics, traces. That is usually the desired incentive: the team generating the volume sees the bill and has a reason to reduce it. What lands on the monitoring account is the <em>consumption</em> side: Logs Insights bytes scanned for cross-account queries, dashboard and API usage, and any alarms defined there. A central observability team should therefore expect a bill that scales with how much investigating happens, and should be prepared for the \"why is the platform team's CloudWatch bill so high\" conversation with an explanation of cross-account query charges. Where a centralization pipeline is used instead, the picture changes: the logging account pays a <em>second</em> ingestion charge on everything forwarded, which is exactly why you forward a filtered subset rather than everything. Cost allocation tags, consolidated billing with per-account cost reports, and per-log-group <code>IncomingBytes</code> dashboards published back to the owning teams are what keep this honest.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Interview Scenario: Fifty Accounts, Three Regions</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An organization runs roughly fifty AWS accounts across three Regions — production workloads split per domain, plus dev and staging accounts, plus shared services — and on-call currently switches consoles by hand. The design I would propose:</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Accounts.</strong> One dedicated <strong>observability account</strong> as the monitoring account for operations, and a separate hardened <strong>log archive account</strong> owned by security. These are different accounts on purpose: operators need broad read access, and the audit store needs restricted, write-once-ish access. <strong>OAM topology.</strong> An OAM sink in the observability account in us-east-1, with a resource policy conditioned on the organization id so any account in the organization may attach and nothing outside it can. Links deployed by <strong>CloudFormation StackSets</strong> targeting the organizational units, with one stack instance per account <em>per Region</em>, sharing metrics, log groups, traces and Application Signals. Non-production OUs share metrics and traces but filter log groups more aggressively, because nobody needs to read dev debug logs from the central account and it is a needless data-exposure surface. StackSets with automatic deployment to the OU means a newly vended account is linked on creation — the alternative, a manual step, guarantees gaps.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Alarms and paging.</strong> Alarms are defined in the workload accounts, in the Region of the metric, by the owning team, from a shared module so naming and severity conventions are uniform. Every alarm action targets a local SNS topic that forwards to the central incident tool, so escalation policy is centralized while thresholds stay with the people who tune them. Composite alarms in each account roll per-service alarms into one service-level page, which is what keeps a Region-wide dependency failure from producing four hundred pages. <strong>Logs.</strong> Account-level subscription filter policies deployed by StackSet ship a filtered subset — security-relevant and audit events — to Firehose in the log archive account, landing partitioned Parquet in S3 with lifecycle transitions and Athena for investigation; day-to-day operational log search happens through OAM against the source accounts, with no second copy. <strong>Cross-Region.</strong> Dashboards in the observability account carry multi-Region widgets with the Region in every title; regional alarms fan in via SNS; a small number of genuinely global signals — customer-facing endpoint health — come from Synthetics canaries and Route 53 health checks so there is one authoritative \"is the product up\" signal independent of any single Region. <strong>Conventions.</strong> Enforced log group naming, mandatory tags for owner, environment, service and cost-centre, and OAM label templates so every graph says <code>prod-payments</code> rather than a twelve-digit account number. <strong>Cost.</strong> Ingestion stays with the producing account so teams see their own volume; the observability account absorbs query cost, reported back per team from cost allocation tags.</p>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"I use CloudWatch cross-account observability: an OAM sink in a dedicated monitoring account, scoped by organization id, and links deployed by StackSets from every source account — one per account per Region — sharing metrics, logs, traces and Application Signals, with filters where data should not leave.
