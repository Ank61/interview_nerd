Reasoning: root should be used approximately never, so the base rate is near zero and the false-positive cost is negligible. Page on it, do not email it.</li>\n<li><strong>Console login without MFA.</strong> <code>ConsoleLogin</code> where <code>additionalEventData.MFAUsed</code> is <code>No</code> and the identity is not a federated SAML or IAM Identity Center session that enforced MFA upstream. This is both a detection and a compliance control.</li>\n<li><strong>Failed console logins, especially bursts.</strong> <code>ConsoleLogin</code> with <code>responseElements.ConsoleLogin</code> of <code>Failure</code>, alerting on a rate rather than a single event.</li>\n<li><strong>IAM policy and trust-policy changes.</strong> <code>PutUserPolicy</code>, <code>PutRolePolicy</code>, <code>AttachRolePolicy</code>, <code>AttachUserPolicy</code>, <code>CreatePolicyVersion</code>, and above all <code>UpdateAssumeRolePolicy</code>. A trust policy change is how an attacker grants a role to a principal in an account they control, and it is one of the least-watched, highest-impact events in AWS.</li>\n<li><strong>Access key creation.</strong> <code>CreateAccessKey</code>, particularly for a user other than the caller, and particularly on accounts that should be using short-lived federated credentials exclusively. Long-lived keys are the currency of AWS compromise.</li>\n<li><strong>Security group and NACL changes opening broad access.</strong> <code>AuthorizeSecurityGroupIngress</code> where the CIDR is <code>0.0.0.0/0</code> — and specifically on sensitive ports (22, 3389, 3306, 5432, 6379, 9200). Parse the request parameters rather than alerting on every ingress rule, or the signal drowns.</li>\n<li><strong>CloudTrail itself being tampered with.</strong> <code>StopLogging</code>, <code>DeleteTrail</code>, <code>UpdateTrail</code>, <code>PutEventSelectors</code>, <code>DeleteEventDataStore</code>. The SCP should already prevent these; the detection exists because the SCP might have a carve-out, and because an attempt that is <em>denied</em> is itself an extremely high-quality signal of compromise.</li>\n<li><strong>KMS key deletion scheduled or key disabled.</strong> <code>ScheduleKeyDeletion</code> and <code>DisableKey</code>. Destructive, largely irreversible after the waiting period, and a classic ransomware or sabotage step.</li>\n<li><strong>S3 public access settings changed.</strong> <code>PutBucketPublicAccessBlock</code> loosening settings, <code>DeleteBucketPublicAccessBlock</code>, <code>PutBucketPolicy</code> or <code>PutBucketAcl</code> introducing public access. The most common cause of a real data exposure headline.</li>\n<li><strong>Activity in an unusual Region.</strong> Any non-global-service event in a Region outside your approved list. This is the earliest reliable signal of stolen credentials being used for cryptomining, because attackers deliberately choose Regions defenders do not watch. Cheap to implement, very high signal.</li>\n<li><strong>AccessDenied spikes.</strong> A principal accumulating many authorization failures across many distinct actions and services in a short window — the behavioural shape of enumeration. One action failing 500 times is a broken deployment; 50 actions failing across 20 services is an attacker mapping their access.</li>\n<li><strong>Mass deletion patterns.</strong> A single principal issuing an abnormal volume of <code>Delete*</code> or <code>Terminate*</code> calls in a short window — ransomware, sabotage, or a very bad script. Rate-based, not event-based.</li>\n<li><strong>New or unusual resource-sharing actions.</strong> Sharing an AMI, snapshot or RDS snapshot with an external account (<code>ModifyImageAttribute</code>, <code>ModifySnapshotAttribute</code>, <code>ModifyDBSnapshotAttribute</code>) is a well-documented exfiltration path that never touches the network.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">CloudTrail Insights and its limits</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>CloudTrail Insights</strong> analyses your write management events and raises an Insights event when the <strong>call rate</strong> or the <strong>error rate</strong> for an API deviates sharply from the baseline it has learned. It is genuinely useful for the class of problem no hand-written rule catches: a runaway automation loop, a sudden burst of a rarely used API, a service that starts failing in a new way. Insights events are delivered to their own prefix on the trail (or into an event data store) and can be routed through EventBridge.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The limits matter as much as the capability, and stating them is what makes the answer senior. Insights covers <strong>write management events only</strong> — no read events, no data events. It needs a baseline period before it produces anything, so it is useless on day one and unreliable after a major workload change. Its latency is measured in tens of minutes, so it is a detection-and-investigation aid rather than a response trigger. It is inherently noisy in environments with bursty batch workloads, deployment waves or autoscaling, and it charges per volume of events analysed. Treat it as an additional lens on anomalies, never as the backbone of your detection strategy.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The managed services and which problem each solves</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Amazon GuardDuty — managed threat detection.</strong> Continuously analyses CloudTrail management events (and optionally S3 data events), VPC Flow Logs, Route 53 DNS query logs, and with additional protection plans EKS audit logs, RDS login activity, Lambda network activity and EBS malware scanning. It applies threat intelligence and machine learning you do not have to build. Finding types relevant to credential abuse include <code>UnauthorizedAccess:IAMUser/InstanceCredentialExfiltration.InsideAWS</code> (instance-profile credentials used from outside the instance — one of the highest-fidelity findings AWS produces), <code>Policy:IAMUser/RootCredentialUsage</code>, <code>Recon:IAMUser/MaliciousIPCaller</code>, <code>PrivilegeEscalation:IAMUser/AnomalousBehavior</code>, and the <code>CryptoCurrency:EC2/BitcoinTool.B!DNS</code> family for mining. <strong>Solves:</strong> \"I do not have the threat intelligence or the modelling to write these detections myself.\" Enable it organization-wide via delegated admin; it is one of the highest value-per-effort controls in AWS.</li>\n<li><strong>AWS Security Hub — aggregation and posture scoring.</strong> Normalises findings from GuardDuty, Inspector, Macie, IAM Access Analyzer, Config and partner tools into the AWS Security Finding Format, aggregates them across accounts and Regions, and continuously scores your environment against standards such as the AWS Foundational Security Best Practices, CIS AWS Foundations Benchmark, PCI DSS and NIST 800-53. <strong>Solves:</strong> \"findings are scattered across ten services and two hundred accounts and I have no single queue or score.\" It is the integration point your ticketing and paging should hang off.</li>\n<li><strong>Amazon Detective — graph-based investigation.</strong> Ingests CloudTrail, VPC Flow Logs, GuardDuty findings and EKS audit logs and builds a behavioural graph over a rolling window, letting you pivot from a finding to the principal, to everything that principal did, to the resources involved, with baselines showing what was normal for that entity. <strong>Solves:</strong> \"I have a finding; now I need to answer the scope question — what else did they touch — in minutes rather than hours of SQL.\" It is an investigation accelerator, not a detector.</li>\n<li><strong>IAM Access Analyzer — access reasoning.</strong> Uses automated reasoning over resource policies to find resources shared <strong>outside your zone of trust</strong> (a bucket, key, role, queue or Lambda function reachable by an external account or the internet), surfaces <strong>unused access</strong> (roles, users, permissions and credentials that have not been exercised), <strong>generates policies from CloudTrail activity</strong>, and validates and checks proposed policies. <strong>Solves:</strong> \"which of my thousands of resource policies actually grants external access, and what permissions does this role really need.\" It reasons about policy, which is a different question from what GuardDuty or Detective answer.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The mental model: <strong>GuardDuty detects, Security Hub aggregates and scores, Detective investigates, Access Analyzer reasons about permissions, and CloudTrail underlies all of them as the raw record.</strong> They overlap enough that people conflate them; being able to separate them cleanly is a reliable interview differentiator.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Detection quality engineering</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Every detection needs an owner and a runbook.</strong> An alert with no documented response is not a detection, it is a notification. The runbook should state what the alert means, the first three investigative steps, the containment action, and who to escalate to.</li>\n<li><strong>Budget false positives explicitly.</strong> A detection firing more than a few times a week without a genuine finding is training your responders to ignore it. Either tune it or delete it — a deleted noisy detection is better than a tolerated one, because tolerance generalises to the alerts that matter.</li>\n<li><strong>Allow-list by role, not by IP.</strong> The most common tuning mistake is excluding a source IP because the deployment pipeline is noisy. IPs churn, NAT gateways are shared, and an attacker who compromises an instance inside your VPC inherits the very IP you trusted. Exclude by <code>userIdentity.sessionContext.sessionIssuer.userName</code> — the underlying role — or by the principal ARN, which is far harder for an attacker to assume without generating its own signal.</li>\n<li><strong>Suppress with context, not blanket mutes.</strong> \"Do not alert on security group changes made by the Terraform role during a change window, from the pipeline account\" is a defensible rule. \"Do not alert on security group changes\" is not.</li>\n<li><strong>Test your detections.</strong> Deliberately trigger each one in a sandbox account on a schedule — create and delete a dummy access key, add and remove a wide-open security group rule, call an API you expect to be denied — and assert the alert arrived within its target time. Detections rot silently: a log group changes name, a metric filter pattern stops matching after a service changes its event format, an SNS subscription gets removed. Untested detection coverage is imaginary coverage.</li>\n<li><strong>Manage detections as code.</strong> Metric filters, EventBridge rules, Security Hub automation rules and Lake queries belong in version control with review, so a change to a detection has the same scrutiny as a change to production code.</li>\n<li><strong>Measure what matters.</strong> Track time-to-detect and time-to-contain per detection, plus the true-positive rate. If you cannot say which detection has ever caught something real, you cannot prioritise.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Investigation walkthrough: suspected credential compromise</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Trigger.</strong> GuardDuty raises <code>UnauthorizedAccess:IAMUser/InstanceCredentialExfiltration.InsideAWS</code> — credentials issued to an EC2 instance profile are being used from a different AWS account. Treat this as high fidelity; it is one of the strongest signals AWS produces.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>1. Establish identity and scope.</strong> From the finding, extract the role and the <code>accessKeyId</code> of the temporary session. In Lake or Athena, pull every event in the window where <code>userIdentity.accessKeyId</code> matches, and separately every event where <code>userIdentity.sessionContext.sessionIssuer.userName</code> is that role, because the attacker may have minted further sessions. Record the earliest event — that is your provisional compromise time, and everything before it is presumed clean.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>2. Characterise the activity.</strong> Group by <code>sourceIPAddress</code>, <code>userAgent</code> and <code>awsRegion</code>. Legitimate instance activity comes from the instance's own address with an AWS SDK user agent; attacker activity typically shows a different IP, a different or crafted user agent, and often Regions you do not use. Separate read from write: reconnaissance (<code>ListBuckets</code>, <code>GetCallerIdentity</code>, <code>DescribeInstances</code>, <code>ListRoles</code>) versus impact (<code>RunInstances</code>, <code>CreateUser</code>, <code>PutBucketPolicy</code>).</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>3. Hunt for persistence — the step people skip.</strong> Containment fails if the attacker has already established an independent foothold. Search the window for <code>CreateUser</code>, <code>CreateAccessKey</code>, <code>CreateLoginProfile</code>, <code>AttachUserPolicy</code>, <code>PutUserPolicy</code>, <code>CreateRole</code> and especially <code>UpdateAssumeRolePolicy</code> — a trust policy edited to allow an external account ID is a backdoor that survives revoking every credential you know about. Also check for new Lambda functions, EventBridge rules, snapshot sharing to external accounts, and changes to identity provider configuration.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>4. Contain.</strong> For a role, attach a deny-all policy and revoke existing sessions with the standard inline policy that denies all actions where <code>aws:TokenIssueTime</code> is before now — simply detaching permissions does not invalidate sessions already issued. For an IAM user, deactivate the access keys rather than deleting them, so you retain the evidence. Isolate the instance with a quarantine security group, snapshot its volumes before you touch anything, and preserve memory if you have the capability. Remove any backdoor principals found in step 3, and only then begin remediation.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>5. Assess data impact.</strong> This is where your earlier data-event decisions come home. If data events were enabled for the buckets in question, you can enumerate exactly which objects were read. If they were not, <strong>you cannot prove what was accessed</strong> — and absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, so you will likely be obliged to assume the worst for disclosure purposes. Spell that out in the answer; it is the single best argument for enabling data events on sensitive buckets despite the cost.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>6. Close the loop.</strong> Determine the initial access vector — an SSRF against a metadata endpoint that IMDSv2 would have blocked is the archetype here — and fix it. Then write the detection you wish you had had, add it to the tested set, and record time-to-detect and time-to-contain so the next incident can be measured against this one.</p>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"Detection engineering is treating alerts as a product.
