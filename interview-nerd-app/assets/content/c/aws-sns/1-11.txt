Everything that makes SMS hard is external: carriers decide whether your message is delivered, regulators decide what you may send and from where, and the per-message cost is thousands of times higher than an SQS request. A senior engineer answering an SMS question should sound like someone who has been burned by a registration deadline and a spend cap, not someone who has read the <code>Publish</code> API reference. The three axes are <strong>origination</strong> (who you appear to be), <strong>deliverability</strong> (whether carriers accept you), and <strong>spend</strong> (whether you can afford what you just triggered).</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Account posture: the SMS sandbox</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Every new AWS account starts in the <strong>SMS sandbox</strong>. In the sandbox you may send only to <strong>verified destination phone numbers</strong> — you add a number, AWS sends it a one-time verification code, you submit the code, and only then can you message it. There are limits on how many destination numbers you can verify and how many verification attempts you may make. This exists to stop a compromised or careless account from spraying messages at the world, and it catches teams by surprise in exactly one scenario: the feature works perfectly in development because the four engineers verified their own phones, then fails for every real customer the moment it ships.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Moving to <strong>production access</strong> is a support case in which you describe your use case, the message templates you will send, your expected volume, how recipients opt in, and how they opt out. It is reviewed by humans and it takes time — plan for days, not minutes. The operational lesson for an interview: <strong>account posture is a launch dependency with a lead time</strong>, belonging on the project plan next to the load test, not discovered in the final week. Sandbox status is per account and per Region, so a new Region for a new market is a new request.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Origination identities</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The <strong>origination identity</strong> is what the recipient's handset shows as the sender, and it is the single biggest determinant of whether your message arrives. First, <strong>sender IDs are not universal</strong>: they are unsupported in the United States and Canada, optional in many countries, and in others — India, Singapore and Saudi Arabia among them — require <strong>pre-registration of the sender ID and often the message templates themselves</strong> with the local regulator or carrier body. Unregistered traffic in those markets is filtered, sometimes silently. India's DLT regime, where both the header and the content template must be registered, is the canonical example: a message whose body has drifted even slightly from the registered template is rejected by the carrier, not by AWS. Second, some countries overwrite or replace whatever identity you supply, so you cannot assume the recipient sees what you configured.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">10DLC in the United States</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">For US A2P traffic on long codes, <strong>10DLC registration is a hard prerequisite</strong>, not an optimization. It is a two-stage process: you register a <strong>brand</strong> (your legal company identity, verified against external records, which yields a trust score) and then one or more <strong>campaigns</strong> (the specific use case — 2FA, account notifications, marketing — with sample message bodies, opt-in language and opt-in flow evidence). Approved campaigns are associated with your long codes. Your brand trust score and campaign type determine your <strong>throughput allocation</strong> in messages per second and your daily message limits with carriers.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">What happens to <strong>unregistered</strong> A2P traffic on US long codes is the part candidates should know: it is heavily filtered or blocked by carriers, and carriers also apply <strong>unregistered surcharges</strong>. The failure is nasty because it is <em>partial and carrier-specific</em> — one mobile network blocks you while another passes you through, so your aggregate success rate sags to something like 60% and looks like a random deliverability problem rather than a compliance one. Registration takes days to weeks and involves third-party vetting, so like sandbox exit it is a launch dependency with a calendar. Also note that campaign content is bound to the registered use case: sending marketing on a campaign registered for 2FA is a violation that can get the campaign suspended, taking your OTP flow down with it — which is a strong argument for separating those flows onto different origination identities.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Message types: transactional versus promotional</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SNS lets you set an <code>AWS.SNS.SMS.SMSType</code> message attribute (or an account default) to <strong>Transactional</strong> or <strong>Promotional</strong>. Transactional means the message supports a transaction the customer initiated — an OTP, a password reset, a delivery notification — and is routed for <strong>maximum reliability</strong> at a higher price. Promotional means marketing content, routed on a cost-optimized path and subject to additional filtering, including local quiet-hours restrictions in some jurisdictions. Setting an OTP as Promotional to save money is a false economy that produces exactly the symptom teams then spend a week debugging: a login flow with a 15% failure rate concentrated in particular carriers. Conversely, marking bulk marketing as Transactional is both expensive and, in some jurisdictions, a compliance problem. Set it explicitly <strong>per message</strong> rather than relying on the account default, because the account default is a single global setting that will inevitably be wrong for one of your flows.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Spend control</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SMS is the only SNS channel where a bug can cost real money at speed. Per-message prices vary by destination country from fractions of a cent to well over ten cents, so a retry loop against an international destination is a genuine financial incident. Three controls matter.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>The account monthly SMS spend limit</strong> (<code>MonthlySpendLimit</code>). This is a hard cap: once month-to-date spend reaches it, SNS <strong>stops sending</strong>. Raising it requires a support request, so treat it as a safety net and not as a budget, and raise it ahead of known seasonal peaks. Critically, hitting it is a <strong>silent outage</strong> — publishes stop being delivered and there is no page unless you built one.</li>\n<li><strong>The <code>SMSMonthToDateSpentUSD</code> CloudWatch metric.</strong> Alarm at 50%, 80% and 90% of the limit. The 80% alarm is the one that gives you time to file a limit increase before the cap stops your OTPs. Alarming <em>after</em> the cap is hit is not monitoring, it is an incident report.</li>\n<li><strong><code>AWS.SNS.SMS.MaxPrice</code></strong>, a per-message attribute giving the maximum you are willing to pay in USD for that message. If delivering it would cost more, it is not sent. This is the per-message circuit breaker against an unexpectedly expensive destination — useful when a phone-number field accepts arbitrary international input.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Beyond the AWS controls, build application-level ones: <strong>per-user and per-IP rate limits</strong> on any endpoint that triggers an SMS, because OTP endpoints are a favourite target for toll-fraud (SMS pumping), where an attacker drives traffic to premium-rate destinations they profit from. Restrict the destination country set to the markets you actually serve, and alarm on a sudden change in the country distribution of your sends — a spike in an unusual country code is the classic pumping signature.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Deliverability engineering</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Enable <strong>SMS delivery status logging</strong> by setting <code>DeliveryStatusIAMRole</code> and <code>DeliveryStatusSuccessSamplingRate</code> in the account SMS attributes. SNS then writes per-message records to CloudWatch Logs containing the destination, the price charged, the <strong>provider response</strong> and a status code. Those provider codes are the only real diagnostic data you have: they distinguish <em>invalid destination number</em> from <em>blocked by carrier</em> from <em>spam filtered</em> from <em>rate exceeded</em> from <em>unreachable handset</em>, and each implies a different fix. Aggregate them by destination country and by carrier where available; a deliverability problem is almost never global, it is one country or one network. The <code>SMSSuccessRate</code> CloudWatch metric gives the headline number, but on its own it is only a trigger to go read the logs.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Retries.</strong> Retry once, with a delay, and never blindly — a message rejected as an invalid destination will never succeed and retrying it just costs money. Retry on transient provider or network errors only, driven by the response code.</li>\n<li><strong>Fallback channels.</strong> For OTP, the mature design is SMS with fallback to <strong>voice call, email, or an authenticator app / push prompt</strong> after a timeout or a hard failure. A single-channel OTP makes carrier deliverability a hard dependency of your login flow, which is a poor availability posture for something so far outside your control.</li>\n<li><strong>Encoding and segmentation.</strong> A single SMS segment is <strong>160 characters in GSM-7</strong>. If the message contains any character outside the GSM-7 alphabet — most non-Latin scripts, many emoji, and some punctuation such as curly quotes — the whole message switches to <strong>UCS-2</strong> and a segment becomes <strong>70 characters</strong>. Concatenated (multipart) messages also spend a few characters per segment on headers, reducing them to 153 and 67 respectively. Since you are billed <strong>per segment</strong>, one stray curly apostrophe pasted from a design document can more than double the cost and segment count of every message you send. Sanitize templates to GSM-7 where possible, measure segment count in tests, and be deliberate about it for languages that require UCS-2.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Compliance</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SNS automatically handles standard <strong>opt-out keywords</strong> — a recipient replying STOP (and equivalents such as UNSUBSCRIBE, CANCEL, QUIT) is added to the account's opt-out list and SNS will no longer deliver to that number. You can inspect this with <code>ListPhoneNumbersOptedOut</code> and, within limits, reverse it with <code>OptInPhoneNumber</code>. The important nuances: opting a number back in is limited in frequency and <strong>must reflect a genuine re-consent</strong>, not a decision to ignore the opt-out; and the opt-out list is account-wide, so a user who unsubscribed from marketing also stops receiving your transactional messages unless you separate flows onto different origination identities or a different account. That surprise — a customer who replied STOP to a promotion and then never received their OTP — is a real production incident pattern.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Beyond opt-out, the obligations are jurisdictional: <strong>quiet hours</strong> restricting promotional sends to daytime local to the recipient (which means you must know the recipient's timezone, not your server's), prior express consent requirements, mandatory sender identification and opt-out instructions in the message body in some markets, and record-keeping of consent. The consequences of non-compliance escalate from carrier filtering, through campaign or short-code suspension, to regulatory fines — and in the US, TCPA exposure is per message, which at scale is existential. The engineering implication is that <strong>consent state is production data</strong>: it belongs in a durable store with an audit trail, checked before every send, and it is not something to keep only in a marketing tool.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cost model and budgeting</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Pricing is <strong>per message segment, per destination country</strong>, and the spread is enormous — a message to the US costs a fraction of a cent while some destinations cost more than ten cents per segment, and carrier pass-through fees in the US add a further per-message charge on top of the AWS price. This means SMS cost is driven by three multiplicands your code controls: number of recipients, number of <strong>segments</strong> per message (encoding and length), and the <strong>country mix</strong>. A feature whose audience shifts internationally can see its SMS bill multiply without any change in message volume. That is why an SMS-heavy feature deserves its <strong>own budget alarm</strong> and its own dashboard: a per-feature spend view, a country-mix breakdown, and an alarm on cost per successful verification rather than raw spend, so that a drop in deliverability (paying for messages that do not arrive) shows up as a cost signal too.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Migrating to AWS End User Messaging</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">AWS has consolidated SMS capability into <strong>AWS End User Messaging SMS</strong> (the service that absorbed the Amazon Pinpoint SMS and Voice capability), and new SMS work should generally start there. It exposes the things SNS SMS does not: explicit management of <strong>phone pools</strong> and origination identities with failover between them, <strong>configuration sets</strong> and event destinations for richer delivery telemetry into Kinesis Data Firehose or CloudWatch, <strong>two-way SMS</strong> with inbound message handling, <strong>protect configurations</strong> for country-level allow and block lists (a direct control against SMS pumping), per-identity opt-out lists rather than one account-wide list, and integrated 10DLC and sender-ID registration workflows. SNS SMS remains perfectly serviceable for simple, low-volume, one-way transactional sending, particularly when SMS is one subscriber type among several on an existing topic. The migration trigger is any of: needing two-way conversations, needing per-flow opt-out separation, needing country blocking for fraud control, needing origination failover, or needing delivery events streamed into an analytics pipeline.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Production scenario: OTP delivery failing in one country</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Support reports that users in India cannot complete signup: the OTP never arrives. US and UK signups are healthy. <code>SMSSuccessRate</code> is 96% overall, which looks fine, and nobody is alarming on it by country.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step one: segment the metric.</strong> A global success rate hides a single-country failure entirely — 96% overall is consistent with 100% everywhere except one market at 0%. Query the <strong>delivery status logs</strong> filtered to destinations with the country's dialing prefix and aggregate the provider response codes. This immediately distinguishes the classes of cause.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step two: read the failure reason.</strong> For India specifically, the most likely answers are regulatory: the <strong>sender ID or content template is not registered under DLT</strong>, or the message body has drifted from the registered template (a changed word, an added URL) so carriers reject it. Other candidates: the destination numbers are on the account-wide <strong>opt-out list</strong> because those users previously replied STOP to a marketing message — which is exactly the trap of sharing one opt-out list across transactional and promotional flows; the messages are being sent as <strong>Promotional</strong> rather than Transactional and are therefore filtered or held for quiet hours; the account has hit its <strong>monthly spend limit</strong>, which stops delivery silently (check <code>SMSMonthToDateSpentUSD</code> against <code>MonthlySpendLimit</code> before anything else, because it is a one-minute check that explains a total stop); or <code>AWS.SNS.SMS.MaxPrice</code> is set below the per-segment price for that country, so messages are suppressed as too expensive. Also check <strong>encoding</strong>: if the OTP template was localized into a non-Latin script it became UCS-2, which may have pushed it to multiple segments and, combined with a MaxPrice cap, silently blocked it.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step three: remediate and prevent.</strong> Fix the specific cause — re-register the template, correct the SMS type, raise the limit — then add the controls that would have caught it. Alarm on <code>SMSSuccessRate</code> and on successful-verification rate <strong>per destination country</strong>, not globally. Alarm on <code>SMSMonthToDateSpentUSD</code> at 80% of the cap. Separate OTP traffic onto its own origination identity (and, under End User Messaging, its own opt-out list) so marketing opt-outs can never break authentication. Add a <strong>fallback channel</strong> — voice OTP or email — so a country-level carrier problem degrades the signup funnel instead of closing it.
