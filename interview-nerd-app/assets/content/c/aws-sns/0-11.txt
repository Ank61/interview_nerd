{"title":"SNS vs SQS vs EventBridge vs Kinesis — Choosing the Right Service","tags":["sns","sqs","eventbridge","kinesis"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">This is the most frequently asked AWS messaging interview question, and it is asked because the four services genuinely overlap. All of them move data between parts of a system without the sender knowing who the receiver is. The good answer is not a memorised list of features — it is a clear grasp of two shapes, queue and topic, and then an honest account of what each service adds on top.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The one distinction everything else hangs off: queue versus topic</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>queue</strong> is a line of work. Each message is processed by exactly one consumer. If you run ten copies of a worker against one queue, the ten workers share the messages between them — that is work distribution, and it is how you scale processing. Consumers <strong>pull</strong>: they ask the queue for messages when they are ready, so a slow consumer simply drains the backlog more slowly and nothing is lost.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">QUEUE  (SQS)  <i class=c>-- one message, ONE consumer, consumers PULL</i>\n\n                        <i class=o>+</i><i class=c>-------------------------------+</i>\nproducer <i class=c>--- msg A --&gt;  |  A  B  C  D  E   (stored)     |</i>\n           msg B <i class=c>--&gt;    +-------------------------------+</i>\n                            <i class=o>|</i>         <i class=o>|</i>         <i class=o>|</i>\n                         pulls      pulls     pulls\n                            v         v         v\n                        worker <i class=n>1</i>   worker <i class=n>2</i>  worker <i class=n>3</i>\n                         gets A     gets B    gets C     <i class=o>&lt;</i><i class=o>-</i> <i class=k>each</i> message handled ONCE</code></pre></div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>topic</strong> is an announcement. Subscribers can be SQS queues, Lambda functions, HTTP/S endpoints, Kinesis Data Firehose delivery streams, email addresses, SMS numbers and mobile devices — the widest protocol range of the four, and the only one that reaches humans directly. <strong>Subscription filter policies</strong> let each subscriber receive only the messages matching a JSON pattern over message attributes or the body. Crucially, <strong>SNS does not store messages</strong>: it delivers, retries for a while, and then either dead-letters or drops. Standard topics give at-least-once delivery and no ordering guarantee; FIFO topics give strict ordering and deduplication, but only to SQS FIFO queues (and, more recently, some additional endpoint types — check current docs).</li>\n<li><strong>Amazon SQS — durable pull queue.</strong> A queue that stores messages until a consumer explicitly deletes them, with a retention window of up to 14 days. Consumers poll. A <strong>visibility timeout</strong> hides a message while one worker is processing it so no one else takes it; if the worker dies without deleting it, the message reappears and someone else retries. A <strong>dead-letter queue</strong> catches messages that failed too many times. This is the service for work distribution, buffering, and absorbing traffic spikes — the queue is the shock absorber between a fast producer and a slow consumer.</li>\n<li><strong>Amazon EventBridge — the event bus with routing rules.</strong> An <strong>event bus</strong> receives events and evaluates <strong>rules</strong> against them; each rule has an event pattern matched against the event's whole JSON structure and a list of targets to invoke. The routing is richer than SNS filtering: prefix, suffix, numeric ranges, anything-but, wildcards, and matching on nested fields, with over twenty target types. What makes EventBridge distinctive is what feeds it and what surrounds it — <strong>AWS service events</strong> arrive on the default bus automatically (an EC2 instance changed state, a CodePipeline stage failed), <strong>SaaS partner event sources</strong> deliver events from third parties such as payment or support providers straight into a bus, a <strong>schema registry</strong> discovers the shape of your events and generates code bindings, <strong>archive and replay</strong> lets you retain matched events and re-deliver them later, and <strong>EventBridge Scheduler</strong> runs one-off or recurring scheduled invocations. Latency is typically somewhat higher than SNS and the per-event cost is higher too.</li>\n<li><strong>Amazon Kinesis Data Streams — the ordered, replayable log.</strong> A stream is an append-only sequence of records split into <strong>shards</strong> (parallel lanes). Records are <strong>retained</strong> — 24 hours by default, extendable to a year — and consumers track their own position, called an <strong>iterator</strong> or checkpoint. That means several independent consumer applications can read the same records at their own pace, and any of them can <strong>replay</strong>: rewind to an earlier point and re-process history, which is exactly what you do after fixing a bug or launching a new analytics job that needs yesterday's data. Records are strictly ordered within a shard, keyed by a <strong>partition key</strong>, so all events for one user land in one shard in order. It suits high-throughput telemetry, clickstreams, IoT and real-time analytics.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The decision table</h4>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">They combine — this is normal, not a cop-out</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Interviewers like candidates who know these are not four competing products but four pieces that fit together. The combinations below are standard architecture, not clever tricks.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>SNS to SQS fan-out — the default durable pattern.</strong> Publish once to a topic; each consuming team subscribes its own queue. Every team gets every message (topic semantics), and each team's messages sit safely in its own queue until its workers get to them (queue semantics), with independent retries, visibility timeouts and DLQs. One team's outage cannot affect another's. If you learn one pattern, learn this one.</li>\n<li><strong>EventBridge to SQS.</strong> Same reasoning at the bus level: put a queue in front of a target that is slow, rate-limited, or occasionally down, so EventBridge's delivery attempt succeeds immediately and the target drains at its own pace.</li>\n<li><strong>SNS to Firehose to S3.</strong> Subscribe a Kinesis Data Firehose delivery stream to a topic so every published message is also batched into S3. Since SNS stores nothing, this is how you keep a durable record of what was published.</li>\n<li><strong>Kinesis to Lambda to SNS.</strong> Stream processing detects a condition and publishes a notification to humans or downstream services.</li>\n<li><strong>All four in one company.</strong> A realistic large system: EventBridge as the cross-domain event backbone with schema registry and replay; SNS for high-fan-out notifications inside a domain and all human-facing alerts; SQS in front of essentially every consumer for durability; Kinesis for the clickstream and telemetry that analytics replays nightly. Nobody is doing it wrong.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">SNS vs SQS</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The head-to-head interviewers open with. SNS is <strong>push, one-to-many</strong>; SQS is <strong>pull, one-to-one</strong>. SNS notifies everybody who is listening at the moment of publish; SQS hands out work to whichever worker asks next.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The consequence that matters most: <strong>SNS does not hold messages for you</strong>. If a subscriber's endpoint is down through the retry window, that delivery is dead-lettered or lost. SQS holds messages for up to 14 days and does not care whether any consumer is running. So if a message must not be lost, there must be a queue somewhere in the path — which is precisely why SNS-to-SQS fan-out exists. Also worth naming: SQS gives you per-consumer retry control, visibility timeouts and DLQs; SNS gives you one topic-wide publish and per-subscription filtering and DLQs.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">SNS vs EventBridge</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The most nuanced of the three, and the one where a fair answer scores well.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>SNS wins on</strong> simplicity, latency, cost per message at high volume, and breadth of delivery protocols — it is the only one that can email, text or push to a phone, and it handles very high fan-out cheaply. <strong>EventBridge wins on</strong> routing expressiveness (matching nested JSON, ranges, prefixes, anything-but rather than SNS's flatter attribute-and-body matching), the ability to consume AWS service events and SaaS partner events without writing a producer, the schema registry, archive and replay, input transformation before invoking a target, more than twenty native target types, and scheduling.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The guidance most AWS practitioners now give: <strong>new event-driven architectures often start with EventBridge</strong>, because a central bus with rich rules is easier to evolve as event types multiply and because you get AWS and SaaS events for free. <strong>SNS remains excellent</strong> — and often the better choice — for straightforward fan-out to a known set of subscribers, for very high message volumes where per-event cost matters, for the lowest latency, and for anything that must reach a human. Neither answer is wrong; the interview points come from explaining the trade-off rather than declaring a winner.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">SNS vs Kinesis Data Streams</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">These look similar — both send one message to several consumers — but they are opposites in the dimension that matters. SNS <strong>pushes and forgets</strong>: no storage, no positions, no history. Kinesis is a <strong>retained, replayable log</strong>: records stay for hours to a year, each consumer tracks its own position, and anyone can rewind.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Practical consequences. A new Kinesis consumer launched today can process last week's records; a new SNS subscriber sees only what is published from now on. Kinesis guarantees order within a shard, so all events for one user arrive in sequence; a standard SNS topic guarantees nothing about order. Kinesis costs money per shard-hour whether or not data flows, while SNS costs nothing when idle. And Kinesis is built for sustained high-volume record throughput per shard, whereas SNS is built for high fan-out of discrete notifications. Choose Kinesis when the data is a stream to be analysed, aggregated and re-processed; choose SNS when the message is an announcement to be acted on once.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">A decision flowchart</h4>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\"><i class=k>Start</i>: I need <i class=k>to</i> move data <i class=k>between</i> components.\n\nDoes <i class=k>ONE</i> consumer handle <i class=k>each</i> message (dividing up work)?\n   YES <i class=o>-</i><i class=o>&gt;</i> SQS. SNS is cheaper per message at high volume, typically lower latency, supports far higher fan-out, and is the only one of the two that can deliver to email, SMS and mobile devices. EventBridge is the better default for a new event backbone; SNS remains the better tool for large-scale fan-out and human notification.</li>\n<li><strong>\"SNS can buffer for a slow consumer.\"</strong> No. SNS retries according to its delivery policy and then dead-letters or drops. Buffering is a queue's job — that is the entire reason for SNS-to-SQS.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Kinesis is just a faster SNS.\"</strong> Different shape altogether. Kinesis retains and orders records and lets consumers replay from a position; SNS delivers once and keeps nothing. Kinesis also bills for provisioned capacity while idle.</li>\n<li><strong>\"SQS can broadcast to several consumers.\"</strong> Not within one queue — each message goes to exactly one receiver. Several consumers means several queues, which means a topic or bus in front of them.</li>\n<li><strong>\"You must choose only one.\"</strong> Real systems combine them. Picking one service for every job is the actual mistake.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Standard SNS or SQS guarantees order and exactly-once.\"</strong> Standard tiers are at-least-once with no ordering guarantee. Consumers must be idempotent. FIFO variants exist but carry throughput and endpoint-type restrictions.</li>\n<li><strong>\"EventBridge is just SNS with better filters.\"</strong> The filtering is richer, but the real differentiators are AWS and SaaS event sources, the schema registry, archive and replay, input transformers and the target catalogue.</li>\n</ul>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"I start from queue versus topic. A queue — SQS — gives each message to exactly one consumer and stores it until deleted, so it's for dividing up work and buffering. A topic — SNS — pushes every message to every subscriber and stores nothing, so it's for notification and fan-out, including to humans by email, SMS and push.
