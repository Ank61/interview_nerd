{"title":"Event-Driven Architecture with SNS — EventBridge Comparison and System Design","tags":["system-design","eventbridge","event-driven","architecture"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">This is the capstone topic, and it is where SNS stops being a service you configure and becomes a decision you defend. Senior interviews rarely ask \"how does SNS work\"; they ask \"you have an event that six teams care about — what do you build, and why not the other thing\". The strong answer requires three things: a precise understanding of where SNS sits relative to EventBridge, Kinesis and Step Functions; an opinion about event schema and ownership that survives organizational growth; and the ability to draw two or three reference architectures with their failure modes. This topic covers all three, plus the anti-patterns that show you have operated these systems rather than only read about them.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The mental model: primitive versus bus</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The cleanest framing is that <strong>SNS is a fan-out primitive and EventBridge is an event bus</strong>. SNS does one thing with exceptional efficiency: take a message and push a copy to every subscriber, fast, cheaply, at very high throughput, over an unusually broad set of delivery protocols including human-facing ones like SMS, email and mobile push. EventBridge does something categorically different: it provides a <em>routing fabric</em> with content-based rules, input transformation, a schema registry, archive and replay, SaaS partner and AWS service event sources, and cross-account buses. SNS asks \"who subscribed to this topic\"; EventBridge asks \"which rules match this event\".</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">That distinction explains almost every practical difference. SNS filtering is a per-subscription policy on message attributes (or, with payload scope, on the body) — genuinely useful, but a matching language, not a routing engine. It cannot transform the message, cannot route to a target based on a computed condition, and cannot replay. EventBridge rules can match deep into the event structure, reshape the payload per target with an input transformer, and fan out to dozens of target types including Step Functions, API Destinations with managed auth and rate limiting, and cross-account buses. What EventBridge does not have is SNS's human delivery channels or its throughput and price point.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Decision table: SNS versus EventBridge</h4>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; margin-top: 0.75rem\">\n<table style=\"width: 100%; border-collapse: collapse; font-size: 0.92rem\">\n<thead><tr style=\"text-align: left\" data-box=\"1\"><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Dimension</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Amazon SNS</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Amazon EventBridge</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">What it means for design</th></tr></thead>\n<tbody>\n<tr><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>Routing model</strong></td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Subscription-based. Choose per your org.</td></tr>\n</tbody>\n</table>\n</div>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Choose X when</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Choose SNS when</strong> the pattern is genuinely fan-out rather than routing; when volume is very high and cost per event matters; when latency must be minimal; when you need human channels (SMS, email, push); when consumers are mostly SQS queues and Lambda functions; or when you need FIFO ordering.</li>\n<li><strong>Choose EventBridge when</strong> routing decisions depend on event content in ways filter policies express awkwardly; when you need replay after a consumer bug; when events originate from AWS services or SaaS partners; when you need per-target payload transformation or an authenticated third-party HTTP call via API Destinations; when you want a central, auditable view of who consumes what; or when the bus crosses account and organizational boundaries.</li>\n<li><strong>Use both — the common real answer.</strong> Most mature architectures run <strong>EventBridge as the organizational event bus</strong>, where cross-domain events are published, governed, archived and routed, and <strong>SNS as the high-volume fan-out and notification layer</strong> underneath it. EventBridge routes a domain event to an SNS topic, which fans it out to twenty team queues cheaply; or an internal service publishes at very high volume to SNS while a small subset of business-significant events is forwarded to EventBridge for the bus. Saying \"both, and here is the boundary\" is a stronger answer than picking one, provided you can articulate the boundary — mine is <strong>EventBridge for cross-domain routing and governance, SNS for intra-domain fan-out and delivery</strong>.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Event design that survives</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Whichever you pick, the thing that determines whether the architecture is still workable in three years is the <strong>event contract</strong>. A stable envelope separates metadata every consumer needs from the domain payload, so routing and observability never depend on parsing domain data:</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>json</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\"><i class=x>{</i>\n  <i class=a>&quot;event_type&quot;</i><i class=x>:</i>     <i class=s>&quot;order.shipped&quot;</i><i class=x>,</i>        <i class=c>// the routing key; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Additive changes only within a major version.</strong> Adding an optional field is backwards compatible. Removing a field, renaming it, narrowing its type, or changing the meaning of an existing value is not — and consumers you have never met will break.</li>\n<li><strong>Never repurpose an enum value.</strong> Changing <code>order_shipped</code> to <code>OrderShipped</code> is a breaking change that silently empties every exact-match filter policy. This is the single most common production break in filtered fan-out.</li>\n<li><strong>Version by publishing both during migration.</strong> Publish v1 and v2 side by side with <code>event_version</code> as a message attribute so subscribers filter to the version they understand, migrate consumers one at a time, then retire v1 once the v1 delivery metric reaches zero. Never big-bang a schema change on a topic with unknown consumers.</li>\n<li><strong>Put routing fields in message attributes.</strong> <code>event_type</code>, <code>event_version</code>, and any high-cardinality routing dimension belong in attributes so filtering works without payload-scope policies and survives raw message delivery.</li>\n<li><strong>Events describe facts, not instructions.</strong> <code>order.shipped</code> is a fact any number of consumers may interpret; <code>send_shipping_email</code> is a command disguised as an event and couples the publisher to one consumer's behaviour.</li>\n<li><strong>Prefer thin events with a claim check for large payloads.</strong> A lean event with identifiers stays inside the 64 KB billing chunk, keeps you far from the 256 KB limit, and lets consumers fetch detail only when needed — at the cost of a read amplification and a consistency window the consumer must handle.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Governance:</strong> the publishing domain owns the topic and the schema; consumers own their subscriptions, their filter policies, their DLQs and their idempotency. Subscription should be self-service through infrastructure-as-code with required owner tags, but the publisher should be able to see the subscriber list, because a published event with unknown consumers cannot be changed safely. The uncomfortable truth to state in an interview: <strong>the moment you publish an event, its schema becomes a public API with an unbounded consumer set</strong>, and it should be reviewed with the same seriousness.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Choreography versus orchestration</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Fan-out is <strong>choreography</strong>: each consumer reacts independently and no component knows the overall process. It gives maximum decoupling, independent scaling and easy extension — adding a consumer requires no change anywhere else. Its costs are that <em>no one can see the whole process</em>, there is no place to express \"step 3 only after steps 1 and 2 succeed\", compensation on partial failure is ad hoc, and debugging means correlating logs across many services.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Orchestration</strong> with Step Functions inverts this: an explicit state machine owns the sequence, with visible execution history, built-in retries and catchers, timeouts, parallel and map states, and compensating transactions for a saga. You trade decoupling for visibility and control.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The practical rule: <strong>use choreography when consumers are genuinely independent reactions to a fact, and orchestration when there is a business process with a defined outcome</strong>. Signals that a fan-out has become a workflow and should be promoted to a state machine: consumers that must run in a specific order; a consumer that publishes an event solely to trigger the next step; compensation logic appearing in consumers (\"if payment failed, undo the reservation\"); the need to answer \"where is order A-1029 in the process\"; or timeouts that span services. A common and correct hybrid is <strong>SNS fan-out at the boundary, Step Functions inside</strong>: the domain event is broadcast to all interested parties, and the one consumer that owns a multi-step process starts a state machine execution.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; Filter policies push consumer-side discard logic into the subscription, cutting both SNS delivery and downstream compute cost.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Failure modes.</strong> A publisher contract change silently empties a filter policy — mitigate with the filtered-out alarms and versioned schemas. One subscriber going dark is invisible in aggregate metrics — mitigate with the fan-out ratio alarm and a canary. A poison message loops until the redrive policy moves it — mitigate with a low <code>maxReceiveCount</code> and DLQ alarms. Duplicates are guaranteed — mitigate with consumer idempotency keyed on <code>event_id</code>. Severity lives in a message attribute so the paging subscription filters to P1 and the ticketing subscription takes P2 and P3 — one topic, differentiated routing. The chat integration goes through Lambda rather than a bare HTTPS subscription because chat webhooks need formatting and because deduplicating alarm storms requires state. Ticketing goes via SQS because third-party APIs are rate limited and flaky, and a queue gives you retry and backpressure instead of lost tickets.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Failure modes.</strong> The paging endpoint is the only true single point of failure for P1 — mitigate with a second, independent paging path and a heartbeat that pages if no heartbeat arrives, because an alerting pipeline that fails silently is worse than none. Alarm storms fan out N times and can rate-limit your chat and ticketing integrations — mitigate with deduplication in the Lambda and aggregation windows. The entire pipeline lives in one Region, so a regional event can take out your ability to be told about it — mitigate by running the alerting path in a second Region.
