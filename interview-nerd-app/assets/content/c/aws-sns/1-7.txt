They are about the places where encryption, identity and network controls interact and produce <strong>silent</strong> failures or <strong>invisible</strong> exposure: an encrypted topic that cannot deliver to an encrypted queue, an HTTP subscriber that trusts a forged notification, a Subscribe grant that quietly becomes an egress channel, a service publisher that can be tricked into publishing to someone else's topic. This topic covers those five areas and the auditing that catches them.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">KMS key strategy</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SNS server-side encryption encrypts message bodies at rest using a data key from KMS. The first decision is <strong>AWS managed key (<code>aws/sns</code>) versus customer managed key (CMK)</strong>.</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; margin-top: 0.75rem\">\n<table style=\"width: 100%; Any multi-account event backbone therefore requires a CMK, and that is a sentence worth being able to say without hesitation.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A CMK's key policy must grant two distinct sets of principals. The <strong>publishing principals</strong> need <code>kms:GenerateDataKey*</code> and <code>kms:Decrypt</code> — the data key is generated on the publisher's behalf, which surprises people who expect only SNS to touch the key. And <strong>SNS itself</strong>, as the <code>sns.amazonaws.com</code> service principal, needs <code>kms:GenerateDataKey*</code> and <code>kms:Decrypt</code> so it can encrypt on receipt and decrypt to deliver. Scope the service grant with <code>aws:SourceAccount</code> and <code>aws:SourceArn</code> so it applies only to your topics.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The encrypted-topic-to-encrypted-queue chain</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This is the most frequently missed failure in all of SNS, and interviewers love it. When an encrypted topic delivers to an <strong>encrypted SQS queue</strong>, two different keys are involved: the topic's key, used to decrypt the message for delivery, and the <strong>queue's key</strong>, used to encrypt it on the way in. SNS must be able to use the <em>queue's</em> key. That means the queue's KMS key policy must grant <code>kms:GenerateDataKey*</code> and <code>kms:Decrypt</code> to <code>sns.amazonaws.com</code>. If it does not, everything looks correct — the topic policy is right, the queue policy is right, the subscription exists and is confirmed — and messages simply do not arrive.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>css</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">publisher ──Publish──▶ TOPIC (CMK-<i class=e>A</i>)  ──deliver──▶ QUEUE (CMK-<i class=e>B</i>)\n    │                      │                          │\nneeds on CMK-<i class=e>A</i>:      SNS needs on CMK-A:      SNS needs on CMK-B:\n GenerateDataKey*     GenerateDataKey*         GenerateDataKey*\n Decrypt              Decrypt                  Decrypt\n                                                      │\nMISSING HERE  ───────────────────────────────────────▶ delivery fails,\n(the classic bug)                                      no message, only\n                                                       NumberOfNotifications\n                                                       Failed moves</code></pre></div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The diagnostic signature: <code>NumberOfNotificationsFailed</code> rises for that subscription while <code>NumberOfMessagesPublished</code> looks healthy and nothing appears in the queue. Enable <strong>delivery status logging</strong> on the subscription to get the actual failure reason into CloudWatch Logs, and check CloudTrail for <code>kms:GenerateDataKey</code> <code>AccessDenied</code> events naming the SNS service principal — that is the fastest confirmation. Attach a <strong>DLQ</strong> so these are captured rather than lost.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Disabling a key is an instant revocation lever</strong>, and the most powerful one you have. Disable the CMK and publishing to the topic fails and delivery of encrypted content stops, immediately, across every account, without touching a single IAM policy. In a suspected compromise that is exactly what you want. But state the blast radius honestly: it is not selective. Everything using that key stops — every publisher, every subscriber, every other resource sharing the key. That is the argument for <strong>key granularity by blast radius</strong>: a key per sensitivity domain or per topic family, so that pulling the lever for the payments topic does not also stop deployment notifications. Scheduled key deletion is different and far more dangerous: it is irreversible after the waiting period and renders archived FIFO messages permanently undecryptable.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Message signature verification</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SNS signs every notification it sends to HTTP/S endpoints. The notification JSON contains <code>Message</code>, <code>MessageId</code>, <code>Timestamp</code>, <code>TopicArn</code>, <code>Type</code>, a <code>Signature</code>, a <code>SignatureVersion</code>, and a <code>SigningCertURL</code>. An HTTPS subscriber that does not verify the signature is trusting <strong>anything that can POST to its URL</strong> — and that URL is, by definition, publicly reachable. Anyone who learns it can forge notifications, including <code>SubscriptionConfirmation</code> and <code>UnsubscribeConfirmation</code> messages, which means an attacker can inject events into your system or unsubscribe you from a real topic.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Correct verification, in order:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Validate the <code>SigningCertURL</code> before fetching it.</strong> This is the classic vulnerability. The certificate URL arrives <em>in the attacker-controllable payload</em>. A naive implementation fetches whatever URL is in the message, and an attacker simply hosts their own certificate, signs a forged notification with the matching private key, and the verification passes perfectly. You must check that the URL is HTTPS and that its <strong>host matches the expected AWS pattern</strong> for the Region — an <code>sns.&lt;region&gt;.amazonaws.com</code> host under the <code>amazonaws.com</code> domain — and that the path ends in <code>.pem</code>. Match on the parsed hostname, not with a substring check: <code>sns.us-east-1.amazonaws.com.evil.example</code> contains the expected string.</li>\n<li><strong>Build the canonical string to sign</strong> from the specific fields, in the documented order, for the message <code>Type</code>. Notifications and subscription confirmations use different field sets.</li>\n<li><strong>Verify the signature</strong> against the certificate's public key, and cache the certificate so you are not fetching it per message.</li>\n<li><strong>Then</strong> check the <code>TopicArn</code> is one you actually expect, and only then act on the payload. Verifying the signature proves the message came from SNS; it does not prove it came from <em>your</em> topic.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>SignatureVersion</strong> matters. Version <strong>1</strong> uses SHA-1, which is no longer acceptable for signature purposes; version <strong>2</strong> uses SHA-256. You set the topic's <code>SignatureVersion</code> attribute to <code>2</code> so SNS signs with the stronger algorithm, and — the part people miss — <strong>your verifier must reject version 1</strong>. If the endpoint accepts either, an attacker can simply present a version 1 message and you have gained nothing from the topic setting. Treat the topic attribute and the verifier's accepted-versions list as a pair that must be changed together. In practice, use the AWS-maintained SNS message validator library for your language rather than hand-rolling the canonical string, and confirm it enforces both the host check and the version.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Related hardening for HTTPS subscribers: require HTTPS rather than HTTP so the payload is not readable in transit, handle the <code>SubscriptionConfirmation</code> flow deliberately rather than auto-confirming anything that arrives, and remember that raw message delivery strips the signed envelope — if you enable it on an HTTPS subscription you have removed your ability to verify at all, which is a real trade-off to state.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Least privilege and the subscription condition keys</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Separate the three roles cleanly: <strong>publishers</strong> need <code>sns:Publish</code> on specific topic ARNs and nothing else; <strong>subscribers</strong> need <code>sns:Subscribe</code> plus the ability to manage their own endpoint; <strong>administrators</strong> need <code>sns:CreateTopic</code>, <code>sns:SetTopicAttributes</code>, <code>sns:AddPermission</code> and <code>sns:DeleteTopic</code>, and that set should belong to a deployment pipeline role, not to humans. <code>sns:*</code> on <code>Resource: \"*\"</code> in a developer role is a finding.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The advanced control is the pair of <strong>subscription condition keys</strong>:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong><code>sns:Protocol</code></strong> restricts which protocols a subscription may use. Pinning it to <code>sqs</code> means a Subscribe grant cannot be turned into an email, SMS or HTTPS endpoint — it removes the entire class of \"subscribe my own external endpoint and exfiltrate the stream\".</li>\n<li><strong><code>sns:Endpoint</code></strong> restricts the endpoint value itself, and supports ARN and string conditions. Combined with the caller's account ID you can express \"you may subscribe an SQS queue, and only one in your own account\", which is very close to the actual intent of most cross-account grants.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A broad <strong>Deny on <code>sns:Subscribe</code></strong> with a narrow allow is the right default posture for sensitive topics, because Deny in a resource policy or SCP cannot be overridden by a permissive identity policy in another account. The general principle: <strong>the permission to subscribe is a permission to receive the whole stream forever</strong>, so treat it with the seriousness of a data-access grant rather than a routine API permission.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Confused deputy protection</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">When an AWS service publishes to your topic — S3 event notifications, CloudWatch alarms, EventBridge, Config, Lambda — the topic policy grants the <strong>service principal</strong>, not a specific customer. Without further conditions, that grant is usable by <em>any</em> customer's resource of that service type: another account's S3 bucket could be configured to publish notifications to your topic, because the request arrives as <code>s3.amazonaws.com</code> and your policy allows <code>s3.amazonaws.com</code>. That is the <strong>confused deputy</strong> problem — the service is a trusted intermediary being used as a deputy by an untrusted party.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The fix is the global condition keys <strong><code>aws:SourceAccount</code></strong> (the account that owns the calling resource) and <strong><code>aws:SourceArn</code></strong> (the specific resource ARN). Use <code>SourceArn</code> when you know the exact resource, <code>SourceAccount</code> when the resource set is dynamic within your account, and both together as belt and braces. This applies symmetrically everywhere the service principal pattern appears: the topic policy for service publishers, the queue policy for SNS delivery, the log group policy for data protection findings, and the KMS key policy for the SNS service grant.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Private access with VPC endpoints</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">By default an application in a private subnet reaches the SNS API over the internet, which means a NAT gateway, an internet-routable path, and traffic that leaves the VPC. An <strong>interface VPC endpoint (PrivateLink)</strong> for SNS — <code>com.amazonaws.&lt;region&gt;.sns</code> — places an elastic network interface with a private IP in your subnets, and private DNS makes the standard SNS endpoint resolve to it. Publishes then travel over the AWS network without traversing the internet, and you can remove the NAT dependency for this traffic.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two policy layers apply. The <strong>endpoint policy</strong> on the VPC endpoint constrains what can be done <em>through</em> it — scope it to <code>sns:Publish</code> on your specific topic ARNs rather than leaving the default full-access policy, so a compromised instance cannot use the endpoint to enumerate or administer topics. And on the topic side, the <strong><code>aws:SourceVpce</code></strong> condition key lets the topic policy require that requests arrive through a specific endpoint, with <code>aws:SourceVpc</code> as the broader VPC-level equivalent. Combining them gives you a genuinely strong statement: this topic accepts publishes only from this role, only through this endpoint, only from this VPC.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two caveats worth raising. The endpoint covers the <strong>SNS API</strong> — publishing and management. It does not make SNS's <em>outbound deliveries</em> private; delivery to an HTTPS endpoint still goes over the internet, so a fully private path means SQS or Lambda subscribers rather than HTTPS ones. And a <code>SourceVpce</code> condition is brittle in a disaster-recovery sense: recreating the endpoint produces a new ID and every topic policy referencing the old one silently starts denying. Manage both in the same IaC stack.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Auditing and the detections worth building</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">CloudTrail records SNS <strong>management events</strong> — <code>CreateTopic</code>, <code>DeleteTopic</code>, <code>SetTopicAttributes</code>, <code>AddPermission</code>, <code>RemovePermission</code>, <code>Subscribe</code>, <code>Unsubscribe</code>, <code>SetSubscriptionAttributes</code> — by default. <code>Publish</code> and <code>PublishBatch</code> are high-volume <strong>data events</strong> that must be explicitly enabled and cost money at scale; enable them selectively on sensitive topics where knowing who published matters.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The detections that actually catch incidents:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>New subscription created</strong> on a sensitive topic, especially with protocol <code>email</code>, <code>sms</code>, <code>http</code> or <code>https</code>, or with an endpoint outside your accounts. This is the exfiltration detection and it should page, not just log.</li>\n<li><strong>Topic policy widened</strong> — a <code>SetTopicAttributes</code> or <code>AddPermission</code> introducing <code>Principal: \"*\"</code>, removing a condition, or broadening an org scope. Parse the new policy rather than alarming on every call, or you will train people to ignore it.</li>\n<li><strong>Encryption removed or changed</strong> — <code>KmsMasterKeyId</code> cleared or repointed to a different key.</li>\n<li><strong>SignatureVersion downgraded</strong> from 2 to 1, which is a quiet weakening nobody would notice otherwise.</li>\n<li><strong>Delivery-failure spikes per subscription</strong>, which is the signal for the KMS chain break described above, and for a broken queue policy.</li>\n<li><strong>Actions from unexpected principals</strong> — administrative SNS calls from anything other than the pipeline role.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Pair the event-driven detections with a <strong>periodic reconciliation</strong>: list every topic's attributes and subscriptions and diff against IaC. Event detection catches the change; reconciliation catches the change you forgot to detect and the drift that predates your detections.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Security-review scenario: a public-facing topic</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A team asks you to review a topic that partners publish to and that several internal services consume. Findings and remediations, roughly in order of severity. <strong>Encryption uses <code>aws/sns</code></strong> while partners in other accounts publish — that cannot actually work for cross-account publishing, so either the partners are not really cross-account or something else is going on; move to a <strong>CMK</strong> with a key policy granting the partner roles <code>kms:GenerateDataKey*</code> and <code>kms:Decrypt</code> and the SNS service principal the same, scoped with <code>SourceArn</code>. <strong>The topic policy allows <code>sns:Subscribe</code> with <code>Principal: \"*\"</code></strong>, an unrestricted egress path: replace with enumerated consumer roles, add a Deny unless <code>sns:Protocol</code> is <code>sqs</code> and <code>sns:Endpoint</code> is an ARN in a known account, and reconcile the current subscription list — expect to find at least one forgotten email subscription. <strong>The partner grant has no <code>aws:SourceArn</code> or <code>aws:SourceAccount</code></strong> where a service principal is involved: add them. <strong>An HTTPS subscriber does not verify signatures</strong>: require verification with a validated certificate host, set the topic's <code>SignatureVersion</code> to 2 and make the endpoint reject version 1; check whether raw message delivery is on, because it strips the envelope and makes verification impossible. <strong>Internal publishers reach SNS via NAT</strong>: add an interface endpoint with a scoped endpoint policy and an <code>aws:SourceVpce</code> condition on the topic policy for internal publishing. Finally, <strong>no detections exist</strong>: add CloudTrail-driven alarms on Subscribe, policy widening, encryption changes and signature-version downgrade, plus per-subscription delivery-failure alarms and a quarterly reconciliation against IaC.
