That is backwards: the success path is trivial and the failure path is where the incidents live. A senior candidate should be able to draw the entire failure path — retry schedule, backoff shape, throttling, dead-letter queue, alarm, inspect, redrive — and then explain why, for anything that genuinely must not be lost, the correct answer is to stop tuning it and put a queue in front instead.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; The <strong>default HTTP/S delivery policy retries 3 times with a 20-second linear delay</strong> — roughly a one-minute window. A partner endpoint that is down for five minutes, or a deploy that takes ninety seconds, blows straight through it. Teams who have only ever used SQS subscriptions carry an intuition of \"SNS retries for ages\", attach an HTTP subscriber, and are surprised to lose a day's webhooks during a routine restart. Explicitly configuring the delivery policy on every HTTP/S subscription is therefore not tuning, it is a correctness requirement.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Anatomy of a Delivery Policy</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The <code>DeliveryPolicy</code> attribute (settable per subscription, or as a topic-level <code>EffectiveDeliveryPolicy</code> default) has a <code>healthyRetryPolicy</code> with these fields, which divide the retry sequence into four ordered phases:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong><code>numRetries</code></strong> — total retries, maximum 100. This is the <em>total</em>, not an additional count on top of the phase-specific fields; the backoff phase is what remains after subtracting the others.</li>\n<li><strong><code>numNoDelayRetries</code></strong> — the <strong>immediate retry phase</strong>: attempts fired back to back with no delay. Good for absorbing a single dropped connection; useless and harmful against a genuinely overloaded endpoint.</li>\n<li><strong><code>numMinDelayRetries</code></strong> — the <strong>pre-backoff phase</strong>: attempts spaced at exactly <code>minDelayTarget</code> seconds.</li>\n<li><strong><code>numMaxDelayRetries</code></strong> — the <strong>post-backoff phase</strong>: attempts spaced at exactly <code>maxDelayTarget</code> seconds, tacked on the end. This is how you stretch a policy across hours cheaply.</li>\n<li><strong><code>minDelayTarget</code> / <code>maxDelayTarget</code></strong> — the delay bounds in seconds; <code>maxDelayTarget</code> can be up to 3600.</li>\n<li><strong><code>backoffFunction</code></strong> — how the <strong>backoff phase</strong> interpolates between the two bounds: <code>linear</code>, <code>arithmetic</code>, <code>geometric</code> or <code>exponential</code>.</li>\n</ul>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code <strong>Geometric and exponential concentrate attempts early and then back off hard</strong>, which is what you want when the likely causes are either a one-off blip (caught by the early attempts) or a real outage (where hammering makes things worse and you want to wait). <code>numMaxDelayRetries</code> is the cheap way to extend reach: ten post-backoff retries at a 600-second <code>maxDelayTarget</code> adds an hour and a half of coverage for ten requests.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Throttle Policy: Protecting the Endpoint from the Fan-Out</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A delivery policy also carries a <code>throttlePolicy</code> with <code>maxReceivesPerSecond</code>, which caps how fast SNS delivers to that subscription. This matters because <strong>a topic can publish far faster than a subscriber can absorb</strong>, and SNS applies no back-pressure of its own: without a cap, a burst arrives at the endpoint at full rate, the endpoint starts timing out, every timeout becomes a retry, and the retries pile on top of the burst. That feedback loop turns a temporary spike into a sustained outage.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Setting <code>maxReceivesPerSecond</code> to something the endpoint has actually been load-tested at converts the spike into a longer, smoother delivery. The trade-off to state honestly: <strong>throttling increases dwell time</strong>. Messages wait longer inside SNS, invisible to you, and if the burst is large enough the delayed messages can still exhaust their retry window. A throttle policy smooths a spike; it does not buffer an overload. If the subscriber is persistently slower than the publish rate, no throttle value saves you and the answer is a queue.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Dead-Letter Queues on Subscriptions</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Per subscription, never per topic.</strong> The DLQ is configured through the subscription's <code>RedrivePolicy</code> attribute, pointing at an SQS queue ARN. Eight subscriptions means eight independent decisions; a topic-level DLQ does not exist. Subscriptions created outside your IaC — by a console click, by another team, by a cross-account consumer — are the ones that end up without one.</li>\n<li><strong>The queue policy matters.</strong> The DLQ needs a resource policy allowing <code>sns.amazonaws.com</code> to <code>sqs:SendMessage</code>, conditioned on <code>aws:SourceArn</code> matching the topic. Omit it and the redrive itself fails, which you see as <code>NumberOfNotificationsFailedToRedriveToDlq</code> — messages lost at the last possible moment, which is a uniquely irritating way to lose them.</li>\n<li><strong>What is in a DLQ message.</strong> The original message body and SNS envelope, plus message attributes carrying <strong>delivery failure metadata</strong>: an error code and error message, the request ID, and identification of the endpoint and subscription that failed. That metadata is the whole point — it is what turns \"something failed\" into \"this subscription got 403s from this endpoint\", and it is the only durable record you will have, since SNS itself keeps no history.</li>\n<li><strong>FIFO topics take FIFO DLQs.</strong> Match the queue type.</li>\n<li><strong>A DLQ is not a nice-to-have.</strong> Without one, exhausting retries discards the message silently: no exception, no artefact, nothing to replay. Enforce \"every subscription has a <code>RedrivePolicy</code>\" with a Config rule or an IaC lint, not with a wiki page.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The operational loop is <strong>alarm, inspect, fix, redrive</strong>. Alarm on <code>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible</code> on the DLQ being above zero — any DLQ message is an incident, not a statistic — and on <code>NumberOfNotificationsRedrivenToDlq</code> and <code>NumberOfNotificationsFailedToRedriveToDlq</code>. Inspect by receiving a few messages and reading the failure attributes; a DLQ full of 403s is a permissions change, a DLQ full of timeouts is a capacity problem, a DLQ full of 400s is a contract break and the messages may never be replayable. Fix the root cause. Only then redrive.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Redrive Strategy, and Why Blind Replay Is Dangerous</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An SNS subscription DLQ is an ordinary SQS queue, but SQS's built-in console redrive is designed to move messages back to the <em>source queue</em> that declared this queue as its DLQ. An SNS subscription DLQ has no such source queue, so in practice teams write a <strong>replay worker</strong>: receive from the DLQ, decide per message whether it is replayable, and either re-publish to the topic or deliver directly to the intended endpoint, deleting from the DLQ only after success. Build this before you need it; writing a replay tool at 3am while a partner is escalating is not when you want to be reasoning about idempotency.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Never replay blind.</strong> A DLQ holding 400 Bad Request failures will produce 400s again. Classify by the failure metadata first and replay only what a fix actually addresses.</li>\n<li><strong>Rate-limit the replay.</strong> Dumping 200,000 messages back at an endpoint that just recovered re-breaks it. Replay at a fraction of normal rate with the same throttle discipline you would apply to live traffic.</li>\n<li><strong>Watch out for stale events.</strong> A three-day-old \"cart abandoned\" email, or an order-status webhook superseded by two later transitions, can be worse than the original loss. Check for staleness and drop or collapse superseded events; replay is a business decision, not just a technical one.</li>\n<li><strong>Re-publishing to the topic replays to <em>every</em> matching subscription</strong>, not just the failed one — you will re-deliver to subscribers that already succeeded. Prefer delivering directly to the failed endpoint, or add a replay marker attribute and have other subscriptions filter it out.</li>\n<li><strong>Idempotency is the safety net.</strong> Consumers that dedupe on a business key make replay boring, which is exactly what you want.</li>\n<li><strong>Keep a second DLQ for the replayer.</strong> Messages that fail replay should not go back onto the same queue forever in a loop.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">HTTP/S Endpoint Requirements</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Respond 2xx fast.</strong> SNS treats a response slower than the documented window (15 seconds) as a failure. Accept the message, persist or enqueue it internally, return 200, and do the work asynchronously. An endpoint that does the work inline will time out under load, and every timeout becomes a retry on top of work that is still running.</li>\n<li><strong>Confirm the subscription.</strong> SNS first sends a <code>SubscriptionConfirmation</code> containing a <code>SubscribeURL</code> that must be fetched. Do not blindly fetch any URL in a POST body — validate that it is an SNS endpoint in the expected Region and account.</li>\n<li><strong>Verify the signature.</strong> Every message carries <code>Signature</code>, <code>SignatureVersion</code> and <code>SigningCertURL</code>. Validate the signature and check that the certificate URL is an AWS-owned host before fetching it, otherwise your endpoint accepts forged notifications from anyone who knows the URL.</li>\n<li><strong>Be idempotent.</strong> At-least-once plus retries plus DLQ replay means the same message will arrive more than once. Dedupe on a business key.</li>\n<li><strong>Handle the message types.</strong> <code>SubscriptionConfirmation</code>, <code>Notification</code>, <code>UnsubscribeConfirmation</code> — and know that with <code>RawMessageDelivery</code> enabled the envelope is stripped, which changes parsing.</li>\n<li><strong>Return the right status.</strong> A 2xx is success. Anything else is a retry. Returning 200 on an internal error to \"stop the noise\" converts a retryable failure into permanent silent loss.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Production Scenario: Full Failure Path for a Partner Webhook</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">You must deliver order events to a partner's HTTPS webhook. The partner is a small company: their endpoint handles maybe 50 requests per second, they deploy without warning, and they have had multi-hour outages. Your topic peaks at 2,000 events per second. Losing an order event is a contractual problem.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Do not subscribe the partner endpoint to the topic.</strong> This is the key architectural move and it should be the first thing you say. Subscribe an <strong>SQS queue</strong> that you own, and run a small delivery worker that reads from the queue and calls the partner. The queue gives you durable buffering for up to 14 days, a visibility timeout, independent retry with a maximum receive count, its own DLQ, a backlog metric you can graph during the outage, and the ability to pause delivery entirely by stopping the worker. A delivery policy can give you none of those things.</li>\n<li><strong>Filter at the topic.</strong> The partner receives only the event types in the contract, using an allow-list plus <code>{\"anything-but\": {\"prefix\": \"internal.\"}}</code> as a guard, so 2,000 events per second becomes the much smaller slice they care about and no internal event can leak.</li>\n<li><strong>Rate-limit in the worker</strong> to below the partner's tested capacity, with a circuit breaker: after N consecutive failures, stop calling, let the queue accumulate, and retry the breaker periodically. This prevents your retries from keeping their endpoint down.</li>\n<li><strong>Retry with backoff and jitter in the worker</strong>, and use the SQS redrive policy with a modest <code>maxReceiveCount</code> so a genuinely poisonous message reaches the DLQ rather than blocking the backlog.</li>\n<li><strong>Two DLQs.</strong> One on the SNS subscription (catches SNS-to-SQS delivery failures — rare, usually a queue policy change) and one on the SQS queue (catches partner-delivery failures — the common case). Alarm on both, on message age, and on queue depth.</li>\n<li><strong>If you had to subscribe the endpoint directly</strong> — say the partner refuses a queue-based relay — then engineer the delivery policy properly: a couple of immediate retries, <code>minDelayTarget</code> around 20 seconds, <code>maxDelayTarget</code> at 3600, a <code>geometric</code> or <code>exponential</code> backoff function, and a healthy <code>numMaxDelayRetries</code> so the tail stretches over hours; plus a <code>throttlePolicy</code> with <code>maxReceivesPerSecond</code> near 40 to stay under their capacity; plus a DLQ. State plainly that even a maxed-out policy tops out at 100 retries and cannot match a queue's 14-day retention.</li>\n<li><strong>Observability.</strong> Delivery status logging on the subscription, an alarm on any DLQ depth above zero, an alarm on the queue's oldest-message age, and a dashboard the on-call can read during the partner's next outage.</li>\n<li><strong>Replay tooling ready in advance</strong>, rate-limited, staleness-aware, and delivering directly to the partner rather than re-publishing to the topic so other subscribers are not re-notified.</li>\n</ul>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"SNS retries AWS-managed endpoints like SQS and Lambda aggressively — around a hundred thousand attempts over up to 23 days — but HTTP/S endpoints only follow the delivery policy you set, and the default is just three retries about twenty seconds apart.
