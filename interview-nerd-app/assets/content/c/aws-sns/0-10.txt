Published minus delivered minus filtered-out is roughly what went wrong.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The alarm set every production topic should have</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An <strong>alarm</strong> is a CloudWatch rule that watches a metric and notifies you when it crosses a threshold. Three alarms cover the overwhelming majority of real SNS incidents.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Failed notifications above zero.</strong> Alarm on <code>NumberOfNotificationsFailed</code> with a Sum statistic, threshold greater than 0, over one or two consecutive minutes. Treat any failure as worth a look — this metric should genuinely sit at zero. Use <code>treatMissingData: notBreaching</code> so quiet periods do not alarm.</li>\n<li><strong>Dead-letter queue depth above zero.</strong> A <strong>dead-letter queue (DLQ)</strong> is an SQS queue attached to a <em>subscription</em> where SNS parks messages it could not deliver after retries, so they are kept instead of lost. Alarm on that queue's <code>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible</code> greater than 0. Without this alarm the DLQ becomes a place where messages go to be forgotten, which is worse than losing them because you believe you are safe.</li>\n<li><strong>SMS spend approaching the limit.</strong> Alarm on <code>SMSMonthToDateSpentUSD</code> at something like 80 percent of your account's monthly limit. SNS stops sending at the limit without fanfare, so the first symptom is otherwise \"customers stopped getting one-time passwords.\"</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two useful extras: alarm on <code>NumberOfMessagesPublished</code> being unexpectedly low if a topic is supposed to have steady traffic (silence is a failure mode too), and on <code>SMSSuccessRate</code> dropping below a normal baseline.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Delivery status logging: the first thing to turn on during an incident</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Metrics tell you <em>how many</em> failed. <strong>Delivery status logging</strong> tells you <em>which one, to whom, and why</em>. When you enable it, SNS writes one structured JSON record per delivery attempt into CloudWatch Logs, containing the message id, the destination ARN or endpoint, the subscriber's HTTP status code, the <strong>dwell time</strong> (how long the message sat inside SNS before the attempt, in milliseconds), and for failures a provider response explaining the cause.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The essential facts about it:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>It is off by default.</strong> Nobody gets these logs for free. If you have never configured it, the logs for last night's incident do not exist and cannot be recovered.</li>\n<li><strong>You configure it per topic, per protocol.</strong> Each delivery protocol has its own pair of topic attributes — for example <code>SQSSuccessFeedbackRoleArn</code> and <code>SQSFailureFeedbackRoleArn</code>, and equivalents for <code>HTTP</code>, <code>Lambda</code>, <code>Firehose</code> and the mobile platforms. Turning it on for SQS does nothing for your HTTP subscriber.</li>\n<li><strong>It needs an IAM role.</strong> You pass an IAM role ARN that SNS assumes to write into CloudWatch Logs (it needs permissions such as <code>logs:CreateLogGroup</code>, <code>logs:CreateLogStream</code> and <code>logs:PutLogEvents</code>). Without a valid role, nothing is written and nothing obviously complains.</li>\n<li><strong>Success has a sample rate; failure does not.</strong> The <code>...SuccessFeedbackSampleRate</code> attribute is a percentage from 0 to 100 controlling how many <em>successful</em> deliveries are logged, because at volume logging every success is expensive. Failures are always logged once you set the failure role. A sensible production setting is a low success sample rate, such as 5 or 10, with failures at full detail — and turn success up to 100 temporarily while debugging.</li>\n<li><strong>It costs money.</strong> The charges are CloudWatch Logs ingestion and storage, not SNS. That is the reason for the sample rate, and a reason to set a log group retention period instead of keeping records forever.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This is the correct first move when a subscriber claims it is not receiving messages: turn on failure logging plus a 100 percent success sample rate for that protocol, republish, and read the actual record. It converts \"it doesn't work\" into \"the endpoint returned 403 after 2 retries.\"</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Reading the logs with CloudWatch Logs Insights</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>CloudWatch Logs Insights</strong> is a query tool that runs over log groups, and because delivery records are JSON, its fields are directly queryable. Three queries earn their keep: count failures grouped by status code to see whether you have one broken endpoint or many; filter by a specific <code>notification.messageId</code> to trace one message end to end; and average <code>delivery.dwellTimeMs</code> over time to spot a subscriber that is slowing down before it starts failing. Insights charges by data scanned, so always narrow the time range first.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">CloudTrail: who changed the topic</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>CloudTrail</strong> is the AWS service that records API calls made in your account — who called what, from where, and when.
