{"title":"Message Archiving and Replay for FIFO Topics","tags":["sns","fifo","archive-replay","recovery"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">For most of its life, the single most important architectural fact about Amazon SNS was that <strong>it does not store messages</strong>. A publish is a fan-out attempt; if a subscription's endpoint is broken and its retries exhaust, the message is gone unless you attached a dead-letter queue or put a durable queue in the path. Every SNS design principle — put an SQS queue in front of every consumer, never subscribe a Lambda directly if you care about the event, attach a DLQ to every subscription — descends from that one fact. <strong>Message archiving and replay for FIFO topics</strong> changes the statement, but only for FIFO topics, and knowing precisely where the boundary sits is the whole point of this topic in an interview.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">What archiving actually is</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">On a <strong>FIFO topic</strong> you can set an <strong>archive policy</strong>, a topic attribute whose content is a <code>MessageRetentionPeriod</code> expressed in days. From the moment it is enabled, every message published to that topic is retained in the topic itself for that window, in order, per message group. You are not subscribing an archiver; the topic <em>is</em> the archive. Retention can be set from one day up to a year, and you can raise or lower it later — raising it does not retroactively archive messages published before the policy existed, and lowering it makes older messages immediately ineligible for replay.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two boundaries must be stated crisply, because interviewers listen for them:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>This is a FIFO-topic capability, not a general SNS one.</strong> Standard topics do not offer archive and replay. If you need history on a standard topic, the classic answers still apply and you should say so: subscribe a <strong>Firehose delivery stream</strong> that lands every message in S3 and replay from there with your own tooling, or put an <strong>SQS queue in the path</strong> with a long retention period so the messages simply wait for the consumer to come back.</li>\n<li><strong>Archiving is not a queue.</strong> A subscriber does not read the archive; it receives deliveries. The archive exists so that <em>you</em> can ask SNS to re-deliver a time range to a subscription. There is no random access, no seek-by-offset, no consumer-managed cursor. If you want Kafka semantics, this is not Kafka.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Because the capability lives on FIFO topics, everything else about SNS FIFO comes along: strict ordering within a <code>MessageGroupId</code>, deduplication within a five-minute window by <code>MessageDeduplicationId</code> or content hash, and the throughput characteristics of FIFO rather than standard. If your workload cannot live with FIFO's constraints, archive and replay is not available to you and you are back to Firehose or a queue.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Replay in depth</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>replay</strong> is started on a <strong>subscription</strong>, not on a topic, by setting a <strong>replay policy</strong> on that subscription with a <code>StartingPoint</code> timestamp and optionally an <code>EndingPoint</code>. SNS then redelivers the archived messages in that window to that one subscription. The other subscriptions on the topic are unaffected and never see the replayed traffic — which is precisely what makes this usable in production, because you can repair one broken consumer without re-notifying eleven healthy ones.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">                      FIFO topic (ArchivePolicy: <i class=n>14</i> days)\npublish ──▶ [ msg archive, ordered <i class=k>per</i> MessageGroupId ]\n                │                │                │\n     live ──────┼────────────────┼────────────────┼──── live\n                ▼                ▼                ▼\n         sub A (healthy)   sub B (REPLAYING)   sub C (healthy)\n                           ┌──────────────────────────┐\n                           │ archived <i class=k>window</i> <i class=n>08</i>:<i class=n>00</i><i class=n>-14</i>:<i class=n>00</i>\n                           │ delivered <i class=k>in</i> <i class=k>group</i> <i class=k>order</i>,\n                           │ <i class=k>then</i> catches up <i class=k>to</i> live  │\n                           └──────────────────────────┘\n         unaffected        idempotency REQUIRED       unaffected</code></pre></div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Ordering and live traffic.</strong> The topic keeps accepting publishes while a replay runs. SNS preserves ordering within each message group: for a given <code>MessageGroupId</code> the subscription receives the archived messages in their original order and then transitions to the live stream, so the consumer sees a coherent sequence rather than an interleaving of old and new for that group. The consequence is that a replaying subscription is <strong>behind</strong> — it is working through history while live events accumulate — and it catches up only when replay throughput exceeds the live publish rate. If you replay six hours of a topic that publishes faster than the subscriber can drain, the subscriber never catches up; that is a capacity problem you must think about before starting, not after.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Replay states.</strong> The subscription exposes a replay status you can poll with <code>GetSubscriptionAttributes</code>, moving through a lifecycle roughly of <em>pending</em> while SNS sets the replay up, <em>in progress</em> while archived messages are being delivered, and then <em>completed</em> when the window has been delivered and the subscription is back on pure live traffic, or <em>failed</em> if the replay could not proceed. Operationally you want to watch this, because \"I started a replay\" and \"the replay finished\" are different facts, and a runbook that does not check for completion tends to produce an engineer who declares recovery done while half the window is still outstanding.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">What this genuinely unlocks</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Recovering a broken subscriber.</strong> The canonical case. A consumer's endpoint was misconfigured, its role lost a permission, or its queue policy was tightened at 08:00 and nobody noticed until 14:00. Deliveries failed, retries exhausted, and without an archive those six hours were simply lost — you would be reconstructing state from database snapshots and upstream logs. With an archive you fix the cause and replay 08:00 to 14:00 to that subscription alone.</li>\n<li><strong>Onboarding a new subscriber that needs recent history.</strong> A new service joins the platform and needs the last few days of events to build its local projection. Previously this meant a bespoke backfill: someone writes a script to read the S3 archive, transform it into the event shape, and inject it into the new consumer's queue — a week of work, usually with subtle differences from the real event path. Now you subscribe it and replay the window through the exact same delivery path as live traffic, which also serves as a genuine integration test.</li>\n<li><strong>Reprocessing after a consumer-side bug.</strong> A consumer processed events for two days with a bug that computed the wrong value. Fix the code, deploy, replay the window, and let idempotent handlers recompute. This is only safe if the handler is genuinely idempotent — see below.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The common thread is that all three previously required an <strong>external archive plus custom re-injection tooling</strong>, and that tooling is always subtly wrong: it replays through a different path than production, it loses ordering, it does not respect filter policies, and it rots because nobody exercises it between incidents.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Constraints, costs and gotchas</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>The retention window is a hard floor on your recovery.</strong> You cannot replay what was not archived. Choose retention from your realistic worst-case detection time, not your optimistic one — if a silent subscriber failure typically takes a day to notice and a day to get approval to replay, a two-day retention is already too short. Retention is also the knob you cannot change retroactively.</li>\n<li><strong>Cost has two components.</strong> Archived message <strong>storage</strong> is billed for what you retain, and <strong>replayed deliveries are billed like deliveries</strong> — a replay of six hours of traffic costs roughly what publishing those six hours cost in delivery terms. Long retention on a high-volume topic is not free, and \"just set it to 365 days on everything\" is the answer of someone who has not looked at a bill. Size retention per topic against the value of recovery.</li>\n<li><strong>Replay re-delivers, so consumers must be idempotent.</strong> This is the single most important design consequence. A replay deliberately sends messages the consumer may have already successfully processed — you usually cannot replay <em>exactly</em> the failed subset, because your window boundaries are timestamps and the failures were not neatly aligned to them. If the handler charges a card, sends an email, or increments a counter, a replay is an outage of its own making. Idempotency must be keyed on something stable in the message — a business event ID, or the SNS message ID — and enforced with a conditional write or a dedup table. Note that <strong>FIFO deduplication does not save you</strong>: the dedup window is five minutes and applies to publishes, not to replays of hours-old messages.</li>\n<li><strong>Filter policies apply during replay.</strong> Replayed messages pass through the subscription's <strong>current</strong> filter policy, not whatever policy was in effect when the message was originally published. That cuts both ways and both are worth saying out loud. It is useful: you can widen a filter and replay to pick up events the subscription previously discarded, which is a clean way to fix \"we only subscribed to two event types and now need a third, retroactively\". It is also a trap: if someone narrowed the filter policy after the incident window, your replay will silently deliver fewer messages than you expect, and you will conclude the archive was incomplete when in fact the filter dropped them. Check the filter policy before you interpret replay counts.</li>\n<li><strong>Replay is per-subscription and one at a time.</strong> Plan a multi-subscriber recovery as a sequence, and expect each to be a separate operation with its own status to watch.</li>\n<li><strong>Enabling archiving is not retroactive.</strong> Turning it on today gives you nothing about yesterday. This is worth enabling pre-emptively on important FIFO topics precisely because you cannot enable it after the incident.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Comparing the archiving options</h4>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto;
