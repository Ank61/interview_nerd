{"title":"SNS to SQS Fan-Out at Scale","tags":["sns","sqs","fan-out","raw-message-delivery"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">SNS-to-SQS fan-out is the flagship AWS event-distribution pattern, and every candidate can draw it. What distinguishes a senior answer is knowing what the pattern actually costs to operate: the envelope semantics that break consumers on a config flip, the queue policy that makes a subscription silently inert, the KMS permissions that fail without an error anyone can see, the duplicate semantics that compound across two at-least-once hops, and the monitoring that proves a subscriber is not quietly missing traffic. This topic is about running the pattern, not drawing it.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The envelope and raw message delivery</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">By default, a message SNS delivers to an SQS queue is not your message — it is a JSON <strong>envelope</strong> whose <code>Message</code> field contains your payload as a string. The envelope carries <code>Type</code> (usually <code>Notification</code>), <code>MessageId</code>, <code>TopicArn</code>, <code>Subject</code>, <code>Message</code>, <code>Timestamp</code>, <code>SignatureVersion</code>, <code>Signature</code>, <code>SigningCertURL</code>, <code>UnsubscribeURL</code>, and, when present, a <code>MessageAttributes</code> map. A consumer therefore performs a <strong>double parse</strong>: parse the SQS body into the envelope, then parse <code>envelope.Message</code> into the domain object. Message attributes are inside the envelope, so SQS-level attribute filtering, message-attribute-based routing, and anything else that reads SQS message attributes sees nothing.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Turning on <strong>RawMessageDelivery</strong> on the subscription changes this. The SQS message body becomes exactly the string you published — byte for byte — and the SNS message attributes are promoted to <strong>SQS message attributes</strong>. The benefits are real: one parse instead of two, smaller messages (the envelope costs a few hundred bytes of the 256 KB limit on every single message), attributes visible to SQS-native tooling, and a payload that is identical whether the producer published through SNS or wrote directly to the queue — which makes migration and local testing far easier. The cost is that you lose the envelope metadata: no <code>TopicArn</code>, no SNS <code>MessageId</code>, no <code>Timestamp</code>, no signature. If a consumer needs to know which topic a message came from because it is subscribed to several, that provenance must move into a message attribute or into the payload itself.</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; If consumers still expect the envelope, every message after the flip fails to parse — and because those failures happen in the consumer, they manifest as a DLQ filling up or a crash loop, not as any SNS-side signal. Worse, messages published just before the flip are still in flight in the old format, so for a window the queue contains <strong>both shapes</strong>. The safe sequence is: first deploy consumers that detect the shape and handle both (check whether the parsed body has a <code>Type</code> of <code>Notification</code> and a <code>Message</code> field, and unwrap only then); confirm the tolerant consumer is fully rolled out; flip the attribute; drain the mixed window; then, much later, remove the compatibility branch. Doing this per-subscription means you can migrate one consumer at a time, which is one of the quiet virtues of fan-out.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The queue policy — the number one silent failure</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SNS delivers to SQS by calling <code>sqs:SendMessage</code> as the service principal <code>sns.amazonaws.com</code>. SQS queues are resource-policy-governed, and a queue's default policy allows only its owner. Therefore <strong>a subscription without a corresponding queue policy statement is inert</strong>: the subscription exists, the console shows it as confirmed, filter policies look right, and nothing arrives. The statement must allow <code>sqs:SendMessage</code> on the queue ARN to principal <code>sns.amazonaws.com</code>, and it must be scoped with a condition on <code>aws:SourceArn</code> equal to the topic ARN. Without that condition, <em>any</em> SNS topic in <em>any</em> AWS account can inject messages into your queue — a genuine confused-deputy exposure that shows up in security reviews. For cross-account setups, also consider <code>aws:SourceAccount</code>.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Note the asymmetry that makes this hard to detect: when SNS is denied by the queue policy, the delivery does fail and <code>NumberOfNotificationsFailed</code> on the topic increments — but that metric is <strong>aggregated at the topic level</strong>, so in a fan-out to eight queues where one is misconfigured, the metric is a small non-zero number against a large delivered count and nobody notices. This is exactly why per-subscription delivery status logging and the publish-versus-sent reconciliation described below exist.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Per-subscriber isolation: the actual architectural payoff</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The reason to put a queue between SNS and each consumer — rather than subscribing consumers directly — is <strong>fault and lifecycle isolation</strong>. Each queue is an independent buffer with its own properties.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Independent retry.</strong> A slow consumer sets a long visibility timeout; a fast one sets a short one. One consumer's retry storm does not touch another's.</li>\n<li><strong>Independent DLQ.</strong> Each queue has its own redrive policy and <code>maxReceiveCount</code>, so poison messages are quarantined per consumer and can be redriven back to just that queue after a fix.</li>\n<li><strong>Independent scaling.</strong> The analytics consumer can run at concurrency 5 while the payments consumer runs at 200, sized to their own downstreams.</li>\n<li><strong>Independent filter policy.</strong> The search indexer subscribes only to <code>order.created</code> and <code>order.updated</code>; the fraud service takes everything. Filtering happens at SNS, so unwanted messages are never charged to or stored in the uninterested queue.</li>\n<li><strong>Independent availability.</strong> A consumer that is down for four hours simply accumulates a backlog; the other seven are unaffected, and no publisher is aware.</li>\n<li><strong>Add a consumer without touching the publisher.</strong> This is the deployment-coupling win: a new team creates a queue, subscribes it, and starts consuming. The publishing service is not redeployed, not code-reviewed, and not even notified.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Duplicates and ordering across the chain</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A standard topic feeding a standard queue is <strong>at-least-once twice over</strong>. SNS may deliver the same notification to a queue more than once; SQS standard may then deliver the same message to a consumer more than once; and a consumer whose visibility timeout expires mid-processing will see the message again. Ordering is best-effort at both hops, so a create and an update published milliseconds apart can arrive reversed. Treating duplicates as an exception to be logged is a design error at scale — they are a normal, expected occurrence.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The correct defence is <strong>idempotency keyed on a business identifier</strong>, not on the SNS <code>MessageId</code>. The MessageId is generated per publish call: a publisher that retries after a timeout produces a <em>new</em> MessageId for the <em>same</em> business event, so MessageId-based dedup misses exactly the duplicates most likely to occur. Key instead on something like <code>orderId + version</code> or <code>orderId + eventType + sequenceNumber</code>, recorded with a conditional write in DynamoDB with a TTL. Carrying a monotonically increasing version in the payload also solves out-of-order delivery: the consumer applies an update only when its version exceeds the stored version, making reordering harmless.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">When the business genuinely requires ordering, use a <strong>FIFO topic subscribed to FIFO queues</strong>. FIFO topics accept only FIFO queue subscriptions, require a <code>MessageGroupId</code> on every publish, and preserve strict ordering <em>within a group</em> while giving exactly-once delivery through <code>MessageDeduplicationId</code> or content-based deduplication over a five-minute window. FIFO subscriptions support message filtering, and raw message delivery is required for FIFO-to-FIFO delivery. The trade-offs are real: throughput is bounded per message group, the fan-out is limited to FIFO queues only (no Lambda, HTTP, email or SMS endpoints), and choosing the group key is the whole design — too coarse and you serialize unrelated work, too fine and you lose the ordering you wanted. Pick the smallest key that preserves the invariant, typically the aggregate ID such as the order or account ID.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Encryption across the hop</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Server-side encryption is applied independently at each resource, and mismatched key policies are a classic silent failure. If the <strong>topic</strong> uses a customer managed KMS key, the <em>publisher's</em> principal needs <code>kms:GenerateDataKey</code> and <code>kms:Decrypt</code> on that key — otherwise <code>Publish</code> fails outright, which at least is loud. If the <strong>queue</strong> uses a customer managed key, then <strong>SNS</strong> must be able to use it: the key policy must allow <code>kms:GenerateDataKey</code> and <code>kms:Decrypt</code> to principal <code>sns.amazonaws.com</code>, ideally scoped by <code>aws:SourceArn</code> or <code>aws:SourceAccount</code>. Miss that and SNS cannot write to the encrypted queue; the delivery fails, it counts in the topic-level <code>NumberOfNotificationsFailed</code>, and the queue simply stays empty. Note that the AWS managed key <code>aws/sqs</code> cannot have its key policy modified, so it <strong>cannot</strong> be used for a queue receiving cross-account SNS deliveries — this is a well-known trap. Consumers likewise need <code>kms:Decrypt</code> on the queue's key, and the DLQ needs the same treatment, which teams routinely forget until the DLQ is needed.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cross-account fan-out</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Fan-out across accounts is common in a domain-owned microservice estate: the orders team owns the topic in their account, and consuming teams own queues in theirs. The mechanics are two resource policies facing each other — a topic policy allowing <code>sns:Subscribe</code> (and often <code>sns:Receive</code>) to the consumer account's principals, and a queue policy in the consumer account allowing <code>sns.amazonaws.com</code> to <code>SendMessage</code> scoped to the producer's topic ARN. When the queue owner is a different account from the subscriber creating the subscription, the subscription enters <strong>PendingConfirmation</strong> and must be confirmed, which surprises people who expect SQS subscriptions to always be instant.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The operational points that matter more than the mechanics: <strong>cost attribution</strong> is naturally split — the producer pays for publishes and for the notification deliveries, the consumer pays for SQS requests and storage and for the KMS calls on their own key — which is usually the fairness property you want. <strong>Ownership</strong> must be explicit: the topic owner effectively owns the schema contract and any change breaks consumers they may not know about, so a schema registry or a documented compatibility policy is not optional. And the topic owner can see <em>that</em> a subscription exists but has no visibility into whether the consumer is healthy, so the reconciliation responsibility sits with the consumer.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Scaling, backpressure and monitoring the seam</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The queue absorbs the fan-out burst, which is precisely its value — but absorbing a burst is not the same as protecting the downstream. If the consumer scales freely against the backlog, it will happily drive a 500-connection database into collapse while the queue drains beautifully. Bound consumer concurrency explicitly (reserved concurrency or <code>MaximumConcurrency</code> on the event source mapping for Lambda, or a fixed worker pool for containers) and size it to the downstream's real capacity. Then the queue does its job: depth grows, latency grows, nothing breaks.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage</strong> is the single best health signal for a consumer. Depth alone is ambiguous — a large queue draining fast is fine — but rising age means the consumer is genuinely losing ground against the arrival rate. Alarm on age, not just depth.</li>\n<li><strong>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible</strong> tells you the size of the backlog and is the right input for scaling policies.</li>\n<li><strong>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesNotVisible</strong> rising without the visible count falling suggests messages are being received and then failing before deletion, cycling toward the DLQ.</li>\n<li><strong>DLQ depth and DLQ age</strong> must both be alarmed. A DLQ nobody alarms on is a data-loss mechanism with extra steps.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Monitoring the seam</strong> is the discipline that catches silent subscriber loss. Compare the topic's <code>NumberOfMessagesPublished</code> against each queue's <code>NumberOfMessagesSent</code> over the same window. For a subscriber with no filter policy the counts should track one-to-one; for one with a filter policy the ratio should be stable and known. A CloudWatch math expression per subscriber alarming when the ratio deviates is a cheap, high-value control: it detects a queue policy broken by a template change, a filter policy that accidentally matches nothing after a schema rename, and a KMS permission that lapsed on key rotation. Complement it with per-subscription <strong>delivery status logging</strong> (set <code>SQSSuccessFeedbackSampleRate</code> and the failure role) so failures carry a reason rather than being an anonymous count.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">When EventBridge is the better answer</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SNS and EventBridge overlap on fan-out, and picking the wrong one is a common design-review finding. Choose <strong>SNS</strong> for high-throughput, low-latency, high-fan-out distribution of a single logical event type to a known set of subscribers, when you need mobile push, SMS or email endpoints, when you need FIFO ordering, or when per-message cost at very high volume dominates. Choose <strong>EventBridge</strong> when you need <strong>content-based routing over the full event body</strong> with rich pattern matching (prefix, numeric ranges, <code>anything-but</code>, nested fields) rather than SNS's attribute-oriented filtering; when you want an <strong>event bus with many heterogeneous event types</strong> and rules that select across them; when you need <strong>archive and replay</strong> of past events; when you want <strong>schema discovery and a registry</strong>; when you need <strong>input transformation</strong> before delivery; when you want to consume <strong>AWS service events or SaaS partner events</strong>; or when you want a much wider set of native targets (Step Functions, ECS tasks, Kinesis, API destinations) without writing glue. EventBridge has higher per-event latency and lower throughput ceilings than SNS and costs more per million events, so for a pure high-volume fan-out to a handful of queues, SNS remains the right tool. A perfectly respectable hybrid is EventBridge as the routing layer with SNS topics as high-fan-out targets.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Production scenario: one queue in the fan-out receives nothing</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An <code>order-events</code> topic fans out to six queues. The new <code>loyalty-points</code> queue, added last week, has processed zero messages since deployment. The other five are healthy. Work the path in order, cheapest check first.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>1. Does the subscription exist and is it confirmed?</strong> <code>list-subscriptions-by-topic</code> — a <code>PendingConfirmation</code> state on a cross-account queue subscription is an immediate answer. <strong>2. Is there a filter policy?</strong> <code>get-subscription-attributes</code> and read <code>FilterPolicy</code> together with <code>FilterPolicyScope</code>. This is the most common cause by a wide margin: a policy scoped to <code>MessageAttributes</code> when the publisher puts the routing field in the body, a value case-mismatch (<code>Order_Created</code> versus <code>order_created</code>), or a field the publisher renamed. A filter that matches nothing is <strong>not an error</strong> — SNS reports it as filtered, so nothing shows as failed anywhere. <strong>3. Queue policy.</strong> <code>get-queue-attributes</code> for the <code>Policy</code> and confirm a statement allowing <code>sns.amazonaws.com</code> to <code>SendMessage</code> with <code>aws:SourceArn</code> matching this topic; a copy-pasted policy naming a different topic is very common. <strong>4. KMS.</strong> If the queue uses a customer managed key, confirm the key policy grants <code>kms:GenerateDataKey</code> and <code>kms:Decrypt</code> to <code>sns.amazonaws.com</code>, and that the queue is not using the unmodifiable <code>aws/sqs</code> key in a cross-account setup. <strong>5. The metrics discriminator.</strong> If <code>NumberOfNotificationsFailed</code> on the topic is non-zero and correlates in time, the problem is delivery (queue policy or KMS). If it is zero, SNS believes it has nothing to deliver, which points squarely at the filter policy. <strong>6. Consumer.</strong> Finally check <code>NumberOfMessagesSent</code> on the queue itself — if it is non-zero, messages <em>are</em> arriving and the bug is in the consumer, not the fan-out.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The durable fix after resolution is not just correcting the misconfiguration but adding the control that would have caught it: a per-subscriber CloudWatch math alarm on the ratio of queue <code>NumberOfMessagesSent</code> to topic <code>NumberOfMessagesPublished</code>, plus subscription delivery status logging, plus an integration test in the deployment pipeline that publishes a synthetic event and asserts it lands in every subscribed queue.</p>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"Fan-out gives each consumer its own queue, so retry, DLQ, scaling and filtering are all independent and you add a subscriber without touching the publisher.
