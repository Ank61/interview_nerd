But SNS also has two channels that reach a human being directly on their phone — a <strong>mobile push notification</strong> (the banner that slides onto a phone's lock screen) and an <strong>SMS</strong> (a plain text message). These two are worth learning separately because they are the only SNS channels where a third party outside AWS — Apple, Google, or a mobile phone carrier — sits between your publish call and the person, and that third party has its own rules, its own failures, and its own regulators.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Mobile push, explained from the ground up</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Your app cannot just open a socket to a phone and shout. Phones spend most of their life asleep with the screen off, so each phone platform runs one always-on connection from the device to the platform's own servers, and everybody's notifications travel down that single pipe. Apple's is called <strong>APNs</strong> (Apple Push Notification service). Google's is <strong>FCM</strong> (Firebase Cloud Messaging), used by Android. There are others: <strong>ADM</strong> (Amazon Device Messaging) for Fire devices, and <strong>Baidu</strong> for Android phones in China that ship without Google services.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">When your app starts for the first time on a phone, it asks the operating system for permission to send notifications. If the user agrees, the OS talks to APNs or FCM and hands your app back a long random string called a <strong>device token</strong> (FCM calls it a registration token). A device token is simply an address: it identifies <em>this installation of this app on this one physical phone</em>. It is not a user id and not a phone number. Your app sends that token up to your backend, and your backend stores it.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SNS then gives you two objects that wrap all of this. A <strong>platform application</strong> is an SNS resource that holds the credentials for one push service — for example your Apple signing key, or your FCM server credentials — for one of your apps. Think of it as \"my iOS app's mailbox key at Apple.\" A <strong>platform endpoint</strong> is an SNS resource that represents one device token registered under that platform application. In plain words: the platform application is the door to Apple, and each platform endpoint is one phone behind that door. Each endpoint gets its own ARN (Amazon Resource Name, the unique AWS identifier for a resource), and you publish to that ARN exactly the way you would publish to a topic.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">Phone<i class=o>/</i>app                 Your backend            SNS                     Apple <i class=o>/</i> Google      Phone\n   <i class=o>|</i>  ask OS <i class=k>for</i> permission     <i class=o>|</i>                   <i class=o>|</i>                          <i class=o>|</i>                <i class=o>|</i>\n   <i class=o>|</i><i class=c>--&gt; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">One device or many: direct publish versus topic broadcast</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Once endpoints exist you have two ways to reach people, and choosing correctly is a common interview question.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Direct publish to one endpoint ARN.</strong> Use this for anything personal: \"your driver is arriving,\" \"Sam replied to your comment,\" \"your payment failed.\" One call, one phone. You look up the endpoint ARN for that user in your own database.</li>\n<li><strong>Publish to a topic that endpoints subscribe to.</strong> A topic is a named broadcast channel; when you subscribe a platform endpoint to a topic, every publish to that topic goes to every subscribed phone. Use this for genuine broadcasts: \"the match kicks off in 10 minutes,\" \"the store is now open.\" One call, a million phones, and SNS does the fan-out for you.</li>\n<li><strong>Segmenting with topics.</strong> A common design is one topic per interest group — <code>sports-alerts</code>, <code>promo-us</code>, <code>promo-uk</code> — and you subscribe each device's endpoint to the topics that user opted into. That keeps the publish side simple. Note that a topic has a subscription limit (in the millions, generous but not infinite), and that for genuinely large campaign segmentation the messaging-focused services described later are a better fit.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Token lifecycle: the thing that surprises everyone</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This is the number one operational surprise with SNS mobile push, and it is worth learning before you hit it in production. <strong>Device tokens are not permanent.</strong> They change when the app is reinstalled, when the user restores a phone from a backup onto new hardware, sometimes when the OS updates, and they simply expire in some conditions. Meanwhile, if the user deletes your app, Apple or Google will eventually tell SNS that the token is dead.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">When SNS learns a token is bad, it sets that platform endpoint's <code>Enabled</code> attribute to <code>false</code> — the endpoint is now <strong>disabled</strong>, meaning SNS will accept your publish call but silently not deliver. That silence is the trap: your code sees a successful API response and assumes the user got the notification.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Re-register on every app launch.</strong> Have the app fetch its current token each time it starts and send it to your backend. If the token differs from the stored one, update the endpoint (<code>SetEndpointAttributes</code> with the new <code>Token</code> and <code>Enabled</code> set to <code>true</code>) rather than creating a second endpoint.</li>\n<li><strong>Handle the duplicate-token case.</strong> If you call <code>CreatePlatformEndpoint</code> with a token that already exists under different attributes, the API returns an error whose message contains the existing endpoint ARN. The standard recipe is: catch it, parse out the ARN, then update that endpoint's token and re-enable it. This is genuinely how AWS documents the flow.</li>\n<li><strong>Clean up disabled endpoints.</strong> Periodically list your endpoints, find the ones with <code>Enabled</code> false, and delete them along with the stale row in your user table. Otherwise you slowly accumulate dead endpoints and your \"notifications sent\" number quietly stops matching reality.</li>\n<li><strong>Never treat one token as one user.</strong> A person with a phone and a tablet has two tokens; a shared tablet may have one token for several logins. Store a list of endpoints per user, and remove an endpoint on logout if the app is a shared-device app.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Platform-specific payloads with the JSON message structure</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Apple and Google do not accept the same JSON shape. Apple wants an <code>aps</code> object with an <code>alert</code>; Android/FCM wants a <code>notification</code> object. If one topic has both iOS and Android endpoints on it, a single plain-text message is passed through to both, which gives you a lowest-common-denominator notification and no control over sound, badge or custom data.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The fix is the <strong>JSON message structure</strong>: you set the publish parameter <code>MessageStructure</code> to <code>json</code>, and then your <code>Message</code> is itself a JSON object whose keys are platform names. Each phone receives only the value under its own platform's key. Reserve high priority for things the user genuinely needs now; platforms may throttle apps that mark everything urgent.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">SMS: sending a text message</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SMS is simpler to start with: you can publish straight to a phone number in E.164 format (the international format, plus sign, country code, number, no spaces — <code>+14155550123</code>) with no subscription at all, or you can subscribe phone numbers to a topic and text a group at once. There is no app to install and no token to manage. The complexity moves somewhere else: into carriers and law.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Transactional versus promotional.</strong> This is the <code>SMS.SMSType</code> message attribute, and it is not a billing label you pick freely — it declares what kind of message this is. <strong>Transactional</strong> means the message was triggered by the recipient's own action and they need it: a one-time password, a delivery code, an outage alert. <strong>Promotional</strong> means marketing. AWS optimizes transactional messages for reliability, which in practice means better routing and higher delivery success, and it costs more per message; promotional is cheaper and may take less reliable routes. Carriers in many countries also treat the two categories differently and will filter promotional traffic harder. Labelling marketing as transactional to get better delivery is both a policy violation and, in several jurisdictions, a legal one.</li>\n<li><strong>The SMS sandbox.</strong> A new AWS account starts in the <strong>SMS sandbox</strong>, a safety mode where you can only text phone numbers you have explicitly added and verified with a code. This exists to stop a compromised key from texting the world on your bill. To text arbitrary customers you open a support case to <em>move out of the sandbox</em> (request production access), describing your use case, your opt-in flow, and your expected volume.</li>\n<li><strong>Spending limits and quotas.</strong> Your account has a monthly SMS spend limit in US dollars — a hard ceiling, so once you hit it SNS stops sending rather than sending you a shocking bill. New accounts start low, and raising it is also a support request. You can also set a per-message maximum price. Set these deliberately: a bug that loops over your user table sending OTPs is expensive in minutes.</li>\n<li><strong>Origination identities.</strong> The \"from\" a recipient sees comes from an origination identity you own. A <strong>long code</strong> is an ordinary local phone number: easy to get, low throughput, fine for low volume. A <strong>short code</strong> is a 5-6 digit number: high throughput and strong deliverability, but it requires carrier registration, takes weeks, and carries a real monthly cost. A <strong>sender ID</strong> is an alphabetic name like <code>MYSHOP</code> shown instead of a number: pleasant, free in the countries that allow it, but the recipient cannot reply to it.</li>\n<li><strong>Country-by-country regulation is the real difficulty.</strong> Which identity types are allowed, whether sender IDs must be pre-registered, whether local numbers are required, and what content is permitted all vary by destination country, and the rules change. The United States has moved to requiring registration for most business messaging on standard numbers; India requires registered sender IDs and templates; some countries forbid sender IDs entirely. Always check the current AWS documentation for each country you send to rather than assuming your home country's rules travel.</li>\n<li><strong>Opt-out is handled for you, and it is the law.</strong> In most countries a recipient must be able to stop messages by replying with a keyword such as STOP. SNS recognises those replies, records the number on an <strong>opt-out list</strong> for your account, and silently stops delivering to it — you do not have to build this. You can list opted-out numbers with <code>ListPhoneNumbersOptedOut</code>. Opting a number back in is deliberately restricted, because casually undoing an opt-out is exactly the abuse the rule exists to prevent.</li>\n<li><strong>Delivery status logging for SMS.</strong> You can have SNS write a log record for each SMS to CloudWatch Logs (the AWS service that stores log lines and lets you search them) including whether the carrier accepted it, the price charged, and a failure reason such as an invalid number or a blocked destination. This is off until you configure it, and it is the only way to answer \"did that OTP actually reach the customer.\"</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">When SNS is the wrong tool: AWS End User Messaging and Pinpoint</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Be honest about this in an interview, because it shows judgement. SNS mobile push and SMS are excellent for <em>operational</em> messages: alert the on-call engineer, text a one-time password, push \"your build finished.\" They are deliberately thin — no campaign scheduling, no per-user profiles, no A/B testing, no template management, no engagement analytics.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">For customer-facing messaging at scale — segmentation, journeys, campaigns, templates, per-channel analytics across SMS, push, email, voice and in-app — AWS's dedicated offering is what is now branded <strong>AWS End User Messaging</strong>, which absorbed the SMS, push and voice channels formerly known as Amazon Pinpoint. That is where origination identity management, registration workflows and campaign tooling properly live. A good answer is: \"SNS for operational and alerting traffic, End User Messaging for marketing and customer engagement,\" and a note that AWS has been consolidating that story, so check current docs.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Common beginner misconceptions</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>\"A successful Publish means the phone got it.\"</strong> No. It means SNS accepted the message. Delivery through APNs, FCM or a carrier happens afterwards and can fail. Users have several devices; devices get wiped and reused. Map users to a set of endpoints in your own database.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Tokens are stable once I have them.\"</strong> They change and expire, and endpoints get disabled after an uninstall.
