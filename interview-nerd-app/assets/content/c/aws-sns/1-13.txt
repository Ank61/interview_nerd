{"title":"SNS Cost Optimization at Scale","tags":["cost-optimization","pricing","filter-policies","finops"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">The economics of SNS are counterintuitive in one specific way, and that one insight drives every optimization decision: <strong>cost scales with deliveries, not with publishes</strong>. A single Publish to a topic with ten subscribers is one publish request and ten notification deliveries. Adding an eleventh subscriber raises the cost of every message in the system by ten percent, forever, without anyone touching the publisher. Teams that reason about SNS cost as \"messages times a rate\" consistently underestimate by the fan-out factor, and then discover the problem when a topic that was a rounding error becomes a line item. This topic covers the billing dimensions precisely, the levers in priority order, the topic-sprawl failure mode, attribution and anomaly detection, and how the cost shape compares to EventBridge for the same distribution pattern.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A note on figures: exact rates change and vary by Region, so the numbers used in the worked model below are <strong>illustrative and must be checked against the current pricing page</strong>. What genuinely matters in an interview, and what does not change, is the <em>relative order of magnitude</em> between delivery protocols. Getting that ordering right is the answer; quoting a rate to two decimal places is not.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The billing dimensions, precisely</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Publish requests.</strong> Charged per million API requests, with a monthly free tier. The subtlety that catches people: <strong>published data is billed in 64 KB chunks</strong>, so a 256 KB message counts as four requests, not one. A service publishing fat payloads pays four times the request cost of one publishing lean events — which is a direct financial argument for the claim-check pattern (put the blob in S3, publish a small event with a pointer).</li>\n<li><strong>Deliveries, priced per protocol.</strong> This is where the spread lives and where architectural decisions are made. Deliveries to <strong>SQS, Lambda and Kinesis Data Firehose carry no per-delivery notification charge</strong> — you pay only the publish request plus whatever the downstream service costs. <strong>HTTP/S</strong> deliveries carry their own per-million rate, broadly the same order of magnitude as the publish charge. <strong>Mobile push</strong> is similar in order of magnitude. <strong>Email and email-json</strong> are priced per hundred thousand, which puts them roughly two orders of magnitude above SQS-class delivery per message. <strong>SMS</strong> is priced per message and <strong>varies dramatically by destination country</strong>, and is commonly three to four orders of magnitude more expensive than a queue delivery. The practical mental model is: queue and function deliveries are free, webhooks are cheap, email is expensive, SMS is in a different universe.</li>\n<li><strong>Data transfer.</strong> Standard AWS data transfer rates apply to data leaving the Region — relevant for HTTP/S subscribers on the public internet and for cross-Region delivery, irrelevant for in-Region SQS and Lambda fan-out.</li>\n<li><strong>KMS request charges on encrypted topics.</strong> Server-side encryption with a customer managed key means KMS API calls, billed per ten thousand requests on the <em>KMS</em> bill, not the SNS bill. On a very high volume topic this can become a visible cost that nobody attributes to messaging because it appears under a different service. A customer managed key also carries a flat monthly key charge; AWS managed keys avoid that but give you no key policy control.</li>\n<li><strong>FIFO topic specifics.</strong> FIFO topics are priced on their own schedule, with publish charges based on payload bytes rather than plain request counts. If you enable <strong>message archiving and replay</strong>, you pay for <em>archived storage</em> per GB-month over the retention period and for <em>replayed messages</em> when you actually replay. Retention is set per topic, so a 365-day retention on a high-volume FIFO topic that nobody ever replays is pure waste.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The structural insight: cost is a function of the subscription matrix</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Write the cost as a sum over subscriptions rather than over messages and everything becomes clear. First, <strong>every new subscriber is a permanent multiplier</strong> on an existing cost base, which is why subscription creation deserves governance even though it feels like a trivial operation. Second, <strong>the only term you can reduce without removing a consumer is f_i</strong> — the filter fraction — which is why filtering is the dominant lever. Third, <strong>the protocol term dominates everything else when the protocol is expensive</strong>: one email or SMS subscription can outweigh the entire rest of the topic, so the optimization question for those is never \"how do we make delivery cheaper\" but \"how do we send fewer of them\".</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The levers, in priority order</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>1. Filter policies — the dominant lever.</strong> A message filtered out is <em>never delivered and never charged for that delivery</em>. This is the crucial mechanic: filtering is not a small saving, it is the removal of a cost term. A subscriber that receives everything and discards 95 percent in application code pays for 100 percent; the same subscriber with a filter policy pays for 5 percent. Moving that discard logic from the consumer into a filter policy is a 20x reduction on that subscription with no functional change. It also removes the downstream cost — SQS requests, Lambda invocations and duration — which is frequently larger than the SNS saving itself. Always measure with <code>NumberOfNotificationsFilteredOut</code> to confirm the policy is actually doing work.</li>\n<li><strong>2. PublishBatch.</strong> <code>PublishBatch</code> sends up to ten messages in one API call to a topic, cutting request <em>count</em>. Note carefully what it does and does not do: it reduces API call overhead and connection cost, but <strong>billing is still based on 64 KB chunks of published data</strong>, so batching ten tiny messages is a genuine saving while batching ten near-256 KB messages saves far less. It also improves throughput and reduces the chance of publish throttling, which matters operationally as much as financially.</li>\n<li><strong>3. Consolidate or aggregate chatty events.</strong> If a service publishes one event per row of a bulk import, consider publishing one event per batch with a list of identifiers, or a coalesced \"inventory changed\" event rather than an event per attribute. This attacks <code>M</code> directly, which reduces publishes <em>and</em> every delivery term simultaneously — the highest-leverage change available, though it is a contract change and must be versioned.</li>\n<li><strong>4. Choose a cheaper delivery protocol.</strong> Replacing a direct HTTP/S subscription with an SQS queue that the consumer polls converts a charged delivery into a free one, and simultaneously gives you buffering, retry and a DLQ. This is the single most common \"cost optimization that is actually a reliability improvement\" in SNS. The same reasoning applies to replacing per-user email notifications with a digest, or an SMS alert with a push notification where the product allows.</li>\n<li><strong>5. Manage KMS cost on high-volume encrypted topics.</strong> KMS charges per request, so encryption on a very high throughput topic generates a real KMS bill. SNS caches data keys internally, but unlike SQS it does not expose a tunable reuse period, so your levers on the SNS side are choosing between an AWS managed key and a customer managed key (the latter adds a monthly key charge but gives you key policy control and cross-account grants), and avoiding needless key proliferation across many topics. Where the fan-out targets <strong>encrypted SQS queues</strong>, the real tunable is on the queue: raising <code>KmsDataKeyReusePeriodSeconds</code> toward its maximum of 86400 sharply reduces KMS calls, trading a longer data-key lifetime for cost. Also verify you actually need CMK encryption per topic — many organizations apply it reflexively to low-sensitivity operational topics.</li>\n<li><strong>6. Right-size FIFO archive retention.</strong> Archive retention is a per-topic setting and archived storage is billed per GB-month. Pick retention from the actual replay requirement — typically \"how far back would we ever realistically reprocess\", which is usually days or a few weeks, not a year.</li>\n<li><strong>7. Audit SMS as its own budget line.</strong> SMS deserves separate treatment because it is the only SNS dimension where a bug can produce a genuinely large bill in hours. Set an account <strong>SMS monthly spend limit</strong>, alarm on <code>SMSMonthToDateSpentUSD</code> at a fraction of it, and review spend by <code>Country</code> — a single high-cost destination country, or a fraud pattern pumping OTP requests toward expensive destinations, can dominate. Distinguish <strong>Transactional</strong> from <strong>Promotional</strong> SMS types deliberately: transactional costs more and has higher delivery priority, so sending marketing traffic as transactional is a pure waste.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Topic sprawl — the organizational cost failure</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The most expensive SNS mistake is usually not a rate, it is a shape. Teams create one topic per event type — <code>order-created</code>, <code>order-shipped</code>, <code>order-cancelled</code>, <code>order-refunded</code> — and then every consumer that cares about orders subscribes to all four. Now every consumer maintains four subscriptions, every new event type requires every consumer to change, and cross-cutting consumers like audit and analytics maintain dozens. The cost consequences are indirect but large: publishers often end up publishing the same logical event to multiple topics; you cannot filter across topics so consumers subscribe broadly and discard in code (paying full delivery cost); and the operational overhead of DLQs, alarms and IAM policies multiplies by topic count.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The alternative is <strong>one topic per domain, with a mandatory <code>event_type</code> message attribute and subscriber-side filter policies</strong>. Consumers subscribe once and filter to what they need; adding an event type requires no subscriber change; cross-cutting consumers subscribe with no filter. Governance to keep it that way: topics created only through infrastructure-as-code with required tags; a named owning team per topic; a documented event catalogue; a review gate for new topic creation that asks \"why can this not be an event type on an existing domain topic\"; and a periodic audit that flags topics with a single subscriber or with near-duplicate names. The counter-pressure is legitimate and worth acknowledging — separate topics give cleaner IAM boundaries and blast-radius isolation — so the honest rule is <strong>split topics on trust and lifecycle boundaries, not on event type</strong>.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Attribution, tagging and anomaly detection</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Messaging cost is hard to attribute because the cost is incurred by the topic but caused by the subscribers. Practices that work: apply <strong>cost allocation tags</strong> to every topic (owning team, service, environment, data classification) and activate them in the billing console so they appear in Cost Explorer; separate environments into different accounts so dev traffic never hides inside production spend; and, because tags live on topics rather than subscriptions, maintain a subscription inventory — a scheduled job that walks <code>ListSubscriptions</code> and records protocol, endpoint owner and filter policy per subscription, so you can attribute delivery cost to the consuming team rather than the publishing one. Without that, the platform team owning the topic absorbs the cost of every consumer's decisions and has no lever to push back.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">For detection, <strong>AWS Cost Anomaly Detection</strong> with a monitor scoped to SNS (and a separate one for KMS if you run encrypted high-volume topics) catches the step changes that matter: a new subscriber added quietly, a publisher loop, or an SMS fraud pattern. Pair it with the operational metrics — a sudden rise in <code>NumberOfMessagesPublished</code> or a fall in <code>NumberOfNotificationsFilteredOut</code> predicts a bill increase days before the bill arrives, which is the difference between a conversation and an incident.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">SNS fan-out versus EventBridge: the cost shape</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The comparison senior interviews want is not \"which is cheaper\" but \"how do the cost curves differ\". <strong>SNS is priced on publishes plus per-protocol deliveries.</strong> <strong>EventBridge is priced primarily on events published to a bus</strong>, with rule evaluation and delivery to targets not separately charged in the same per-delivery way — so EventBridge's cost is largely a function of event count and is comparatively insensitive to the number of matching rules, while SNS's cost grows with the number of chargeable-protocol subscriptions. On a per-event basis EventBridge's ingestion rate is typically a small multiple of the SNS publish rate.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The practical conclusions: for <strong>very high volume fan-out to SQS and Lambda</strong>, SNS is usually the cheaper primitive because those deliveries carry no notification charge and the publish rate is low. For <strong>moderate volume with complex routing</strong>, EventBridge often wins on total cost of ownership even at a higher per-event rate, because the routing logic you would otherwise build and operate is free. If you enable EventBridge <strong>archive and replay</strong>, budget for archive storage and replay charges — the feature is genuinely valuable but it is not free.
