{"title":"SNS Delivery Semantics and Reliability Internals","tags":["at-least-once","ordering","durability","quotas"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Amazon SNS is usually introduced as \"pub/sub fan-out\", which is accurate and almost useless for a senior interview. The interesting question is what the service actually guarantees, what it deliberately does not guarantee, and what that forces you to build around it. SNS gives you a highly available publish endpoint that accepts a message, stores it redundantly across multiple Availability Zones while it is in flight, and then attempts delivery once per subscription with an independent retry schedule. Everything else — ordering, exactly-once processing, durability you can read back, back-pressure — is either absent on standard topics or is your responsibility. A senior candidate is expected to state those boundaries crisply and then design around them.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">At-Least-Once Delivery and Where Duplicates Actually Come From</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SNS delivers each message to each subscription <strong>at least once</strong>. That phrasing is not hedging: it is a design consequence of running delivery over an unreliable network with retries. The canonical duplicate is not \"SNS sent it twice by mistake\" — it is a <strong>lost acknowledgement</strong>. SNS makes an HTTP call to your endpoint (or an internal call to SQS, Lambda or Firehose). Your side receives the request, commits the work, and begins writing the 200 response. The connection is reset, a load balancer times out, or a node is terminated mid-response. From SNS's point of view the attempt failed, so it retries. From your point of view the work has already been done. You now have two invocations for one logical event.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Ack loss.</strong> Work committed, response never observed. The dominant source of duplicates and the one you cannot engineer away from the SNS side.</li>\n<li><strong>Slow endpoint.</strong> An HTTP/S endpoint that does not respond inside the documented response window (15 seconds) is treated as a failure even if it eventually completes the work. A subscriber that does the work synchronously and then times out will be retried while the original work is still running.</li>\n<li><strong>Publisher retry.</strong> The SDK retries a <code>Publish</code> whose response was lost. That produces two distinct messages with two different message IDs on a standard topic — SNS has no idea they are the same event, because standard topics have no deduplication.</li>\n<li><strong>Application-level replay.</strong> A redrive from a DLQ, a backfill job, or an at-least-once upstream (a Lambda triggered by a stream) republishing.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The operational conclusion is blunt: <strong>every SNS subscriber must be idempotent</strong>, even though \"it is only a notification\". The counter-argument you hear — \"duplicates are rare, we will live with it\" — falls apart the moment the notification triggers a side effect: a charge, an email, an inventory decrement, a webhook to a partner. The standard fix is a <strong>deduplication key</strong> derived from the business event (order ID plus state transition), not from the SNS message ID, because a publisher retry produces a new message ID for the same business event. Store that key in DynamoDB with a conditional write and a TTL comfortably longer than the maximum retry window, and make the handler's effect convergent rather than incremental (set status to SHIPPED, do not increment a counter).</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Ordering on Standard Topics: Best-Effort Means No Guarantee</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Standard SNS topics attempt to deliver in the order published and frequently succeed, which is exactly what makes them dangerous. Teams observe ordered delivery in a low-traffic test environment, assume it is a property, and ship logic that depends on it. Under load or during a partial failure the assumption breaks. The specific mechanisms are worth naming:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Independent retry timelines.</strong> Message A fails its first delivery attempt and is retried twenty seconds later. Message B, published afterwards, succeeds immediately. B is processed before A. Nothing is broken; the retry schedule simply reordered them.</li>\n<li><strong>Multiple delivery paths.</strong> SNS is a distributed service; two messages can traverse different internal hosts and partitions with different latencies. There is no single serialising queue behind a standard topic.</li>\n<li><strong>Concurrent consumers downstream.</strong> Even if SNS delivered perfectly in order into an SQS standard queue, multiple consumer threads pull and process concurrently. The ordering guarantee you actually care about is end to end, and it is lost at the first concurrent hop.</li>\n<li><strong>Publisher concurrency.</strong> Two application threads publishing \"related\" events have no defined order at the service boundary at all.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The senior answer is not \"use FIFO\". It is: decide whether you need ordering or you need <strong>convergence</strong>. Most notification workloads need convergence, which you get from monotonically increasing version numbers or event timestamps plus a conditional write that rejects stale updates, or from a state machine that treats out-of-order transitions as no-ops. That is cheaper, scales without group-ID design, and survives duplicates for free. Reach for FIFO only when the business semantics genuinely require serial application of a sequence.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Subscription Independence and What \"Published Successfully\" Means</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">When you publish one message to a topic with eight subscriptions, SNS fans out to eight <strong>independent delivery pipelines</strong>. Each has its own attempt, its own retry schedule, its own filter policy evaluation, its own delivery policy (for HTTP/S), its own DLQ, and its own CloudWatch dimension. A partner webhook that has been down for six hours does not slow down, block, or back-pressure the Lambda subscription on the same topic. This is the single most valuable property of the fan-out pattern and it is worth saying explicitly in an interview.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The corollary is the part candidates miss. A successful <code>Publish</code> returns a <code>MessageId</code> and an HTTP 200. That response means exactly one thing: <strong>SNS has durably accepted the message and will attempt delivery</strong>. It says nothing about whether any subscriber received it, nothing about whether any subscriber processed it successfully, and nothing about whether a subscriber even exists. A topic with zero subscriptions accepts publishes happily and discards every message. A topic whose only subscription has a filter policy that no longer matches accepts publishes happily and delivers nothing. This is why \"the publish succeeded, so the downstream must have got it\" is a wrong statement, and why end-to-end verification has to come from a downstream signal (a queue depth, a processed-events metric, a business-level reconciliation), never from publish success.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Durability Boundaries: SNS Is Not a Log</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SNS stores messages redundantly across multiple Availability Zones inside a Region while they are being delivered and retried. For everything else, the architectural rule is: <strong>put an SQS queue between SNS and any consumer that must not lose work</strong>. FIFO topics are much lower (documented at 300 publishes per second per FIFO topic, with <code>PublishBatch</code> of up to 10 messages raising the effective message rate).</li>\n<li><strong>Payload size.</strong> 256 KB per message. Larger payloads use the claim-check pattern: write the body to S3, publish a pointer (the SNS Extended Client Library does this for you). This is also just good design — big payloads make filtering, logging and replay expensive.</li>\n<li><strong>Subscriptions per topic.</strong> Very large (millions), so subscription count is essentially never the constraint; topic count per account is in the hundreds of thousands. The real constraint in a fan-out design is downstream capacity, not SNS.</li>\n<li><strong>Batching.</strong> <code>PublishBatch</code> sends up to 10 messages in one request, which reduces request count and cost but does not change delivery semantics — each message is still fanned out and retried independently, and a batch can partially fail.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">When you exceed a rate quota, <code>Publish</code> fails with a throttling error rather than queuing. The AWS SDKs retry throttled requests automatically with <strong>exponential backoff and jitter</strong>, but the default retry count is small; a sustained overload will surface as publish exceptions in your application. The correct handling is to treat a throttled publish as a real failure in a critical path: either buffer locally and retry with a longer backoff, or — better — decouple by writing to a local queue or stream and publishing from a worker whose rate you control. Sizing a spike against a per-topic quota and then discovering there is also a per-account dimension is a classic production surprise.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Failure Modes Worth Naming in an Interview</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>SNS does not back-pressure.</strong> If a subscriber is slower than the publish rate, SNS does not slow down. It attempts delivery, fails or times out, retries on schedule, and eventually gives up. A slow HTTP subscriber back-pressures nothing — it just loses messages at the end of the retry window. The fix is a queue in front of it (the queue absorbs the burst and the consumer drains at its own pace) or a <code>throttlePolicy</code> with <code>maxReceivesPerSecond</code> on the subscription.</li>\n<li><strong>Silent loss with no DLQ.</strong> A subscription without a <code>RedrivePolicy</code> discards messages when retries are exhausted. No error is raised anywhere in your application. The only evidence is a CloudWatch metric that nobody is alarming on. Treat \"every production subscription has a DLQ\" as a deployment-time check, not a best practice.</li>\n<li><strong>Filter drift.</strong> A publisher stops setting a message attribute (a refactor, a new code path) and a subscription's filter silently matches nothing. Publishes succeed, deliveries go to zero, and no alarm fires unless you watch <code>NumberOfNotificationsFilteredOut</code> per subscription.</li>\n<li><strong>Permission drift.</strong> A change to the SQS queue policy or Lambda resource policy causes SNS to fail delivery with an authorisation error for every message. Retries burn through the window and everything lands in the DLQ — or nowhere.</li>\n<li><strong>Cross-Region and cross-account assumptions.</strong> SNS delivery is in-Region; cross-Region fan-out means a subscriber in the other Region or an explicit relay. Cross-account subscriptions need policy on both sides.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Production Scenario: \"The Notification Was Lost\"</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Support escalates: a customer's order-shipped notification never arrived. The publishing service's logs show a successful <code>Publish</code> with a <code>MessageId</code>. The team's first instinct is \"SNS lost it\". Work the pipeline instead, in this order:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Was it even a delivery?</strong> Compare <code>NumberOfMessagesPublished</code> on the topic against <code>NumberOfNotificationsDelivered</code> and <code>NumberOfNotificationsFilteredOut</code> <em>per subscription</em>. If FilteredOut moved, this is a filter policy problem, not a delivery problem — very often a publisher that stopped setting an attribute, or a type mismatch (the number 42 sent as the string \"42\"), or a case difference.</li>\n<li><strong>Did delivery fail?</strong> If <code>NumberOfNotificationsFailed</code> moved, enable <strong>delivery status logging</strong> on the subscription (an IAM role plus a success sample rate) and read the CloudWatch Logs entries: they carry the endpoint, the status code, the provider response and the attempt count. That distinguishes a 500 from your endpoint, a timeout, and an authorisation failure.</li>\n<li><strong>Where did it end up?</strong> Check the subscription DLQ. If there is no DLQ, you have just found the real bug — the message was dropped and there is no artefact to look at, which is unrecoverable by design.</li>\n<li><strong>Was it delivered but not processed?</strong> If Delivered moved, SNS did its job and the defect is downstream: the consumer crashed after receiving, the SQS message aged out, or an idempotency key wrongly suppressed it as a duplicate.</li>\n<li><strong>Was it ordering?</strong> If a later \"order cancelled\" event was applied after \"order shipped\" because of a retry, nothing was lost — the events were reordered. That is a convergence bug in the consumer, and the fix is a version guard, not FIFO.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The structural remediation from this incident is almost always the same three items: attach DLQs to every subscription, alarm on <code>NumberOfNotificationsFailed</code> and on per-subscription <code>NumberOfNotificationsFilteredOut</code> dropping to zero, and put SQS queues in front of consumers that must not lose work so that a \"lost\" message becomes an observable message sitting in a queue.</p>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"SNS guarantees at-least-once delivery per subscription and best-effort ordering on standard topics.
