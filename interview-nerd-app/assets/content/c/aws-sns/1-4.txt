{"title":"Message Data Protection — PII Discovery, Redaction, and Payload Governance","tags":["sns","data-protection","pii","compliance"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Most engineers who have used Amazon SNS for years can describe topics, subscriptions, filter policies and encryption at rest, and then stop. Almost nobody mentions <strong>message data protection policies</strong>, which is exactly why they are one of the highest-leverage things to know in a senior AWS interview. A data protection policy is a JSON document attached to a topic that inspects the <em>body of every message</em> for sensitive data, and then audits, blocks, or de-identifies it — inbound on publish or outbound per subscriber. It turns SNS from a dumb pipe into a control point where compliance can actually be enforced, rather than a place where personal data quietly leaks into a dozen downstream systems.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The problem: fan-out multiplies compliance exposure</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Consider a topic called <code>customer-events</code>. A team adds a field to the event payload — at first it is an internal identifier, then someone adds <code>email</code> \"for debugging\", then a downstream team asks for <code>phone</code>, then a support tool adds the last four digits of a card and eventually the whole PAN because a developer was in a hurry. The topic has eleven subscriptions: six SQS queues, two Lambda functions, an HTTPS endpoint operated by a vendor, a Firehose stream that lands everything in S3, and an email subscription that somebody created during an incident two years ago and never deleted.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The moment that payload changed, personal data started flowing to eleven destinations, at least three of which are outside the producing team's account and one of which writes it to durable storage indefinitely. Nobody approved this. Nobody can even see it, because a message body is opaque to IAM, to CloudTrail, and to the topic owner. This is the defining property of fan-out risk: <strong>the blast radius of a payload mistake is the subscription list, not the publisher</strong>, and the subscription list grows by accretion. The exposure is invisible until an audit, a customer data-subject request, or a breach investigation forces someone to enumerate where a field actually went.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Classic answers to this are all weak. Code review catches nothing once the producer is a third-party service. Encryption at rest with KMS protects the message from AWS storage compromise but does absolutely nothing about a legitimately subscribed consumer reading plaintext. Filter policies operate on message <em>attributes</em>, not the body, so they cannot see the sensitive field at all. Data protection policies are the one mechanism that inspects the body itself.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">How a data protection policy is structured</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The policy is attached to a topic (via <code>PutDataProtectionPolicy</code>, or the <code>DataProtectionPolicy</code> attribute at create time) and has four moving parts: a policy <code>Version</code>, an optional <code>Configuration</code> block declaring custom identifiers, and a list of <code>Statement</code> objects. Each statement answers four questions: <em>which data</em>, <em>whose traffic</em>, <em>which direction</em>, and <em>what to do about it</em>.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>DataIdentifier.</strong> What to look for. AWS ships <strong>managed data identifiers</strong> for common sensitive types — email address, credit card number, national identifiers such as US SSN or India Aadhaar, phone numbers, bank account numbers, IP addresses, and crucially credential-shaped things like AWS secret access keys and private keys. They are referenced by ARN, for example <code>arn:aws:dataprotection::aws:data-identifier/EmailAddress</code>. The managed identifiers are not naive regexes; the numeric ones apply checksum and keyword-proximity logic so that a random 16-digit order number is not reported as a card.</li>\n<li><strong>Custom data identifiers.</strong> Declared in the policy's <code>Configuration</code> block as a name plus a regular expression, then referenced by name in statements. This is how you catch company-specific shapes: an internal customer ID format, a loyalty number, a session token prefix, a partner API key pattern. There is a small per-policy limit on how many you may declare and a length cap on the regex, so treat them as a handful of high-value patterns rather than a general scanner.</li>\n<li><strong>DataDirection.</strong> <code>Inbound</code> means evaluated when a message is published to the topic. <code>Outbound</code> means evaluated when the message is delivered to a subscription. This single field is what makes the feature genuinely powerful, and it is covered below.</li>\n<li><strong>Principal.</strong> Which principals the statement applies to. For inbound statements this is the publishing principal; for outbound statements it is the subscribing principal — the account or role that owns the endpoint receiving the message. <code>[\"*\"]</code> means everyone.</li>\n<li><strong>Operation.</strong> One of <code>Audit</code>, <code>Deny</code>, or <code>Deidentify</code>.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The three operations</h4>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; margin-top: 0.75rem\">\n<table style=\"width: 100%; border-collapse: collapse; font-size: 0.92rem\">\n<thead><tr style=\"text-align: left\" data-box=\"1\"><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Operation</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Effect</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Direction</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">When to use</th></tr></thead>\n<tbody>\n<tr><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Audit</td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Message passes through untouched; a finding describing what was detected is written to an audit destination</td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Inbound only</td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Discovery phase — find out what is actually flowing before you change behaviour</td></tr>\n<tr><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Deny</td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Inbound: the <code>Publish</code> call is rejected with an authorization error. It describes what was published to the topic; it is not a per-delivery log. And note the sharpness of inbound <code>Deny</code>: it fails the publish API call. That is a behaviour change for the producer, not a silent drop, which is exactly why rollout order matters.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Direction: the differentiator</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The inbound/outbound split lets one topic serve consumers with different data entitlements, which previously required either publishing two topics with two payload shapes or building a redaction Lambda in the middle of the fan-out. If you de-identify inbound, the raw value is gone for everyone, permanently, and no outbound exception can bring it back. So if even one consumer legitimately needs the real value, the sensitive handling must be expressed outbound, with the privileged subscriber either excluded from the statement or matched by a narrower <code>Principal</code>.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Audit destinations and sampling</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An <code>Audit</code> operation names a <code>FindingsDestination</code>, which can be a <strong>CloudWatch Logs log group</strong>, a <strong>Firehose delivery stream</strong>, or an <strong>S3 bucket</strong>. CloudWatch Logs is the right default for discovery because you can immediately run Logs Insights queries over findings. Firehose is right when you want findings joined into an existing security data lake, and S3 when you simply want cheap long retention. The destination must grant SNS permission to write — for a log group this normally means a resource policy on a log group under the <code>/aws/vendedlogs/</code> prefix; for S3 and Firehose the equivalent bucket policy or delivery-stream permissions must exist. A policy referencing a destination SNS cannot write to is a common first-attempt failure.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Audit statements carry a <strong>SampleRate</strong>, a percentage of messages to inspect and report on. This is a cost-and-volume control, not a security control. On a topic doing tens of thousands of messages per second, a 99 percent sample rate generates an enormous findings volume; 1 to 5 percent is plenty to answer \"does this topic carry email addresses at all, and roughly how often\". The important reasoning is statistical: sampling reliably detects a field that appears in most messages, and unreliably detects a rare field that appears in one message in ten thousand — which is often the one you care about, because rare leakage is usually an error path dumping an exception payload. So use a low sample rate for the broad survey and a high one for a short, targeted window when you are hunting a rare pattern.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A finding tells you which data identifier matched and where in the message, without reproducing the sensitive value itself — which matters, because a findings destination full of the actual PII would simply relocate the compliance problem into a log group.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Masking versus redaction, and the consequence for consumers</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Masking</strong> replaces each character of the matched value with a chosen character, so <code>alice@example.com</code> becomes something like <code>#################</code>. The field remains present and its length is preserved, which keeps schemas valid and, importantly, keeps <em>shape</em> information visible for debugging. <strong>Redaction</strong> removes the value entirely, which is stronger — preserved length is itself a small information leak, and for short high-entropy values like a 4-digit PIN, masking tells an observer exactly how many characters to brute force.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Either way, the design consequence is the same and is the thing interviewers probe: <strong>a de-identified message is no longer sufficient for any consumer that needs the real value</strong>, and de-identification is irreversible — SNS is not tokenising, there is no lookup table to reverse it. So the moment you apply de-identification you must answer: how does the legitimate consumer get the value? Three answers, in order of preference:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>It does not need it.</strong> Most consumers need a stable customer identifier, not an email address. Publish the ID and let the consumer call the customer service if it genuinely needs contact details. This converts an uncontrolled broadcast into an audited, authorised, per-request access.</li>\n<li><strong>Outbound exception.</strong> Keep the raw value in the topic and de-identify outbound for everyone except the one entitled principal. Simple, but it means the raw value is stored in the topic and any future subscription is one policy mistake away from seeing it.</li>\n<li><strong>Claim check.</strong> Do not put the value in the message at all — see below.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Operational rollout: Audit, measure, then enforce</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This is the part that separates a candidate who has read the docs from one who has run it. <strong>Never start at Deny.</strong> An inbound <code>Deny</code> causes <code>Publish</code> to fail. If a producer treats publish failures as fatal you have created an outage in the producing service; if it treats them as non-fatal you have created silent, permanent event loss. Either is a bad Tuesday. The safe sequence:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Phase 1 — Audit, broad, low sample.</strong> Attach an Audit statement covering every managed identifier that is plausibly relevant, at a low sample rate, into CloudWatch Logs. Run it for at least a full business cycle so weekly batch jobs and month-end processes are included. The output is a factual inventory: which identifiers appear, how often, from which publishers.</li>\n<li><strong>Phase 2 — Analyse and socialise.</strong> Take the findings to the producing teams. Usually a large fraction turns out to be fixable at the source in a sprint — someone logging a whole request object into an event field. Fixing the producer is always better than masking at the topic.</li>\n<li><strong>Phase 3 — De-identify outbound.</strong> Apply de-identification to the consumers that clearly do not need the data. This is reversible in the sense that you can change the policy, and it does not fail any publish.</li>\n<li><strong>Phase 4 — Deny, narrowly.</strong> Only for the categories that are genuinely never acceptable — card numbers, credentials, private keys — and only after alarming on Audit findings has gone quiet, so you have evidence that Deny will not fire in production. Announce the change and monitor publish error rates on the switch.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Add a permanent Audit statement alongside enforcement so that a new sensitive field appearing next quarter creates a finding rather than a surprise, and alarm on finding counts rather than eyeballing a log group.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Complementary payload governance</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Data protection policies are a safety net, not a design. The architecture around them matters more.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Schema definition and versioning.</strong> Every event should have an explicit, reviewed schema — in a registry such as the EventBridge schema registry, or at minimum a JSON Schema checked into a repository with the producer. A schema turns \"someone added a field\" into a pull request that a privacy reviewer can see. Version additively: new optional fields are a minor version, removing or retyping a field is a major version that requires a parallel-publish migration because SNS gives you no way to coordinate consumer upgrades.</li>\n<li><strong>Claim check.</strong> Publish a pointer, not the payload: put the sensitive body in S3 under a key the consumer must be authorised to read, and publish an event containing the object key, a content hash, and non-sensitive metadata for filtering. Now access is governed by an S3 bucket policy and KMS key policy, every read is an auditable S3 data event, retention is controlled by a lifecycle rule, and deletion for a data-subject request is a single object delete rather than an unbounded hunt across eleven consumers. The cost is an extra round trip and a lifecycle race — the consumer must handle the object being gone. This pattern also solves the separate problem of SNS's 256 KB message size limit.</li>\n<li><strong>Never put secrets in messages.</strong> Passwords, API keys and tokens do not belong in an event under any policy. If a consumer needs a credential it should assume a role or read Secrets Manager itself. The managed identifiers for AWS secret keys and private keys exist precisely because this happens; treat any such finding as a security incident with credential rotation, not as a policy tuning exercise, because the value has already been broadcast.</li>\n<li><strong>Retention awareness.</strong> Data protection applies at publish and delivery. It does nothing about copies already sitting in an SQS queue, an S3 archive, or a consumer's database. Enabling a policy today does not clean up yesterday.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Production scenario: a compliance review of a customer-data topic</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Your topic <code>customer-lifecycle</code> is subscribed to by an internal fraud service, a marketing analytics queue, a data lake via Firehose, and a partner's HTTPS endpoint in another account.
