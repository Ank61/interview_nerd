{"title":"Cross-Account and Multi-Region SNS Architectures","tags":["sns","cross-account","multi-region","disaster-recovery"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Once an event backbone outgrows a single team, the interesting problems stop being about SNS mechanics and become about <strong>boundaries</strong>: who owns the topic, who owns the queue, who pays, who can subscribe, what happens when a Region is impaired, and how you stop a permissive topic policy turning your event stream into an exfiltration channel. A senior candidate is expected to describe the full cross-account subscription flow from memory, name the ownership and billing split, and be honest about what multi-Region actually buys you.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cross-account fan-out, end to end</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The canonical shape is a platform team in account A owning a topic, and consumer teams in accounts B, C and D each owning an SQS queue subscribed to it. Three resource policies have to line up, and the direction of each is what candidates get wrong:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>The topic policy (account A)</strong> must allow the consumer principal to call <code>sns:Subscribe</code> on the topic, and typically <code>sns:GetTopicAttributes</code> so their infrastructure-as-code can read it. This is the permission to <em>create</em> a subscription.</li>\n<li><strong>The queue policy (account B)</strong> must allow the <code>sns.amazonaws.com</code> service principal to call <code>sqs:SendMessage</code> on the queue, conditioned on <code>aws:SourceArn</code> equal to the topic ARN. Without the condition, <em>any</em> topic in <em>any</em> account that learns the queue URL can deliver into it.</li>\n<li><strong>The caller's IAM identity policy</strong> must allow <code>sns:Subscribe</code> and the queue-side setup actions. Resource policy and identity policy must both allow for a cross-account call — this is the standard cross-account IAM rule and is a common source of confusing <code>AuthorizationError</code> responses.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">There is one more step people forget: a cross-account subscription created by the <em>queue owner</em> is confirmed automatically for the SQS protocol, but if the <em>topic owner</em> creates the subscription on behalf of another account, the subscription may sit in <code>PendingConfirmation</code> until the endpoint owner confirms. The cleaner pattern — and the one to advocate — is that <strong>the consumer creates their own subscription</strong>, because then the consumer's IaC owns both sides of their integration.</p>\n<div retry</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The <strong>topic owner pays for publishes and for SNS's delivery attempts</strong>. The <strong>queue owner pays for SQS requests, storage and their own consumer compute</strong>. This split is not trivia — it is the reason the model scales organisationally. A consumer team that subscribes carelessly and then polls aggressively pays for its own mistake, and a platform team is not funding an unbounded number of downstream consumers' processing.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The ownership split for reliability follows the same line. SNS's delivery retry policy to the queue is the topic owner's concern; <strong>everything after the message lands in the queue — visibility timeouts, redrive policy, DLQ, poison-message handling, backlog alarms — belongs to the consumer</strong>. This is the strongest argument for the queue-per-consumer pattern over subscribing Lambda or HTTPS endpoints directly: it puts a clean, ownable durability boundary at the account line. A consumer can be down for a day and recover by draining its own queue, with no involvement from the platform team and no impact on any other consumer. Direct Lambda or HTTPS subscriptions push failure handling back across the boundary into the topic owner's DLQ and retry policy, which nobody wants to operate on someone else's behalf.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The topic policy as an exfiltration path</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This is the control worth raising unprompted in an interview. A topic policy that grants <code>sns:Subscribe</code> too broadly — <code>\"Principal\": \"*\"</code>, or an account-level grant to a wide set of accounts, or an unconditioned <code>aws:PrincipalOrgID</code> on a topic carrying sensitive data — means that <strong>anyone matching that principal can subscribe an endpoint of their choosing and receive every event published to that topic, forever, silently</strong>. There is no approval step. The topic owner may not notice; nothing in the publisher's behaviour changes. If the permitted protocol set includes <code>https</code> or <code>email</code>, the endpoint can be outside AWS entirely, and you have a continuous, self-service data egress channel with no egress controls in front of it.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Defences, in order of strength:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Enumerate principals</strong> rather than wildcarding. Grant <code>sns:Subscribe</code> to specific account IDs or, better, specific role ARNs — the consumer's deployment role, not its whole account.</li>\n<li><strong>Constrain the subscription shape</strong> with the <code>sns:Protocol</code> and <code>sns:Endpoint</code> condition keys, so a grant to subscribe means \"subscribe an SQS queue in your own account\", not \"subscribe anything\". This is covered in depth in the security topic and is the single most effective control here.</li>\n<li><strong>Scope by organisation</strong> with <code>aws:PrincipalOrgID</code> as a guardrail, not as the whole grant.</li>\n<li><strong>Detect</strong>: a CloudTrail-driven detection on <code>Subscribe</code> and <code>SetTopicAttributes</code> for sensitive topics, and a periodic reconciliation of the actual subscription list against the expected one in IaC. New subscriptions on a sensitive topic should page someone.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Organisation-wide patterns</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">In an AWS Organizations estate, the <code>aws:PrincipalOrgID</code> condition key lets a topic policy say \"any principal in my organisation\" without enumerating accounts that change weekly. It is the right tool for a genuinely organisation-wide, low-sensitivity backbone — a deployment-events or infrastructure-notifications topic — because it removes the policy update from the path of every new account. <code>aws:PrincipalOrgPaths</code> narrows this further to a specific organisational unit, which is how you express \"any account in the production OU\" without listing them.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two caveats. First, org-scoping is a <strong>coarse</strong> control: every principal in the org includes every developer with a broad role in every sandbox account. For a topic carrying customer data that is far too wide; combine it with protocol and endpoint conditions, or enumerate. Second, these conditions do not remove the need for the consumer-side queue policy, and they do not tell you who actually subscribed — you still need the CloudTrail detection.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">On governance, the sustainable model is a <strong>delegated administration</strong> split: a platform team owns topic definitions, naming, encryption standards and the policy templates, expressed in IaC and applied via a pipeline; consumer teams own their queues, their subscriptions, their filter policies and their DLQs in their own accounts and repositories. Service Control Policies at the OU level provide the hard floor — for example denying <code>sns:SetTopicAttributes</code> on production topics to anyone but the platform pipeline role, or denying the creation of <code>email</code> and <code>https</code> subscriptions outright in accounts that have no legitimate need for them.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cross-Region delivery versus a topic per Region</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An SNS topic is a <strong>Regional</strong> resource. There is no built-in replication, no global topic, no cross-Region ordering. You have two options and they trade off cleanly:</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; margin-top: 0.75rem\">\n<table style=\"width: 100%; border-collapse: collapse; font-size: 0.92rem\">\n<thead><tr style=\"text-align: left\" data-box=\"1\"><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Option</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">How it works</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Costs and risks</th></tr></thead>\n<tbody>\n<tr><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>Cross-Region subscription</strong></td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Subscribe a queue or HTTPS endpoint in Region 2 directly to the topic in Region 1</td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Every message crosses the Region boundary individually, paying inter-Region data transfer per message and adding tens of milliseconds of delivery latency. And note that the replication forwarder does not preserve ordering or exactly-once semantics across Regions — a replayed or retried forward produces a duplicate in the remote Region, which the remote consumers must absorb.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Multi-Region architectures, honestly</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Active-active</strong> means both Regions publish and both consume. The messaging tier is the easy part — two topics, two sets of consumers, a forwarder or dual publish. The hard parts are the consequences: an event produced in Region 1 and forwarded to Region 2 may be processed in both, so every consumer needs <strong>cross-Region idempotency keyed on a business identifier</strong>, and a dedup store that is itself replicated (a DynamoDB global table, say) or partitioned so that only one Region owns a given key. Ordering across Regions is simply not available: FIFO ordering is per topic, per message group, within a Region, and a forwarder that retries will reorder. If your domain requires global ordering, the honest answer is to partition ownership so each entity is owned by exactly one Region at a time, and route by entity rather than pretending the messaging tier can give you a global sequence.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Active-passive</strong> is far more common and far more defensible: the primary Region carries all traffic, the secondary has the same topics and subscriptions deployed and idle, and failover means redirecting publishers. This is simpler because the duplicate and ordering problems mostly vanish, but it has its own failure mode — the <strong>idle path is untested</strong>. A secondary Region whose topic policies drifted, whose KMS key grants were never created, or whose queue policies reference the wrong topic ARN will fail at exactly the moment you need it. Regular failover exercises are the only thing that makes active-passive real.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The sentence worth saying out loud: <strong>the hard part of multi-Region is never SNS — it is the consumers' state and deduplication across Regions.</strong> Standing up a second topic is an afternoon. Making a consumer's database, its idempotency store and its ordering assumptions correct when the same logical event can arrive twice from two Regions is a quarter of work, and it is where the design conversation should go.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Disaster recovery for the messaging tier</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Be precise about what you lose if a Region is impaired. Because SNS on a standard topic <strong>does not store messages</strong>, what is lost is the <strong>in-flight notifications</strong>: anything published but not yet delivered, and anything a publisher could not publish during the impairment. There is no backlog waiting to be drained on the SNS side when the Region recovers, and no way to retrieve what was in flight.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two things change that answer. A <strong>queue in the path</strong> means messages that were delivered to SQS before the impairment are durably waiting, so a consumer outage — as opposed to a full Region impairment — loses nothing. And <strong>archiving on a FIFO topic</strong> means the published history is retained and can be replayed to a subscription once the Region recovers, converting \"those events are gone\" into \"we replayed the window\". Neither helps if the Region hosting them is the impaired one, which is the point: for a true Region-loss scenario your RPO is determined by <strong>where the publisher can still write</strong>. That is why the strongest pattern is publishers that fail over to the secondary Region's topic, and consumers in both Regions, with idempotency absorbing the overlap. Set explicit RTO and RPO for the messaging tier and confirm the publishers' failover behaviour — a publisher with a hard-coded topic ARN and no retry to the secondary makes every other piece of DR investment irrelevant.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Infrastructure as code and drift</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Cross-account messaging is where IaC discipline is decided. The split that works: the topic, its policy and its encryption live in the platform team's stack; each subscription and its queue live in the consumer's stack in the consumer's account. The consumer's stack takes the topic ARN as a parameter and creates both the queue policy and the subscription, so one pull request in one repository owns one integration. Avoid the anti-pattern of the platform stack creating subscriptions on behalf of consumers — it makes the platform team a bottleneck, and it means deleting a consumer's queue leaves an orphaned subscription in someone else's stack.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Drift</strong> is the chronic problem. Topic policies get widened by hand during an incident and never reverted; a subscription created in the console during a demo persists for three years; a filter policy edited manually diverges from the repository. The mitigations are a periodic reconciliation that lists actual subscriptions and topic attributes and diffs them against the expected set (CloudFormation drift detection, a Config rule, or a scheduled script), an SCP that prevents manual <code>SetTopicAttributes</code> and <code>Subscribe</code> outside the pipeline role, and CloudTrail alarms so a manual change generates a ticket rather than a surprise at the next audit.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Interview scenario: an organisation-wide event backbone</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">You are asked to design an event backbone for an organisation with forty accounts across two Regions, where a platform team publishes domain events and any team may consume. The design: <strong>one topic per event domain</strong> in the platform account (not one giant topic — the topic is the unit of both authorisation and blast radius, so <code>orders</code>, <code>payments</code> and <code>customers</code> are separate), encrypted with customer managed KMS keys because cross-account access requires one. Sensitivity tiers the policy: low-sensitivity topics use <code>aws:PrincipalOrgID</code> plus a condition restricting <code>sns:Protocol</code> to <code>sqs</code> so nobody can attach an email or HTTPS endpoint; topics carrying customer data enumerate the specific consumer roles and go through a review, because org-wide would include every developer in every sandbox.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Every consumer owns an <strong>SQS queue in its own account</strong> with its own redrive policy and DLQ, subscribes itself from its own IaC, and applies a filter policy so it receives only what it needs. The platform team owns publishing, encryption, topic policy templates and the subscription-change detection. For the second Region, a <strong>topic per Region with a forwarder</strong> rather than cross-Region subscriptions, because with forty accounts the fan-out would otherwise cross the wire once per consumer. The stance is active-passive with publishers failing over, and the design document states plainly that consumers must be idempotent on the business event ID because failover and forwarder retries will produce duplicates. Governance: SCPs denying topic-attribute changes outside the pipeline role, CloudTrail detections on <code>Subscribe</code>, <code>SetTopicAttributes</code> and any removal of encryption, and a quarterly reconciliation of live subscriptions against IaC. And the honest caveat in the review: the hard work for consuming teams is not subscribing, it is making their state correct under duplicate delivery.</p>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"Cross-account fan-out needs three policies to line up: the topic policy allowing the consumer to Subscribe, the consumer's queue policy allowing sns.amazonaws.com to SendMessage with a SourceArn condition, and the caller's own IAM policy.
