{"title":"S3 Transfer Acceleration","tags":["s3","transfer-acceleration","cloudfront","performance"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\"><strong>S3 Transfer Acceleration</strong> speeds up uploads and downloads to and from an S3 bucket by routing traffic through CloudFront's global network of <strong>edge locations</strong> and then over the optimized <strong>AWS backbone network</strong>, rather than letting it travel over the unpredictable public internet all the way to the bucket's home region.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">How It Works</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Normally, a client uploading directly to <code>my-bucket.s3.us-east-1.amazonaws.com</code> routes over the public internet the entire way to <code>us-east-1</code>, and public internet routing between distant geographies is often congested, indirect, and subject to unpredictable latency. With Transfer Acceleration enabled, the client instead uploads to a special endpoint, <code>my-bucket.s3-accelerate.amazonaws.com</code>, which resolves to the nearest CloudFront <strong>edge location</strong>. From there, the data travels over AWS's private, highly optimized <strong>backbone network</strong> directly to the bucket's region. Concretely, for a walked-through example: a <strong>user in India</strong> uploading to a bucket in <code>us-east-1</code> connects to a CloudFront edge location in India, and that edge location forwards the bytes across AWS's backbone to the S3 bucket in the US — avoiding the congested, multi-hop public internet path the request would otherwise take.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">When It's Useful</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Transfer Acceleration provides the most benefit when:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li>Clients are <strong>geographically distant</strong> from the bucket's region (for example, uploading from Asia to a US bucket, or vice versa).</li>\n<li>Files are <strong>large</strong>, so the cumulative benefit of a faster network path is significant rather than negligible.</li>\n<li>The standard internet route to the bucket's region is known to be <strong>slow, congested, or highly variable</strong>.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">It provides little to no benefit — and simply adds cost — when the client is already close to the bucket's region, when files are small, or when the public internet path is already fast and direct.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Testing Before You Enable It</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Because Transfer Acceleration carries an additional per-GB cost, AWS provides the <strong>Amazon S3 Transfer Acceleration Speed Comparison tool</strong>, a browser-based tool that measures and compares upload speed to your bucket with and without acceleration from your current location, across multiple AWS regions. The correct interview answer to \"how do you know if it will help?\" is: <strong>run the speed comparison tool first</strong> rather than assuming — it will show empirically whether the accelerated path outperforms the direct path for your actual network conditions, before you commit to paying for it in production.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cost</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Transfer Acceleration is billed as an <strong>additional per-GB data transfer fee</strong> on top of standard S3 data transfer pricing, charged only for the bytes actually accelerated (and typically only charged if acceleration actually provided a speed benefit over the standard path, per AWS's pricing model).
