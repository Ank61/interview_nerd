{"title":"S3 Disaster Recovery Design","tags":["disaster-recovery","replication","versioning","architecture"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Designing S3 for disaster recovery means combining several independent features — <strong>Versioning, Cross-Region Replication, Object Lock, KMS, CloudFront, and Route 53/application failover</strong> — into a system that survives not just hardware failure but regional outages, human error, and malicious deletion. The single most important idea to state clearly in an interview is this: <strong>S3's extremely high durability (11 nines) is not the same thing as having a recoverable copy.</strong> Durability describes the probability that a stored object survives against internal storage-hardware failure across S3's redundant infrastructure — it says nothing about protection from an application bug that overwrites data, an operator who deletes the wrong prefix, or a ransomware actor who encrypts and deletes objects wholesale. All three of those failure modes replicate perfectly to a second region just like a legitimate write does, because S3 (and replication) cannot distinguish \"correct write\" from \"mistaken write.\"</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Replication Is Not Backup</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This is the crux of the DR conversation. Replication is a <strong>synchronization mechanism</strong>: it copies whatever happens in the source to the destination, as faithfully and quickly as possible. A backup is fundamentally different — it is a <strong>point-in-time, isolated copy</strong> that is deliberately insulated from ongoing changes to the source, so that a mistake in the source cannot propagate into it. If someone accidentally runs a script that deletes every object under a prefix, plain CRR will happily replicate every one of those deletes to the DR region within minutes — you now have two empty prefixes, not one intact copy and one empty one. This is why real S3 DR design layers <strong>Versioning</strong> (so deletes are soft, producing recoverable delete markers instead of destroying data) and <strong>Object Lock</strong> (so objects genuinely cannot be deleted or overwritten during a retention window, even by an attacker with valid credentials) on top of replication, turning it from pure synchronization into something closer to an actual backup.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">RPO and RTO in an S3 Context</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>RPO (Recovery Point Objective)</strong> — how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time. With standard CRR this is typically \"a few minutes\" of unreplicated writes; with <strong>Replication Time Control</strong> it becomes a guaranteed ceiling of 15 minutes for 99.99% of objects. An RPO of zero would require synchronous multi-region writes, which S3 replication does not provide (it is asynchronous by design).</li>\n<li><strong>RTO (Recovery Time Objective)</strong> — how long it takes to actually fail over and resume serving traffic. For S3-backed static content or object storage, RTO is largely driven by how fast <strong>Route 53 health checks and DNS failover</strong> (or CloudFront origin failover) detect the primary region is down and redirect traffic to the DR region's bucket/origin — this can be minutes, not the days associated with restoring from cold backup tape.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">A Concrete DR Architecture</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Versioning enabled</strong> on both the primary and DR buckets — a prerequisite for replication, and the mechanism that turns overwrites/deletes into recoverable prior versions rather than permanent loss.</li>\n<li><strong>Cross-Region Replication (with RTC)</strong> from the primary bucket to a bucket in a geographically distant Region, giving a continuously up-to-date copy with a bounded RPO.</li>\n<li><strong>Object Lock in Compliance Mode</strong> on the DR bucket (or both) for critical data classes, so that even a compromised admin credential or a malicious insider cannot purge the DR copy during its retention window — directly defending against ransomware and insider threats that durability alone does nothing to stop.</li>\n<li><strong>KMS with Regional keys</strong> in each Region, with the replication role granted cross-key permissions, so encrypted data remains protected at rest in both locations without a shared-key single point of failure.</li>\n<li><strong>CloudFront with an origin group</strong> (primary + secondary origin) or Route 53 DNS failover with health checks against the primary Region's endpoint, so read traffic (and, for static/JAMstack-style apps, effectively all traffic) automatically shifts to the DR bucket/Region when the primary becomes unreachable.</li>\n<li><strong>Application-level failover logic</strong> for write paths, since S3 replication is one-directional by default — an application that needs to keep writing during a regional outage typically needs bidirectional replication or a documented \"promote DR to primary\" runbook, not just passive failover.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; What protects you?\" If the bucket only has durability guarantees and no versioning, the objects are simply gone — durability protected the bits against disk failure, not against a valid, authorized-looking DeleteObject call. With <strong>Versioning</strong> enabled, a normal delete only adds a delete marker, and prior versions remain recoverable by removing the marker or restoring specific versions — unless the attacker also issues version-specific permanent deletes, which <strong>Object Lock in Compliance Mode</strong> would categorically block regardless of the caller's permissions, including root.
