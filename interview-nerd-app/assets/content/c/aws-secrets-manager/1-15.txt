{"title":"Secret Governance at Scale — Sprawl, Migration, Cost, and Build-vs-Buy","tags":["aws","secrets manager","governance","architecture"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Secrets Manager is easy with ten secrets and hard with ten thousand. The technical mechanics do not change — rotation, KMS, IAM, replication all work identically — but a completely different class of problem appears, and it is organisational rather than technical. Nobody knows who owns a secret. Nobody knows what reads it. Nobody can delete anything because nobody can prove it is unused. The bill becomes visible enough that someone proposes going back to environment variables. And a platform team has to decide, defensibly, whether Secrets Manager is even the right tool for every workload. This topic is the one that separates an engineer who can <em>use</em> the service from one who can <em>own</em> it across an organisation.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Secret Sprawl and How It Happens</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Sprawl is not carelessness; it is the predictable result of a system with easy creation and no lifecycle. Every team creates secrets freely because that is the correct behaviour we asked for. Nothing ever forces a secret to be reviewed, re-owned, or deleted. The result, reliably, at around the two-year mark:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>No owner.</strong> The person who created it left. The team was reorganised. The tag says the name of a team that no longer exists. When rotation breaks at 3am, the page goes nowhere.</li>\n<li><strong>No known consumers.</strong> Nobody can say what would break if it were rotated or deleted, so nothing is ever rotated or deleted. Fear of the unknown consumer is the single biggest blocker to rotation adoption in real organisations.</li>\n<li><strong>No expiry.</strong> Secrets created for a two-week proof of concept in 2023 are still there, still billed, still readable, still part of the blast radius of any over-broad IAM policy.</li>\n<li><strong>Inconsistent naming.</strong> <code>prod-db</code>, <code>ProdDatabase</code>, <code>db_prod_new</code>, <code>db_prod_new_v2_FINAL</code>. Wildcard IAM policies become impossible to write safely, which pushes teams toward <code>Resource: \"*\"</code>, which destroys least privilege — <strong>naming chaos causes authorisation chaos</strong>, and that is the real cost.</li>\n<li><strong>Duplication.</strong> The same database password stored three times by three teams. Rotation now silently breaks two of them, so nobody rotates.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The remedies are boring and they work. They must be <em>enforced</em>, because a convention that is merely documented has a compliance rate that decays to zero.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>A mandatory naming taxonomy.</strong> Hierarchical, slash-delimited, machine-parseable: <code>environment/team/application/purpose</code>, for example <code>prod/payments/checkout-api/stripe-key</code>. The payoff is enormous and often understated: a hierarchical path makes <strong>prefix-based IAM policies</strong> both possible and safe — <code>arn:aws:secretsmanager:...:secret:prod/payments/*</code> grants a team exactly its own production secrets, so least privilege becomes a one-line policy instead of an ever-growing list of ARNs. Environment must come first, so that production can be separated from everything else by prefix.</li>\n<li><strong>Required tags enforced by policy.</strong> At minimum: <code>Owner</code> (a team, never a person — people leave), <code>Environment</code>, <code>Application</code>, <code>DataClassification</code>, and <code>CostCenter</code>. Enforce with an SCP or IAM condition that denies <code>secretsmanager:CreateSecret</code> unless the required tag keys are present via <code>aws:RequestTag</code>, and use <strong>AWS Organizations tag policies</strong> to constrain allowed values so <code>Owner</code> cannot be free text. Enforcement at creation time is the only approach that works, because retrofitting tags onto thousands of existing secrets is a project nobody funds.</li>\n<li><strong>Ownership that survives reorganisation.</strong> Tag a team identifier that maps to a rota or a group, not an individual. Reconcile tags against your service catalogue on a schedule and flag orphans — a secret whose <code>Owner</code> tag names a team that no longer exists is an incident waiting to happen.</li>\n<li><strong>Periodic review driven by data, not by nagging.</strong> <code>DescribeSecret</code> returns <strong><code>LastAccessedDate</code></strong>, and the AWS Config rule <code>secretsmanager-secret-unused</code> operationalises it. Anything not accessed in 90 days is a candidate for deletion. The safe workflow is to <strong>tag, notify the owner, wait, then schedule deletion with a recovery window</strong> rather than force-deleting — the recovery window exists precisely so that a wrong decision is survivable. Note the caveat: <code>LastAccessedDate</code> is day-granular and reflects retrieval, so a secret consumed once a quarter looks dead for most of the quarter. Use a window long enough to cover your longest legitimate cycle.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Structure: One Secret Per Credential, or One JSON Blob?</h4>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; margin-top: 0.75rem\">\n<table style=\"width: 100%; border-collapse: collapse; font-size: 0.92rem\">\n<thead><tr style=\"text-align: left\" data-box=\"1\"><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Dimension</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">One secret per credential</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">One JSON blob with many values</th></tr></thead>\n<tbody>\n<tr><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Cost</td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">A monthly charge per secret — 50 credentials means 50 charges</td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">One charge, one API call. A secret containing your database password <em>and</em> your Stripe key <em>and</em> your SendGrid key is wrong, because those three have different owners, different rotation cadences, different sensitivity, and no reason to share an access-control boundary.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The reasoning to voice in an interview is that <strong>the cost saving from consolidation is a few dollars a month, while the cost of a coarse blast radius is unbounded</strong>. You are trading a rounding error for a security property. The one legitimate exception is a large population of genuinely low-sensitivity, always-read-together values where per-secret charges become real money at thousands of items — and even then, the better answer is usually that those values are <em>configuration, not secrets</em>, and belong in Parameter Store.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Migration Into Secrets Manager</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Migrations fail for organisational reasons, not technical ones. Run it as a programme with five phases:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>1. Inventory and discovery.</strong> You cannot migrate what you cannot see. Scan repositories with gitleaks or trufflehog <em>including full history</em>; scan CI/CD variable stores, Kubernetes Secret objects, ECS task definition environment blocks, Terraform state files (a notorious plaintext reservoir), AMIs and container image layers, configuration management repositories, and wiki pages. Expect the inventory to be two to three times larger than anyone predicted, and expect to find credentials for systems nobody still supports.</li>\n<li><strong>2. Prioritise by sensitivity times blast radius.</strong> Not alphabetically and not by ease. Production database credentials, payment-provider keys and anything granting write access to customer data go first. A read-only staging key goes last. Being explicit that <em>priority is risk-ordered</em> is what makes this sound like an engineer who has run a migration rather than one who has planned one.</li>\n<li><strong>3. Dual-read during transition.</strong> The pattern that makes migration safe: application code tries Secrets Manager first and falls back to the existing environment variable or config file if the secret is absent, logging which path it took. This decouples the code change from the data move, lets you deploy the new code with zero risk, migrate secrets one at a time, and watch the fallback-path metric fall to zero. <strong>The fallback must be removed once that metric reaches zero</strong> — a dual-read left in place forever is a permanent unaudited backdoor, and the cleanup step is the one teams skip.</li>\n<li><strong>4. Cutover and cleanup.</strong> Remove the old value from the environment variable, the config file, the CI variable and the image layer. Verify with the same scanners that found it. A migration that leaves the plaintext in place has added cost without removing risk.</li>\n<li><strong>5. Rotate everything you migrated.</strong> This is the rule that matters most and is most often skipped. <strong>A migrated secret's exposure history is unknown</strong> — it lived in a config file, an image layer, a wiki page, and a dozen laptops for years. Copying it into Secrets Manager does not make it clean; it makes it a known-exposed value in a nicer container. Treat the end of migration as a mandatory rotation event for every credential moved, and you convert an unknown risk into a known-good state. It is also a natural forcing function for building the rotation functions you will need anyway.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two source-specific notes. Migrating from <strong>Parameter Store</strong> is often <em>not</em> worth doing wholesale — SecureString parameters are already encrypted, IAM-controlled and audited, so migrate only the values that need <strong>managed rotation, cross-account resource policies, or multi-Region replication</strong>, and leave the rest. Migrating from <strong>HashiCorp Vault</strong> is a bigger decision than a data move: if you are using Vault's dynamic short-lived credentials, Secrets Manager has no direct equivalent, and migrating means giving up a genuinely stronger security model for operational simplicity. Say that out loud rather than pretending the services are interchangeable.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cost at Scale — and the Honest Framing</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Secrets Manager pricing has a small number of components, and you should be able to name them and their levers without hedging:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>A per-secret monthly charge</strong>, prorated by the hour — around 0.40 USD per secret per month at list price. This is the component that dominates for organisations with many secrets and low call volume.</li>\n<li><strong>A per-API-call charge</strong>, around 0.05 USD per 10,000 calls. Trivial when you cache; the component that dominates for organisations that fetch per request.</li>\n<li><strong>Replicas are billed as separate secrets.</strong> A secret replicated to three additional Regions costs roughly four times the monthly charge. This surprises people who enable multi-Region replication organisation-wide as a default.</li>\n<li><strong>KMS charges ride alongside.</strong> A customer managed key carries its own monthly charge, plus request charges for the decrypt operations that every uncached retrieval generates. Uncached retrieval therefore costs you twice.</li>\n<li><strong>Deleted-but-not-yet-destroyed secrets in the recovery window</strong> still exist. Deleting is not instantaneous cost relief.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The levers, in order of effect: <strong>cache</strong> (kills the per-call and KMS request components outright); <strong>delete dead secrets</strong> found via <code>LastAccessedDate</code> (the biggest lever at high secret counts, and it improves security posture at the same time); <strong>be deliberate about replicas</strong> rather than replicating everything by default; <strong>consolidate only where values genuinely belong to one credential</strong>; and <strong>use Parameter Store for things that are not secrets</strong> — feature flags, endpoint URLs, timeouts, bucket names. That last one is frequently the single biggest saving available, because a large fraction of what teams put in Secrets Manager is configuration that was never sensitive.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Then the honest framing, which an interviewer is specifically listening for: <strong>Secrets Manager cost is almost always negligible next to the cost of a breach.</strong> A thousand secrets is on the order of a few hundred dollars a month — less than one engineer-day. A single leaked production credential can mean an incident, a regulatory notification, and reputational damage measured in orders of magnitude more. So optimise cost through caching and deleting dead secrets because those are also good engineering, but <strong>never let cost drive a team back to environment variables or a config file</strong>. If cost is genuinely the objection, the right answer is Parameter Store for non-secrets and a conversation about what is actually sensitive — not abandoning managed secret storage.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; First, <strong>these are not mutually exclusive</strong> — a very common and entirely defensible end state is Parameter Store for configuration, Secrets Manager for AWS-native credentials that need rotation, and Vault only for the specific workloads that need dynamic credentials or cross-cloud reach. Second, <strong>\"build\" is almost never the right answer</strong>. Teams occasionally propose encrypting values with KMS and storing them in DynamoDB or S3 themselves. That is a weekend to build and a decade to operate: you inherit versioning, staging labels, rotation orchestration, cross-account access, audit, replication and a tier-0 availability obligation, in order to save a few hundred dollars a month. Recognising that the interesting decision is <em>which managed option</em>, not <em>whether to build</em>, is itself the senior answer.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Strategic End State: The Best Secret Is the One That Never Existed</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Everything above manages long-lived secrets well. The strategically superior move is to have fewer of them at all. A secret that does not exist cannot leak, cannot be committed, cannot need rotation, cannot be audited, and cannot be billed. Wherever a workload authenticates to something that supports identity federation, <strong>eliminate the credential rather than storing it</strong>:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>IAM roles for AWS service-to-service access.</strong> An EC2 instance profile, an ECS task role or a Lambda execution role gives short-lived, auto-rotated credentials with no stored value anywhere. Any team still storing IAM access keys in Secrets Manager for in-AWS workloads has the wrong architecture, and saying so is a strong signal.</li>\n<li><strong>IAM Roles for Service Accounts (IRSA), or EKS Pod Identity, on Kubernetes.</strong> A pod assumes an AWS role via its service account and an OIDC trust relationship. No Kubernetes Secret object holding AWS keys, no rotation to manage.</li>\n<li><strong>OIDC federation from CI/CD.</strong> GitHub Actions, GitLab and others can present an OIDC token that an IAM role trusts, with conditions scoping trust to a specific repository, branch or environment. This removes the single most commonly leaked class of long-lived credential in the industry — CI access keys — and it is often the highest-leverage change a platform team can make.</li>\n<li><strong>IAM database authentication</strong> for RDS and Aurora, where an IAM principal obtains a short-lived auth token instead of using a stored password — eliminating the database secret entirely for supported engines.</li>\n<li><strong>IAM Roles Anywhere</strong> for on-premises or other-cloud workloads, using X.509 certificates from your own CA to obtain temporary AWS credentials, extending the same model outside AWS.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">What genuinely remains after all of that: third-party SaaS API keys where the vendor supports nothing better, credentials for legacy systems that cannot federate, database engines without IAM auth, encryption keys and signing material, and partner integrations. That residue is what Secrets Manager should hold — and it is a much smaller, much more governable set than most organisations start with. <strong>The mature target is not \"all our secrets are in Secrets Manager\"; it is \"we have as few secrets as physically possible, and the ones that must exist are managed, rotated and audited.\"</strong></p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Worked Scenario 1: Fintech, 40 Services, One Region, Strict Audit</h4>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">CONSTRAINTS\n  <i class=o>-</i> AWS <i class=k>only</i>; <i class=o>~</i><i class=n>40</i> microservices <i class=k>on</i> EKS; <i class=k>one</i> <i class=k>primary</i> Region\n  <i class=o>-</i> Regulated: must evidence rotation <i class=k>on</i> schedule <i class=o>+</i> <i class=k>full</i> access audit\n  <i class=o>-</i> Small platform team (<i class=n>3</i> engineers), <i class=k>no</i> appetite <i class=k>for</i> tier<i class=n>-0</i> ops\n  <i class=o>-</i> Aurora PostgreSQL, plus a payment provider <i class=k>and</i> a KYC vendor API\n\nDECISION: Secrets Manager <i class=k>as</i> the <i class=k>primary</i> store.
