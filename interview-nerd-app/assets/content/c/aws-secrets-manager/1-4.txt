{"title":"KMS Key Strategy for Secrets at Scale","tags":["kms","encryption","key-policy","secrets-manager"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Every secret in Secrets Manager is encrypted with envelope encryption. When you write a value, Secrets Manager calls <code>kms:GenerateDataKey</code> against the key associated with the secret, encrypts the plaintext with the returned data key, stores the encrypted data key alongside the ciphertext, and discards the plaintext data key. When you read the value, Secrets Manager calls <code>kms:Decrypt</code> to unwrap that data key. This means the KMS key is not a checkbox on a form: it is a second, independent authorization system sitting behind every read of every secret, and at senior level the interesting questions are all about how you design that second system across dozens of teams and thousands of secrets.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">AWS managed key versus customer managed key</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">If you create a secret and say nothing about encryption, Secrets Manager uses the AWS managed key with the alias <code>aws/secretsmanager</code>, created in your account on first use. It costs nothing, AWS rotates its key material automatically on a yearly cadence, and it requires zero operational work. Its limitation is that you do not control its key policy. You cannot edit it, you cannot disable the key, you cannot schedule it for deletion, and you cannot name an external principal in it. Any principal in the account with <code>secretsmanager:GetSecretValue</code> on the secret effectively passes the KMS layer, because the AWS managed key's policy permits use by the account through Secrets Manager.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A customer managed key (CMK) costs roughly one dollar per month per key plus per-request charges, and it hands you four capabilities that genuinely force the choice:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Cross-account access.</strong> A secret shared with another account can only be decrypted if the external principal is allowed in the key policy. The AWS managed key cannot express that, so cross-account sharing mandates a CMK.</li>\n<li><strong>Instant revocation.</strong> Disabling the key, or removing a statement from the key policy, stops every decrypt immediately, across every principal, without touching a single secret or IAM policy. This is the break-glass control auditors ask about.</li>\n<li><strong>Rotation control.</strong> You decide whether key material rotates, and on what schedule, which matters when a compliance regime specifies an interval or when you must prove the schedule.</li>\n<li><strong>A second authorization layer.</strong> Secrets Manager permissions and KMS permissions can be owned by different teams. A developer who is granted <code>GetSecretValue</code> by an application team still cannot read the value unless the security team's key policy allows <code>kms:Decrypt</code>. Separation of duties becomes enforceable rather than procedural.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Key policy versus IAM policy: the mechanics people get wrong</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">KMS is unusual among AWS services: the key policy is the root of authority for the key. An IAM policy granting <code>kms:Decrypt</code> on a key ARN grants nothing on its own. The key policy must first delegate authority to the account, which is what the default statement does when it names the account root principal and allows <code>kms:*</code> on the key. That statement does not give everyone access; it says \"IAM policies in this account are allowed to speak for this key.\" Once that delegation exists, the effective permission is the intersection: the principal needs an allow in its IAM policy and the key policy must not deny it.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">If you remove the account delegation and instead enumerate principals directly in the key policy, IAM policies become irrelevant for that key and the key policy alone decides. Many security teams deliberately do this for the highest-classification keys, because it puts the entire access list in one auditable document. The trade-off is operational: every new consumer requires a key policy edit by whoever owns the key, and a key policy has a size limit, so enumerating hundreds of roles does not scale. The common middle ground is to keep the account delegation, restrict who in the account can attach KMS permissions via a permissions boundary, and add a condition on the key policy that the call must arrive through Secrets Manager using <code>kms:ViaService</code> set to the regional Secrets Manager endpoint. That condition means a principal cannot use the key to decrypt arbitrary ciphertext of its own choosing; the key becomes usable only as part of a secret read.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Grants as the dynamic alternative</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Grants are the third mechanism, designed for programmatic and temporary delegation. A grant is created with <code>kms:CreateGrant</code>, names a grantee principal, a specific list of operations, and optional constraints, and can be retired by the creator or revoked by the key owner. Grants are additive to the key policy and do not require editing it, which is why AWS services themselves use grants extensively. The two properties that matter in interviews are that grants can carry <strong>encryption context constraints</strong> (the grant is only usable when the request carries a matching context) and that they are individually revocable, so a workflow can hand out a narrow, short-lived capability and take it back without a policy change. Grants are eventually consistent on creation, which is why <code>CreateGrant</code> returns a grant token you can pass to the immediately following operation.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Encryption context: constraining a key to a single secret</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Secrets Manager passes an encryption context to KMS on every encrypt and decrypt containing two entries: <code>SecretARN</code> with the ARN of the secret and <code>SecretVersionId</code> with the version identifier. Encryption context is additional authenticated data. It is not secret, it is logged in CloudTrail, and crucially it can be matched in key policy conditions with <code>kms:EncryptionContext:SecretARN</code>.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">That gives you a genuinely advanced control. You can share one CMK across several secrets but write a key policy statement that only allows a particular role to decrypt when the encryption context names one specific secret ARN. The role may hold a broad IAM grant, but the KMS layer will refuse any other secret encrypted under the same key. This is the cleanest way to give a partner or a narrow workload access to exactly one secret without provisioning a dedicated key for it, and it is also how you make CloudTrail readable: the KMS decrypt event carries the secret ARN, so you can audit which secret a decrypt belonged to without correlating across two services.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Segmentation strategy: how many keys?</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The four common axes are environment, team, data classification, and application. If your incident playbook says \"cut off all production access for team X,\" then per team plus per environment is the right grain. Going finer buys little because you rarely revoke at application grain, while every extra key adds a monthly charge, a policy to review, a rotation setting to track, and another object in the migration path when standards change. Encryption context conditions let you get per-secret precision without per-secret keys, which is usually the better trade.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Multi-Region keys and replicated secrets</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A KMS key is a regional resource, and ciphertext produced in one Region cannot be decrypted by a key in another. When you replicate a secret to a second Region, Secrets Manager decrypts in the primary Region and re-encrypts in the replica Region with a key that lives there; by default that is the replica Region's <code>aws/secretsmanager</code> key, or a CMK you nominate per Region.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Multi-Region KMS keys share key material across Regions: you create a primary key and replicate it, and the replicas share the same key material and the same key ID suffix, so ciphertext encrypted by one can be decrypted by another. Each replica is still an independent resource with its own key policy, its own grants, and its own enable/disable state. For replicated secrets this simplifies the policy story, because you can write a single key ARN pattern in automation and reason about one logical key, and it removes the failure case where a Region-local key is missing during failover. The counter-argument is blast radius: shared key material means compromise of the material is global, and a mistaken policy template pushed to all replicas affects all Regions. For most platforms, distinct per-Region keys with identical generated policies are perfectly adequate and slightly safer; multi-Region keys earn their place when the ciphertext itself moves between Regions outside Secrets Manager, or when a disaster-recovery runbook must not depend on a Region-specific key existing.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Operational hazards</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Disabling a key is an instant outage.</strong> Every <code>GetSecretValue</code> against a secret encrypted with that key fails with a KMS invalid-state error. There is no grace period and no fallback. Clients holding cached plaintext keep working, which makes the incident look intermittent and staggered as caches expire.</li>\n<li><strong>Scheduling deletion is worse.</strong> The key enters pending-deletion state immediately and becomes unusable at that moment, not at the end of the window. The 7-to-30-day window exists so you can cancel deletion and recover, not so you can keep using the key. After the window, ciphertext encrypted under that key is unrecoverable and the secret's value is gone permanently even though the secret metadata still exists.</li>\n<li><strong>Discovery is a real problem.</strong> Before touching a key you must know which secrets depend on it. <code>ListSecrets</code> returns a KMS key identifier per secret, AWS Config can record the association, and CloudTrail decrypt events carry the secret ARN in the encryption context. Build that inventory before a key is ever disabled, and require it as a precondition in the deletion runbook.</li>\n<li><strong>Migration is not instantaneous.</strong> Changing a secret's key with <code>UpdateSecret</code> re-encrypts the versions carrying staging labels, but older unlabeled versions remain encrypted under the previous key. The safe migration is: create the new key, grant the same principals, update the secret to the new key, write a new version to force fresh ciphertext, verify reads, then keep the old key enabled through a soak period before disabling it, and only schedule deletion once nothing has used it for the full window.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cost modelling and why caching is a KMS decision</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Customer managed keys carry a small monthly charge per key and a per-request charge for cryptographic operations, with a modest monthly free allowance. The monthly key charge is almost never the problem; the request charge at scale is. Every uncached <code>GetSecretValue</code> against a CMK-encrypted secret produces a KMS <code>Decrypt</code> call, so you pay twice: once to Secrets Manager per ten thousand API calls, once to KMS per ten thousand cryptographic requests.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Work the arithmetic in the interview rather than hand-waving. Two thousand containers each fetching one secret every thirty seconds is roughly 5.8 million requests per month to Secrets Manager and the same to KMS. Introduce an in-process cache with a five-minute refresh and the same fleet makes about 580,000 calls, a ten-fold reduction on both bills, and the tail latency of the hot path drops from a network round trip to a memory read. Cache for an hour and it is roughly 96,000 calls. The cache is therefore not only a resilience measure against throttling and a regional API blip, it is the primary cost lever, and it is the reason the AWS-provided caching libraries exist. The counterweight is rotation: a long TTL means stale credentials after a rotation, so the correct pattern is a moderate TTL combined with an on-authentication-failure refresh, so the cache self-heals within one failed connection rather than waiting for expiry.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Production scenario: key strategy for a regulated multi-team platform</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A fintech platform runs forty microservices owned by eight teams, across separate development, staging and production accounts, in two Regions, and is subject to an audit scope that covers only the services touching cardholder data. The design I would defend: accounts remain the primary boundary, so development and production secrets can never be confused. Inside each production account, provision one CMK per team plus a separate CMK for the cardholder-data classification, giving nine keys per account rather than forty. Every key policy keeps the account delegation statement but adds a <code>kms:ViaService</code> condition pinning use to Secrets Manager, so the keys cannot be repurposed for arbitrary decryption. The cardholder key drops the account delegation entirely and enumerates three roles explicitly, which makes its access list a single auditable document and keeps the audit scope small. Key administration lives with the security team through a permissions boundary that prevents application roles from calling <code>PutKeyPolicy</code>, <code>ScheduleKeyDeletion</code> or <code>CreateGrant</code>. Keys are created by the same Terraform module that creates secrets, so tags and policies are generated, never hand-written. For the two Regions, use per-Region keys generated by the same module rather than multi-Region keys, because no ciphertext crosses Regions outside Secrets Manager replication. Finally, every service uses the caching client with a fifteen-minute TTL and a refresh-on-auth-failure hook, which holds KMS spend to a rounding error and gives the platform a clean answer for the quarterly review: revocation is one key-policy edit per team, recoverable, logged, and blast-radius bounded to that team.</p>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"The AWS managed key is fine until you need one of four things: cross-account sharing, instant revocation, control of rotation, or a second authorization layer owned by a different team.
