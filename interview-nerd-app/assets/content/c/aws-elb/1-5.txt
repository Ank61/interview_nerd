{"title":"Gateway Load Balancer and Transparent Traffic Inspection","tags":["gwlb","geneve","network-inspection","transit-gateway"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">The Gateway Load Balancer is the least understood member of the ELB family and therefore one of the best differentiators in a senior networking interview. It exists to solve a single, specific, genuinely hard problem: how do you insert a fleet of third-party network appliances — next-generation firewalls, intrusion detection and prevention systems, deep packet inspection, data loss prevention — into the traffic path of a VPC so that traffic is inspected, without the appliance behaving as a NAT or a proxy that rewrites addresses, without losing the original source and destination, without single points of failure, and without hand-building a nest of fragile route hairpins that break the moment an appliance instance is replaced. GWLB is the answer, and the mechanism is a transparent layer 3 bump in the wire built on GENEVE encapsulation.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The problem before GWLB existed</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Before GWLB, putting an appliance fleet in the path meant one of a few bad options. You could make the appliance a NAT gateway or a proxy, which works but mangles the addressing — the server behind it sees the appliance's address rather than the client's, breaking source-based rules, logging, and geo policy, and forcing you to reinvent client IP propagation. You could put appliances behind an NLB and route to it, but an NLB is a flow router to a destination service, not a pass-through: traffic sent to it is destined <em>for</em> the target, not destined for somewhere else and merely passing through, so return traffic has nowhere natural to go. Or you could hand-craft route tables pointing at individual appliance ENIs, with a Lambda function watching health and rewriting routes on failure. That last pattern was widely deployed and widely hated: route updates are slow, the blast radius of a bad update is a VPC, scaling is manual, and asymmetric routing silently breaks stateful inspection.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">GWLB replaces all of that with a managed construct that is simultaneously a load balancer and a route target, and that preserves the original packet byte for byte.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Mechanics: a layer 3 bump in the wire over GENEVE</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A GWLB operates at layer 3. It does not terminate connections, does not have a listener with a protocol and port in the usual sense, and does not know or care whether the traffic is TCP, UDP, ICMP, or something exotic. Its listener forwards all IP traffic to a target group whose protocol is <strong>GENEVE</strong> on port <strong>6081</strong>. When a packet arrives, the GWLB selects an appliance target by flow hash, wraps the entire original IP packet — original source address, original destination address, original ports, payload, everything — inside a GENEVE tunnel and sends it to the appliance. The appliance decapsulates, inspects, and then either drops the packet (blocking the flow) or re-encapsulates it and returns it to the GWLB, which strips the tunnel and forwards the original packet onward to its original destination.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>GENEVE (RFC 8926).</strong> A general-purpose network virtualization encapsulation with an extensible type-length-value option space. AWS uses those options to pass metadata — notably a flow cookie and the GWLB endpoint identifier — that the appliance must echo back on the return packet so the GWLB can correlate it with the original flow.</li>\n<li><strong>Appliances must be GENEVE-aware.</strong> This is not optional and not something you can bolt on with a security group. The appliance software has to terminate GENEVE on UDP 6081, handle the AWS TLV options, and re-encapsulate. Every major vendor ships a GWLB-compatible image in the Marketplace; open-source setups typically use a Linux kernel GENEVE interface plus iptables or Suricata, or an eBPF program.</li>\n<li><strong>Transparency.</strong> Because the inner packet is untouched, the destination server sees the true client address. No NAT, no proxy, no <code>X-Forwarded-For</code> gymnastics, and existing source-based firewall rules and logs keep working.</li>\n<li><strong>Bidirectional and protocol-agnostic.</strong> Both directions of a flow can be steered through the same appliance, and non-TCP traffic works identically, which matters for ICMP diagnostics and for UDP-heavy workloads.</li>\n<li><strong>MTU.</strong> The GENEVE header adds overhead on the tunnel segment. GWLB supports large packets on the inner path, but you must not assume the appliance's tunnel interface has room to spare — size the appliance interface MTU for the encapsulation and avoid relying on fragmentation, which the data path does not reassemble for you.</li>\n</ul>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" A GWLB is exposed through a <strong>VPC endpoint service</strong>, exactly like an NLB-backed PrivateLink service, but consumers create a VPC endpoint of the <strong>Gateway Load Balancer</strong> type rather than an interface endpoint. A GWLB endpoint (often written GWLBE) is unusual in two ways: it has no DNS name and nothing ever connects to it by address; instead it is a <strong>route target</strong>. You put the endpoint's <code>vpce-</code> identifier in a route table entry as the destination gateway, and the VPC data plane silently diverts matching traffic through the endpoint, into the GWLB, out to an appliance, back, and on to the original destination.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Because the endpoint is a PrivateLink construct, the appliance fleet can live in a completely different VPC and a different AWS account from the workloads being inspected. That is the enabling property for centralized inspection: a security team owns an inspection VPC with the appliances and the GWLB, publishes it as an endpoint service, and application teams merely place an endpoint in their own VPC and add a route. No peering, no CIDR coordination, no shared route tables.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">NORTH<i class=o>-</i>SOUTH (egress) INSPECTION INSIDE <i class=k>ONE</i> VPC <i class=c>-- the two-route trick</i>\n\n               Internet\n                  <i class=o>^</i>\n                  <i class=o>|</i>\n            <i class=o>+</i><i class=c>-----------+   IGW route table (&quot;edge association&quot;):</i>\n            <i class=o>|</i>    IGW    <i class=o>|</i>     <i class=n>10.0</i><i class=n>.1</i><i class=n>.0</i><i class=o>/</i><i class=n>24</i> <i class=o>-</i><i class=o>&gt;</i> vpce<i class=o>-</i>gwlbe   (ingress <i class=k>return</i> path)\n            <i class=o>+</i><i class=c>-----------+</i>\n                  <i class=o>^</i>\n    public subnet <i class=o>|</i>            public subnet route <i class=k>table</i>:\n            <i class=o>+</i><i class=c>-----------+        0.0.0.0/0     -&gt; One setting makes or breaks it: <strong>appliance mode</strong> on the inspection VPC's TGW attachment. Without it, the TGW may choose a different Availability Zone for each direction of a flow, so the forward path is inspected by an appliance in one zone and the return path by an appliance in another — asymmetric routing, which any stateful firewall will reject as an unsolicited packet. The coarser the tuple, the worse the load distribution — a 2-tuple setting concentrates all traffic between a busy client and a busy server on a single appliance, so choose the finest tuple your appliance's state model tolerates. There are also target group attributes controlling <strong>target failover</strong> behavior: whether existing flows are rebalanced to a different appliance when a target is deregistered or becomes unhealthy, or whether they are left pinned (and therefore dropped). Rebalancing recovers the flow but hands it to an appliance with no state for it, which a strict stateful firewall will reject; leaving it pinned means the flow dies and the client retries. Which you prefer depends on whether your appliance can adopt mid-stream flows.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Health checks, appliance failure, and scaling the fleet</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">GWLB health checks do not run over GENEVE. You configure a conventional TCP, HTTP, or HTTPS check against a separate port on the appliance — vendors expose a dedicated health endpoint for this — and the GWLB uses the result to decide whether to hash new flows to that target. That creates a subtle risk: the health endpoint can be up while the GENEVE data path on the appliance is broken (a crashed tunnel process, a mis-set MTU, a dropped TLV), in which case the appliance is considered healthy and silently blackholes every flow assigned to it. Synthetic end-to-end probes that traverse the inspection path are the only reliable detection, and they are a standard part of a good GWLB design.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">On failure, new flows stop going to the unhealthy appliance within interval multiplied by unhealthy threshold, and existing flows are rebalanced or dropped according to the failover attributes. If <strong>every</strong> appliance in a zone is unhealthy, traffic routed to that zone's endpoint has nowhere to go and is dropped — inspection is fail-closed by nature, so an appliance fleet outage is a connectivity outage. This is the central availability trade-off of inline inspection and must be stated explicitly in a design answer: you have deliberately inserted a dependency into the data path. Mitigations are running the appliances in an Auto Scaling group across at least three zones with generous headroom, scaling on the appliances' own CPU and connection-table metrics rather than on GWLB metrics alone, pre-baking and pre-warming images because commercial firewall instances boot slowly, and having a documented, tested break-glass procedure that removes the endpoint routes to restore connectivity without inspection.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Cost and latency are the other honest trade-offs. You pay GWLB hours plus capacity units on the load balancer, endpoint hours plus data processing for every GWLB endpoint, the appliance compute and licensing (usually the dominant line item by a wide margin), and, in the centralized model, Transit Gateway attachment hours and per-gigabyte processing — with traffic frequently traversing the TGW more than once per flow. Latency adds up as well: the encapsulation round trip plus the appliance's own inspection time, typically low single-digit milliseconds for simple filtering and materially more for TLS interception or full deep packet inspection.
