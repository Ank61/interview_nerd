{"title":"ELB Cost Optimization and Consolidation","tags":["cost-optimization","lcu","cross-az-data-transfer","consolidation"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">The traffic tier is a classic source of quiet cost sprawl. No single load balancer looks expensive, so nobody reviews them; then a platform team discovers four hundred load balancers across their accounts, half of them serving almost no traffic, and a bill with several distinct components that nobody can attribute. Optimizing this well requires knowing precisely <strong>what is billed</strong>, <strong>which dimension is dominating in your case</strong>, and which levers are safe. Prices change and vary by Region, so the durable skill is the structure of the bill and the diagnosis method — not memorized numbers.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">What You Actually Pay For</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>An hourly charge per load balancer.</strong> Every ALB, NLB and GWLB accrues an hourly charge for every hour it exists, whether it serves a single request or none at all. This is the <strong>floor cost</strong> and it is the reason load balancer sprawl is expensive: a hundred idle load balancers cost real money per month before any traffic. It is also why &quot;one ALB per microservice&quot; is a cost decision, not just an architectural one.</li>\n<li><strong>LCU (ALB) or NLCU (NLB) hours.</strong> Usage is measured across several dimensions and you are billed on the <strong>maximum</strong> of them for each hour — not the sum. For ALB the dimensions are <em>new connections</em> per second, <em>active connections</em> per minute, <em>processed bytes</em> per hour, and <em>rule evaluations</em> per second. For NLB they are <em>new flows</em>, <em>active flows</em> and <em>processed bytes</em>, with separate dimension definitions for TCP, UDP and TLS traffic (TLS listeners consume more because NLB does the cryptographic work). GWLB has its own analogous unit. The &quot;maximum, not sum&quot; rule is the single most important mechanic: it means <strong>one dimension sets your bill</strong>, and optimizing any other dimension changes nothing. Find the dominant one before doing any work.</li>\n<li><strong>Cross-Availability-Zone data transfer.</strong> This is where the load balancer types genuinely differ and where most surprises live. For <strong>ALB</strong>, traffic between the load balancer's zonal nodes and targets in other zones is <em>not</em> charged, which is why cross-zone load balancing is on by default at the load balancer level and why leaving it on is usually right. For <strong>NLB</strong>, cross-zone load balancing is off by default and cross-zone traffic <em>is</em> charged as inter-AZ data transfer in both directions. So enabling cross-zone on an NLB carrying heavy traffic can add a meaningful, entirely invisible line item. Separately, your own application's cross-zone chatter — targets calling databases or caches in other zones — is charged normally and is often larger than anything the load balancer contributes.</li>\n<li><strong>Data transfer out.</strong> Bytes leaving to the internet through the load balancer are billed at standard EC2 data transfer out rates, in addition to being counted in the processed-bytes LCU dimension. For byte-heavy workloads this frequently dwarfs the load balancer charges themselves, which is the economic argument for CloudFront: egress from CloudFront is generally priced lower than from EC2, and cached responses avoid origin egress entirely.</li>\n<li><strong>Access log and connection log storage.</strong> S3 storage plus requests. Individually small, but a high-traffic load balancer with logs retained forever and no lifecycle policy becomes a standing cost, and Athena queries over unpartitioned data add scan charges on top.</li>\n<li><strong>AWS WAF.</strong> Charged per web ACL per month, per rule, and <strong>per million requests inspected</strong>, with extra charges for Bot Control, fraud prevention and CAPTCHA. At high request volume the request-inspection component dominates, which is a real argument for inspecting at CloudFront where cache hits never reach the ALB's web ACL — and for scoping expensive rule groups to the paths that need them rather than all traffic.</li>\n<li><strong>Global Accelerator.</strong> A fixed hourly charge per accelerator plus a <strong>data transfer premium</strong> based on the dominant direction of traffic and the source and destination Regions, layered on top of normal data transfer. It is not a cheap add-on; it is bought for static anycast IPs, fast failover and edge-network latency, and the premium should appear in the design discussion explicitly.</li>\n<li><strong>Adjacent charges.</strong> Certificates from ACM for use with ELB are free; private CA and certain other services are not. PrivateLink adds endpoint-hour and data-processing charges on the consumer side. Route 53 hosted zones and queries, and health checks, are small but non-zero.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Diagnosis: Find the Dominant Dimension Before Optimizing</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The method has three steps and should take under an hour.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step 1 — Cost Explorer with usage types.</strong> Filter to the Elastic Load Balancing service, group by <strong>usage type</strong>, and you immediately see the split between load-balancer-hours, LCU-hours and data transfer usage types. Grouping by <strong>resource</strong> (where resource-level data is enabled) or by tag tells you which load balancers and which teams. A bill dominated by <em>hours</em> means you have a sprawl problem and consolidation is the lever. A bill dominated by <em>LCU-hours</em> means one usage dimension is high and you need the next step. A bill dominated by <em>data transfer</em> means caching, compression and payload size are the levers, not load balancer configuration. Enable the Cost and Usage Report for anything more detailed, and use Athena over it when you need per-resource, per-hour attribution.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step 2 — ConsumedLCUs plus the underlying metrics.</strong> <code>ConsumedLCUs</code> tells you the magnitude; it does not tell you which dimension produced it. Graph it beside <code>NewConnectionCount</code>, <code>ActiveConnectionCount</code>, <code>ProcessedBytes</code> and <code>RuleEvaluations</code> (and for NLB, <code>NewFlowCount</code> and <code>ActiveFlowCount</code>), each converted into its LCU-equivalent, and the dominant line is visible immediately. This is the step people skip, and skipping it is why teams spend a sprint reducing payload sizes when their bill was driven by connection churn.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step 3 — Look for the surprising cases.</strong> Several patterns recur:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>An idle load balancer per microservice.</strong> Forty services, forty ALBs, each serving a trickle of internal traffic. Every one pays the full hourly floor. The LCU consumption is negligible; the hours are the entire bill.</li>\n<li><strong>Per-request connection churn.</strong> A client that disables keep-alive, or a poorly configured SDK or health checker, opens a new TCP and TLS connection per request. Because the <em>new connections</em> dimension is measured per second at a far smaller unit than processed bytes, connection churn can make the new-connection dimension dominant on a workload that moves almost no data — and it simultaneously raises latency. The diagnostic is the <code>RequestCount / NewConnectionCount</code> ratio: near 1 means no reuse.</li>\n<li><strong>A chatty health check.</strong> A 5-second interval across a large fleet with many target groups generates a constant stream of connections and requests, and on ALB each request also incurs rule evaluations. It rarely dominates alone, but on an otherwise idle internal load balancer the health checks can be most of the measured usage — an amusing and genuinely common finding.</li>\n<li><strong>Rule evaluation growth after consolidation.</strong> Consolidating onto one ALB with a hundred listener rules means every request may be evaluated against many rules. <code>RuleEvaluations</code> is a billed dimension, so ordering high-traffic rules at low priority numbers is both a latency and a cost optimization.</li>\n<li><strong>Orphaned load balancers.</strong> Deleted CloudFormation or Terraform stacks that left a load balancer behind (often because deletion protection was on, or because it was created outside the stack), abandoned test environments, and load balancers for services decommissioned a year ago. These are pure waste and are best found by looking for load balancers with essentially zero <code>RequestCount</code> over thirty days.</li>\n<li><strong>NLB cross-zone enabled without thought.</strong> Turning it on for even distribution is reasonable, but on NLB it is charged, so on a high-throughput NLB it can be a large hidden item. The alternative is balanced target counts per zone with cross-zone off.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Consolidation Levers, With Their Trade-offs</h4>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; One internet-facing ALB with a wildcard certificate (or several SNI certificates) and listener rules routing <code>service-a.example.com</code> and <code>/service-b/*</code> to their own target groups replaces dozens of load balancers. Each service keeps its own target group, health check, deployment lifecycle and per-target-group metrics and alarms, so observability and deployment independence survive. In Kubernetes, the AWS Load Balancer Controller's <strong>ingress group</strong> feature (the <code>alb.ingress.kubernetes.io/group.name</code> annotation, with <code>group.order</code> controlling rule priority) lets Ingress resources from many namespaces share one ALB while each team still owns its own Ingress object — the standard answer to &quot;we have one ALB per Ingress and it is expensive.&quot;</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The trade-offs must be stated, because an interviewer is listening for whether you understand them. <strong>Blast radius</strong>: one load balancer is now a shared failure domain, and a bad listener rule, an exhausted rule budget, a certificate problem, a WAF misconfiguration or a configuration deploy affects every service on it. <strong>Quotas</strong>: rules per ALB, certificates per load balancer, target groups per load balancer and targets per target group are all bounded, so consolidation consumes a finite budget and a very large platform hits the ceiling — which is where you shard deliberately into a handful of ALBs rather than one. <strong>Change coupling</strong>: many teams now change one resource, so it needs an owner, IaC-enforced rule priorities, and a review process; ingress groups solve the mechanics but not the governance. <strong>Noisy neighbours</strong>: LCU usage is shared, and per-service cost attribution becomes an allocation exercise rather than a line item. My practical default is to shard by <strong>blast-radius tier and exposure</strong>: separate load balancers for internet-facing versus internal, for production versus non-production, and for anything whose availability requirement genuinely differs — then consolidate aggressively within each shard.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two more levers worth precision. <strong>Right-sizing zones</strong> is real but should be approached carefully: each enabled zone gives the load balancer a node there, and fewer zones means less cross-zone traffic and fewer nodes — but it also reduces resilience, and for ALB the cross-zone traffic is not separately charged anyway. Going below three zones to save money is almost always the wrong trade. <strong>Moving cacheable traffic to CloudFront</strong> attacks three components at once: data transfer out is priced lower at CloudFront, cache hits never reach the ALB so processed bytes and connections drop, and requests served from cache are never inspected by the ALB's web ACL so WAF request charges drop too. For a static-asset-heavy site this is frequently the single largest saving available, and it improves latency, which makes it the rare optimization with no real downside beyond invalidation discipline.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Governance: Tagging and Automated Detection</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Optimization that is not enforced regresses within two quarters. Three mechanisms keep it fixed. <strong>Tagging</strong>: require <code>Owner</code>, <code>Service</code>, <code>Environment</code> and <code>CostCenter</code> tags on every load balancer, enforced by a tag policy or an SCP and by an IaC module that refuses to create untagged resources, then activate those tags as <strong>cost allocation tags</strong> so Cost Explorer can attribute spend to teams. Untagged load balancers should be treated as suspected orphans. <strong>Automated detection of unused load balancers</strong>: a scheduled job (Lambda on EventBridge, or a Config custom rule) that lists every load balancer across accounts and Regions, checks <code>RequestCount</code> — or <code>ActiveFlowCount</code> for NLB — over the last thirty days, checks whether any target group has registered targets, and opens a ticket or notifies the owner tag for anything with effectively no traffic. AWS Trusted Advisor and Cost Optimization Hub surface idle load balancers as well; use them as a second source, not the only one. <strong>Guardrails at creation time</strong>: a platform ALB that new services join by default, so creating a dedicated load balancer is an explicit, justified decision rather than the path of least resistance — this is the control that actually prevents sprawl, since everything else is cleanup after the fact.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">One caution about deletion: verify before you delete. Check access logs (not just metrics) for any traffic, check Route 53 and CloudFront for records pointing at the name, and confirm with the owner. Then remove deletion protection and delete — and expect to find that a small number of &quot;idle&quot; load balancers serve a quarterly batch job or a disaster-recovery path. Disabling the listener or shrinking to a single zone first, then deleting after a waiting period, is a safer sequence in a large estate.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem;
