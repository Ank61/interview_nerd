{"title":"TLS at Scale — Mutual TLS, Security Policies, and Certificate Management","tags":["mtls","security-policies","acm","sni"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Attaching a certificate to an HTTPS listener takes thirty seconds. Everything difficult about TLS on load balancers comes afterwards: deciding which protocol versions and ciphers you will accept without silently cutting off paying customers, deciding whether clients must prove their own identity and who then bears the cost of issuing and revoking their certificates, keeping hundreds of certificates valid across renewals that happen unattended, and being clear-eyed about what \"end-to-end encryption\" actually guarantees once the load balancer is in the path. These are governance problems as much as configuration problems, and interviewers probe them because getting them wrong causes outages that are total and instantaneous.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Security policies</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>security policy</strong> is a named, AWS-curated combination of TLS protocol versions and cipher suites that a listener will negotiate. You do not pick individual ciphers on an ALB; you pick a policy, and the names are self-describing once you learn the grammar. <code>ELBSecurityPolicy-2016-08</code> is the long-standing default for HTTPS listeners and permits TLS 1.0 through 1.2 — which is precisely why so many accounts are still accepting TLS 1.0 without anyone having chosen to. The <code>TLS13</code> family, such as <code>ELBSecurityPolicy-TLS13-1-2-2021-06</code>, adds TLS 1.3 while setting a minimum version; the <code>1-2</code> in the name is the <em>minimum</em>, so that policy means TLS 1.2 and 1.3 only. <code>FS</code> policies restrict to forward-secrecy cipher suites (ECDHE key exchange), and <code>Res</code> variants are more restrictive still. <code>FIPS</code> policies, such as the <code>TLS13-1-2-FIPS</code> family, negotiate only FIPS 140-validated cryptography and exist for regulated workloads; they are available in a subset of regions, so verify availability rather than assuming. Compatibility is good among current clients but not universal — very old embedded devices, unpatched libraries pinned to older OpenSSL, and some corporate middleboxes that inspect TLS are the usual casualties, and middleboxes are a real and under-appreciated risk because they fail in confusing ways rather than cleanly. Note also that some policies restrict which certificate key types are usable, so ECDSA versus RSA choices interact with policy choice.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Measuring before you migrate: the TLS 1.0/1.1 problem</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">PCI DSS and general hygiene push everyone toward dropping TLS 1.0 and 1.1, and the change is one API call — which is exactly why it is dangerous. Tightening a security policy takes effect immediately for all new connections, and a client that cannot negotiate an accepted version does not get a nice error page; it gets a <strong>handshake failure</strong>. From the client's perspective your service is simply down, and because the failure happens before HTTP, it produces <em>no</em> access log entry, no HTTP status code and no application metric. You will not see it. Your customer will.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">So the rule is: <strong>measure first, using data you already have</strong>. ALB <strong>access logs</strong> include <code>ssl_protocol</code> and <code>ssl_cipher</code> fields on every HTTPS request, along with <code>client:port</code>, <code>user_agent</code> and <code>request</code>. Query them with Athena over a meaningful window — at least a full month, because monthly batch integrations and quarterly partner jobs are the classic thing you would miss with a week of data. The analysis to produce:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Volume by protocol version.</strong> What percentage of requests are TLS 1.0 or 1.1? A tiny percentage is not automatically safe — see the next point.</li>\n<li><strong>Distinct clients, not requests.</strong> Group by source IP and user agent. One partner integration issuing 0.01 percent of your requests can be your largest contract. Requests measure traffic; distinct clients measure blast radius.</li>\n<li><strong>Which endpoints.</strong> Old TLS concentrated on a webhook or partner API path is a different problem from old TLS on your marketing site, and may be solvable by giving that path its own listener.</li>\n<li><strong>Identify and contact.</strong> Map source IPs to known partners, correlate with authenticated user identity where the logs allow, and give affected parties a deadline with a specific technical ask.</li>\n<li><strong>Cipher inventory too.</strong> The same query over <code>ssl_cipher</code> tells you who depends on ciphers a stricter policy would remove — a change a version-only analysis misses entirely.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Then migrate safely. Stand up a <strong>second listener or second load balancer</strong> with the strict policy and have willing clients test against it. Announce a date. Where you must keep legacy clients alive, move them to a dedicated hostname and listener with the weaker policy so the main endpoint can be hardened — this converts a hard cutover into a segmentation problem. Consider a <strong>brownout</strong>: apply the strict policy for a short, announced window, watch for escalations, and revert; the absence of complaints during a brownout is far stronger evidence than log analysis alone. Add monitoring for the change itself: a drop in <code>RequestCount</code> or <code>NewConnectionCount</code> with no corresponding error-rate increase is the signature of handshake failures, since failed handshakes never become logged requests. Keep the rollback to one <code>modify-listener</code> call, and know that reverting does not fix clients that have already backed off.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Mutual TLS on the ALB</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Mutual TLS makes the <em>client</em> present a certificate. On an ALB HTTPS listener this is configured via <code>MutualAuthentication</code> with a <code>Mode</code> of <code>off</code>, <code>passthrough</code>, or <code>verify</code>.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>passthrough.</strong> The ALB requests the client certificate chain, does not validate it, and forwards the entire chain to the target in the <code>x-amzn-mtls-clientcert</code> header as URL-encoded PEM. Your application does all validation: chain building, trust anchoring, expiry, revocation, and whatever business rules attach identity to a certificate. Choose this when validation logic is genuinely custom — certificate-to-tenant mapping, per-partner trust anchors, an existing PKI library you trust — or when you need the raw certificate for auditing. The cost is that you own the hard part, and getting chain validation right in application code is error-prone.</li>\n<li><strong>verify.</strong> The ALB validates the client certificate against a <strong>trust store</strong> you manage and rejects the connection if validation fails, so unauthenticated clients never reach your application at all. On success it forwards parsed attributes as headers: <code>x-amzn-mtls-clientcert-serial-number</code>, <code>x-amzn-mtls-clientcert-issuer</code>, <code>x-amzn-mtls-clientcert-subject</code>, <code>x-amzn-mtls-clientcert-validity</code> and <code>x-amzn-mtls-clientcert-leaf</code>. Your application reads a subject and maps it to a tenant. This is the mode you want by default: the rejection happens at the edge, and the application handles identity rather than cryptography.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>trust store</strong> is a first-class ELB resource holding a CA certificate bundle uploaded from S3, which you then associate with a listener. It supports <strong>revocation lists</strong> — you add CRLs, also from S3 — and options including whether to ignore client certificate expiry (occasionally needed during a migration) and whether to advertise the trust store's CA names during the handshake, which helps clients with multiple certificates pick the right one but also discloses your CA list. There are documented limits on bundle and CRL size, number of trust stores, and client certificate chain depth; check current quotas rather than guessing.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>The operational burden is the real subject.</strong> mTLS moves you from managing a handful of server certificates to running a certificate lifecycle for every client. You must issue certificates (AWS Private CA is the natural fit, and integrates with the trust store model), distribute them to clients securely, rotate them before expiry — which means every client has a scheduled, coordinated change, and a partner who misses it experiences a total outage — and revoke them promptly when a client is compromised or offboarded. Revocation is the weakest link: CRLs must be regenerated, uploaded and refreshed, and there is a window during which a revoked certificate still works. Add monitoring for imminent client certificate expiry, because the failure mode is binary and arrives without warning. The honest summary: mTLS is a strong control with a heavy, permanent operational tax, and the tax scales with the number of clients.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Where it genuinely belongs:</strong> partner and B2B integrations, where the client population is small, contractual and long-lived, and where a certificate is a far better credential than a shared API key; <strong>IoT fleets</strong>, where devices cannot hold secrets safely and per-device certificates give you individual revocation; and <strong>zero-trust service-to-service</strong> traffic, where every call is authenticated cryptographically rather than by network position — though at that point a service mesh with automated short-lived certificate rotation is usually a better fit than ALB mTLS, because the mesh solves the rotation problem that ALB mTLS leaves to you. Where it does not belong: consumer-facing web and mobile applications, where you would be asking end users to install certificates.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Certificate management at scale</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>ACM</strong> issues public certificates at no charge for use with ELB and renews them automatically, which is the single biggest operational win available. But automatic renewal is only automatic if its precondition holds, and that precondition is the thing people break.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>DNS validation and the classic outage.</strong> With DNS validation, ACM gives you a CNAME record to publish for each domain. That record is not a one-time setup step — it must <strong>remain published for the life of the certificate</strong>, because ACM re-validates domain ownership at renewal. The recurring incident: a team validates a certificate, someone later tidies up the hosted zone and deletes an unexplained CNAME, ACM begins renewal attempts around 60 days before expiry and fails, the certificate's renewal status goes to failed, nobody is watching, and the certificate expires — at which point every client sees a hard TLS failure simultaneously. Prevent it with three things: treat validation CNAMEs as protected infrastructure in code with comments explaining why they exist, alarm on ACM renewal status rather than only on expiry date, and prefer DNS validation over email validation (email validation requires a human to click a link every renewal and is a guaranteed eventual outage).</li>\n<li><strong>Imported certificates.</strong> ACM will store a certificate you obtained elsewhere, but it <strong>cannot renew it</strong>. You own the entire rotation: obtain the new certificate, import it (as a new certificate or as a replacement), and confirm the listener uses it. Set expiry alarms and a calendar process, and treat every imported certificate as recurring toil. Prefer ACM-issued certificates wherever the domain is under your control, and reserve imports for externally mandated certificates.</li>\n<li><strong>Region binding.</strong> An ACM certificate used by an ALB must be in the <em>same region</em> as the ALB. CloudFront requires <code>us-east-1</code>. Multi-region architectures therefore need a certificate per region, each with its own validation records and renewal path — a common source of a single forgotten region expiring.</li>\n<li><strong>SNI and multiple certificates.</strong> One HTTPS listener can serve many certificates: a default certificate plus additional ones, selected per connection by <strong>SNI</strong>. The ALB uses a smart selection algorithm to match the client's SNI hostname to the best available certificate and falls back to the default when a client sends no SNI. There is a documented quota on certificates per load balancer (commonly 25, excluding the default), which is the hard architectural constraint for multi-tenant SaaS.</li>\n<li><strong>Wildcard versus SAN.</strong> A wildcard such as <code>*.example.com</code> covers unlimited single-level subdomains with one certificate, which is ideal for tenant-per-subdomain SaaS: new tenants need no certificate work. It does not cover the apex or multi-level subdomains, and it concentrates risk, since one private key serves everything. A SAN certificate names specific domains — including entirely unrelated ones — giving precise scope and per-domain visibility, but every added domain means reissuing, revalidating and redeploying, which does not scale to hundreds of customer domains. The usual answer is a wildcard plus apex for your own subdomains, SANs for a modest number of distinct brands, and for large numbers of customer-owned vanity domains, CloudFront (much higher certificate limits, and SNI at the edge) or multiple load balancers.</li>\n<li><strong>Monitoring expiry.</strong> Use several layers: the ACM <code>DaysToExpiry</code> CloudWatch metric with an alarm well ahead of expiry; EventBridge events for approaching expiration and for renewal state changes; the AWS Config managed rule for certificate expiration checks across the account; and, most valuable of all, an external synthetic check that actually connects to the endpoint and inspects the presented certificate, because that catches the case where the certificate in ACM is fine but the listener is serving the wrong one.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">ALPN on NLB TLS listeners, and end-to-end encryption</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>ALPN</strong> is the TLS extension by which client and server agree on the application protocol during the handshake — it is how HTTP/2 is negotiated without an extra round trip. On an ALB, ALPN is handled for you: HTTP/2 is negotiated automatically on HTTPS listeners when enabled. On an <strong>NLB TLS listener</strong> you control it explicitly with the <code>alpn_policy</code> listener attribute, whose values are <code>HTTP1Only</code>, <code>HTTP2Only</code>, <code>HTTP2Optional</code>, <code>HTTP2Preferred</code> and <code>None</code>. This matters because an NLB terminating TLS is otherwise protocol-agnostic: if you are fronting a gRPC or HTTP/2 backend through an NLB and the negotiated protocol comes back as HTTP/1.1, clients silently downgrade and gRPC breaks. <code>HTTP2Preferred</code> negotiates h2 when the client offers it and falls back gracefully; <code>HTTP2Only</code> is for controlled internal clients.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>End-to-end encryption</strong> means re-encrypting from the load balancer to the target: set the target group protocol to HTTPS (or TLS for an NLB) and run TLS on your targets. This is required by many compliance regimes and is good practice for sensitive data, since it protects the hop inside your VPC. But be precise about what it buys, because this is a favourite probing question: <strong>the ALB does not validate the target's certificate</strong>. It will happily complete a handshake with a self-signed certificate, an expired certificate, or a certificate whose name does not match. There is no truststore, no hostname verification and no pinning on the target side.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The threat-model consequence: target-side TLS gives you <strong>confidentiality and integrity on the wire against a passive or in-path observer within the VPC</strong>, but it does <strong>not</strong> authenticate the target to the load balancer. An attacker who could redirect traffic to a host they control — by registering a malicious target, manipulating ECS service discovery, or compromising routing — could terminate that TLS with their own certificate and the ALB would not object. So target-side TLS is not a substitute for controlling target registration, locking down security groups, and using IAM to restrict who can register targets; it is a complement. Practical implications: self-signed certificates on targets are acceptable to the ALB and common in practice (using ACM Private CA is better hygiene but the ALB will not check it either), you should not expect certificate-expiry errors to surface from the target side, and the operational cost is real — TLS on every target means certificate distribution and rotation across your fleet, plus extra CPU. Many teams reasonably conclude that HTTP to targets inside a private subnet with tight security groups is an acceptable posture, and reserve end-to-end encryption for regulated data. State the trade-off rather than treating end-to-end TLS as automatically correct.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Interview scenario: enforcing a TLS 1.2 minimum without breaking customers</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A B2B SaaS platform with 400 enterprise customers must stop accepting TLS 1.0 and 1.1 to satisfy an audit finding, with a 90-day deadline. Revert with a single <code>modify-listener</code> call at the first sign of unexpected impact. A clean brownout is much stronger evidence than log analysis alone.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Phase 4 — cut over and prevent regression (weeks 10 to 12).</strong> Apply <code>ELBSecurityPolicy-TLS13-1-2-2021-06</code> permanently — TLS 1.3 for modern clients, 1.2 as the floor, nothing older.
