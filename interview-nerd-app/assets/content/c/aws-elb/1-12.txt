{"title":"Load Balancer Observability at Scale","tags":["observability","cloudwatch-metric-math","alb-access-logs","athena"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">The traffic tier is the only place in your stack that sees every request, from every client, before your code runs. That makes it the single best observability vantage point you own — and the one most teams instrument worst. A typical setup has a dashboard of raw counts, an alarm on <code>HTTPCode_Target_5XX_Count &gt; 100</code> that fires during every traffic peak and is therefore muted, and access logs written to S3 that nobody has ever queried. Advanced observability at the load balancer means three things: metrics expressed as <strong>ratios and percentiles with the right dimensions</strong>, access logs turned into a <strong>queryable, partition-projected table</strong> that answers questions in seconds for cents, and <strong>trace identity</strong> that lets you follow one request from the edge into your application logs.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Metric Design: Attribution First</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The first design decision is attribution. ALB publishes two separate 5XX families and conflating them costs you hours in an incident.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>HTTPCode_ELB_5XX_Count.</strong> The load balancer generated the response itself; your target never produced it (and for a 503 may never have been contacted). This is an <em>infrastructure</em> signal: no healthy targets, no registered targets, the load balancer unable to scale fast enough, a target that closed the connection or returned garbage (502), or a target that blew the idle timeout (504).</li>\n<li><strong>HTTPCode_Target_5XX_Count.</strong> The target returned a 5XX and the load balancer faithfully relayed it. This is an <em>application</em> signal and it belongs to the service team, not the platform team.</li>\n<li><strong>Sub-code metrics.</strong> <code>HTTPCode_ELB_500_Count</code>, <code>HTTPCode_ELB_502_Count</code>, <code>HTTPCode_ELB_503_Count</code> and <code>HTTPCode_ELB_504_Count</code> exist separately. Alarm on them separately: 502 and 504 have completely different root causes and completely different runbooks, so a combined alarm forces the on-call to re-derive which one is happening.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The second design decision is <strong>ratios over counts</strong>. An alarm on 100 errors per minute is an alarm on your own growth: it fires when traffic doubles even though quality is unchanged, and it goes silent at 3am when a total outage produces only 5 requests. Use CloudWatch <strong>metric math</strong> to express availability as a percentage of <code>RequestCount</code>, and guard against divide-by-zero and sparse periods with <code>FILL</code> and an <code>IF</code> on volume. A production error-rate alarm reads roughly as <code>IF(requests &gt; 50, 100 * (elb5xx + target5xx) / requests, 0)</code>, so it is a true SLO burn signal and it cannot false-fire on a handful of requests during a quiet window.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The third is <strong>percentiles over averages</strong>. <code>TargetResponseTime</code> supports extended statistics: request <code>p50</code>, <code>p90</code>, <code>p99</code> and <code>p99.9</code> rather than <code>Average</code>. An average hides the shape of the distribution entirely — a service where 1% of requests take 10 seconds and the rest take 20ms has a healthy-looking average and a broken customer experience. The gap between p50 and p99 is itself a diagnostic: a p50 that rises with p99 means broad saturation, while a flat p50 with a rising p99 means a subset of requests (one endpoint, one shard, one slow dependency, one bad host) is degrading.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Metric Set That Actually Matters</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>RequestCount / NewConnectionCount / ActiveConnectionCount.</strong> Volume, plus the connection-reuse ratio. <code>RequestCount / NewConnectionCount</code> tells you whether clients are using keep-alive; a ratio near 1 means every request pays a fresh TCP and TLS handshake, which shows up as latency and as LCU cost.</li>\n<li><strong>TargetResponseTime.</strong> Time from the load balancer sending the request to the target to receiving the response headers. Always read as percentiles, and always per target group.</li>\n<li><strong>HealthyHostCount and UnHealthyHostCount.</strong> The most important availability signal in the whole set, and the one most often misread — see the dimension discussion below. Alarm on <code>HealthyHostCount</code> being <em>low</em> (a minimum threshold), not on <code>UnHealthyHostCount</code> being high, because the latter goes to zero when targets deregister entirely.</li>\n<li><strong>TargetConnectionErrorCount.</strong> The load balancer could not establish a connection to a target. Non-zero means security groups, a target process not listening, connection-table exhaustion on the instance, or a saturated accept backlog. This is a pure infrastructure signal with no application interpretation, which makes it an excellent early-warning metric.</li>\n<li><strong>RejectedConnectionCount.</strong> The load balancer refused a connection because it hit its own connection limit. Non-zero here means you are at the edge of what the load balancer will accept and you should be talking about pre-warming or architecture, not about your application.</li>\n<li><strong>ClientTLSNegotiationErrorCount and TargetTLSNegotiationErrorCount.</strong> Handshakes that failed on the client side or on the load-balancer-to-target side. The client-side metric is your canary before and after any security policy change, and it also rises during certificate expiry and SNI problems.</li>\n<li><strong>ConsumedLCUs.</strong> The capacity and cost metric in one. It is billed on the maximum of the LCU dimensions, so a spike here without a corresponding spike in <code>RequestCount</code> tells you a different dimension (new connections, active connections, processed bytes, or rule evaluations) is dominating.</li>\n<li><strong>RuleEvaluations.</strong> Rises with listener rule count and traffic; a useful signal when a consolidated ALB starts to look expensive.</li>\n<li><strong>Header handling.</strong> <code>DroppedInvalidHeaderRequestCount</code>, <code>ForwardedInvalidHeaderRequestCount</code> and <code>DesyncMitigationMode_NonCompliant_Request_Count</code> tell you whether non-conforming requests are reaching you — a security signal and a compatibility signal before you tighten desync mitigation mode.</li>\n<li><strong>NLB specifics.</strong> <code>ActiveFlowCount</code>, <code>NewFlowCount</code>, <code>PeakPacketsPerSecond</code>, <code>PortAllocationErrorCount</code>, and the three reset counters <code>TCP_Client_Reset_Count</code>, <code>TCP_Target_Reset_Count</code> and <code>TCP_ELB_Reset_Count</code>. The reset attribution is the NLB equivalent of the ELB-versus-Target 5XX split: it tells you who hung up.</li>\n<li><strong>Classic Load Balancer legacy.</strong> <code>SurgeQueueLength</code> (requests queued waiting for a backend, with a hard cap) and <code>SpilloverCount</code> (requests dropped because the queue was full) are the CLB saturation pair. A non-zero <code>SpilloverCount</code> is customer-visible failure. These metrics have no ALB/NLB equivalent, which is one of the honest arguments for migrating: on a CLB, surge queue growth is your only warning of backend saturation.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Dimensions: Where Aggregates Lie</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Every ALB metric is published with the <code>LoadBalancer</code> dimension, and target-related metrics are also published with <code>TargetGroup</code>, and — critically — with <code>AvailabilityZone</code>. The aggregate view is the one that lies to you.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Per target group.</strong> On a consolidated ALB fronting twenty services, load-balancer-level <code>TargetResponseTime</code> is a weighted blur. A checkout service degrading from 40ms to 900ms may move the aggregate p99 by a few milliseconds if it is 2% of traffic. Every SLO alarm must carry the <code>TargetGroup</code> dimension. The same applies to canaries: a weighted-target-group canary taking 5% of traffic can be returning errors on every request while the aggregate error rate stays inside your alarm threshold. Alarm the canary target group separately and more tightly than production.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Per Availability Zone.</strong> <code>HealthyHostCount</code> aggregated across zones is nearly useless: with cross-zone load balancing off, a zone with zero healthy hosts is a total outage for the third of clients whose DNS resolution pinned them to that zone's node, yet the aggregate looks like two-thirds healthy. Always alarm <code>HealthyHostCount</code> with <code>Statistic: Minimum</code> across the <code>AvailabilityZone</code> dimension, or build a metric-math <code>MIN</code> over per-zone metrics. The same trick catches the harder case: per-zone <code>TargetResponseTime</code> p99 with an alarm on the <em>spread</em> between the worst zone and the median zone, which detects a single-zone regression that the aggregate absorbs. ALB also publishes target-group health signals used by DNS failover and zonal shift — anomalous and unhealthy routing counts — which are worth graphing next to per-zone health because they tell you whether the load balancer has already taken a zone out of DNS on your behalf.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Access Logs: The Field Set and the Three Times</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">ALB access logs are off by default, are written to S3 as gzipped space-delimited records roughly every five minutes, and are the highest-value observability artifact in the traffic tier because they are <strong>per-request</strong>, not aggregated. Enable them everywhere; the S3 cost is trivial compared to one debugging session without them. The fields you will actually use:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>The three processing times.</strong> <code>request_processing_time</code> (load balancer received the request and began sending it to a target — includes time waiting for an available target), <code>target_processing_time</code> (the target's own time to respond), <code>response_processing_time</code> (load balancer finished receiving the response and finished sending it to the client — dominated by slow clients). These three separate &quot;the load balancer was busy or had nowhere to send this&quot; from &quot;your application was slow&quot; from &quot;the client's network was slow.&quot; A value of <code>-1</code> means the transaction did not complete, which is itself the signal.</li>\n<li><strong>elb_status_code and target_status_code.</strong> The same attribution split as the metrics, per request. A <code>target_status_code</code> of <code>-</code> with a 502/503/504 in <code>elb_status_code</code> means the request never got a response from any target.</li>\n<li><strong>target:port and target_processing_time.</strong> Groups errors and latency by individual target address, which is how you find the one bad host in a fleet of two hundred.</li>\n<li><strong>ssl_protocol and ssl_cipher.</strong> The TLS version and cipher actually negotiated. This is how you answer &quot;can we drop TLS 1.0/1.1?&quot; with data instead of hope.</li>\n<li><strong>matched_rule_priority and actions_executed.</strong> Which listener rule matched and what the load balancer did (forward, redirect, fixed-response, authenticate, waf). Essential for routing bugs.</li>\n<li><strong>trace_id.</strong> The <code>X-Amzn-Trace-Id</code> value, the join key between the load balancer and everything downstream.</li>\n<li><strong>error_reason, classification and classification_reason.</strong> The reason a request failed (including desync classification: <code>Acceptable</code>, <code>Ambiguous</code>, <code>Severe</code>), which is how you tune desync mitigation mode safely.</li>\n<li><strong>domain_name and chosen_cert_arn.</strong> The SNI host and which certificate was served — the fastest way to diagnose multi-certificate listeners.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Athena with Partition Projection</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Point Athena at the raw prefix with no partitions and every query scans your entire log history: slow and expensive. Add real partitions and you have to run <code>MSCK REPAIR</code> or add partitions daily forever. <strong>Partition projection</strong> solves both: you declare the partition key as a date range with a template describing the S3 layout, and Athena computes the partitions it needs from the <code>WHERE</code> clause without any metastore lookups. A day-scoped query then reads one day of objects. This is the single highest-leverage configuration choice in log analytics on ALB — it is the difference between a table people use during incidents and a table they avoid.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The queries that repeatedly earn their keep: <strong>slowest endpoints</strong> (p99 of <code>target_processing_time</code> grouped by a normalized URL path, which finds the one endpoint dragging your SLO), <strong>top clients</strong> (request count by client IP and user agent, which finds scrapers and retry storms), <strong>error breakdown by target</strong> (5XX rate per <code>target:port</code>, which isolates the bad host), <strong>TLS version distribution</strong> (count by <code>ssl_protocol</code> and client, run for two weeks before a security policy change), and <strong>requests that never reached a target</strong> (<code>target_status_code = '-'</code>, grouped by <code>elb_status_code</code> and <code>error_reason</code>). Normalize paths by stripping numeric and UUID segments with <code>regexp_replace</code>, or your grouping explodes into a million unique URLs.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Connection Logs and End-to-End Tracing</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Access logs describe requests; they cannot describe connections that never produced a request. <strong>ALB connection logs</strong> (a separate opt-in attribute, also to S3) record every client connection attempt with the TLS protocol and cipher, handshake latency, a TLS error code where the handshake failed, and the client certificate subject for mutual TLS. This is the only way to see failed handshakes as individual events rather than as a bump in <code>ClientTLSNegotiationErrorCount</code>, and it is what you turn on before tightening a security policy or debugging an mTLS trust store. For NLB, access logs are produced only for TLS listeners; a pure TCP listener gives you flow metrics and VPC Flow Logs, not per-connection application detail — a real observability cost of choosing NLB that belongs in your design discussion.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Request tracing.</strong> ALB inserts or extends <code>X-Amzn-Trace-Id</code> with a <code>Root=1-...</code> identifier (adding a <code>Self=</code> component of its own) and writes the same value into the access log. The discipline that makes this powerful is two lines of application code: read the header, put the root id into every structured log line your request handler emits, and propagate it on outbound calls. Now one Athena query on <code>trace_id</code> and one CloudWatch Logs Insights query on the same field reconstruct the whole request. Enable <strong>X-Ray</strong> on the application and the same identifier ties the load balancer record to a segment graph showing which downstream dependency consumed the time. The correlation you want in a dashboard is <code>TargetResponseTime</code> p99 overlaid with target-side metrics — application-reported latency, CPU, GC pause time, database latency — because the gap between load-balancer-observed latency and application-observed latency <em>is</em> queueing, and queueing is the earliest reliable saturation signal you have.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem;
