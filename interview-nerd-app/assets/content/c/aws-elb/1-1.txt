{"title":"Advanced Traffic Management — Weighted Target Groups, Blue/Green, and Canary Releases","tags":["weighted-target-groups","blue-green","canary","codedeploy"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Once a listener rule can forward to several target groups with weights, the load balancer stops being merely an ingress and becomes the <strong>deployment control plane</strong>. Every progressive delivery pattern worth having — blue/green, canary, linear shifting, header-gated dogfooding — is a specific choreography of weights, stickiness and metrics on top of that one primitive. The senior skill is not knowing that weights exist; it is knowing exactly what the weights do and do not guarantee, what state and schema constraints sit underneath, and how to build an automated rollback that actually fires before customers notice.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Weighted forward actions: the primitive</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A listener rule's <code>forward</code> action can reference up to <strong>five target groups</strong>, each with an integer <code>Weight</code> from 0 to 999. The weights are <strong>relative, not percentages</strong>: the share a target group receives is its weight divided by the sum of all weights in the action. Weights of 90 and 10 and weights of 900 and 100 are identical in effect; weights of 3 and 1 send 75 percent and 25 percent. Using a base of 1000 is a common convention purely because it makes per-mille reasoning easy — a weight of 5 out of 1000 is a half-percent canary, which is finer than you can express with a base of 100.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Applied per request, not per connection.</strong> The weighting decision happens on each request the rule matches. Because ALB pools and reuses connections, consecutive requests on the same client connection can land in different target groups. This is the single most important property to internalise, and the reason stickiness exists.</li>\n<li><strong>A weight of zero drains.</strong> Setting a target group's weight to 0 stops new requests being routed to it while leaving it attached to the rule — and, critically, leaving its <strong>health checks and per-target-group metrics intact</strong>. That makes zero-weight the correct parking state for a standby environment: fully warm, fully monitored, one API call away from carrying traffic.</li>\n<li><strong>Changes take effect in seconds, with no DNS involvement.</strong> A <code>modify-rule</code> call re-weights the fleet essentially immediately. This is the fundamental advantage over Route 53 weighted records.</li>\n<li><strong>It composes with conditions.</strong> Weights live inside an action, and actions live behind conditions, so you can weight traffic only for a particular host, path, header or query string. A canary restricted to <code>/api/v2/search</code> is just a weighted forward on a path-condition rule.</li>\n<li><strong>Independent target group configuration.</strong> Each target group carries its own health check, deregistration delay, algorithm, stickiness and slow-start settings, so blue and green can differ in those respects during a migration.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Target group stickiness — and when it is mandatory</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Because weighting is per request, a browser session can be split across versions: the HTML from green, an XHR from blue, a follow-up from green again. If the two versions are not <strong>wire-compatible</strong> — different JSON shapes, different asset hashes, different CSRF token formats, a changed cookie contract — this produces user-visible breakage that no amount of per-version testing would have caught, because the bug only exists in the mixture.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Target group stickiness</strong> solves it. Configured inside the forward action as <code>TargetGroupStickinessConfig</code> with <code>Enabled</code> and <code>DurationSeconds</code>, it makes the ALB set an additional cookie — named <code>AWSALBTG</code>, with <code>AWSALBTGCORS</code> for cross-origin cases — that records which <em>target group</em> the client was assigned to. For the configured duration (up to seven days) that client keeps going to the same target group regardless of weights. Distinguish it carefully from <strong>target stickiness</strong> (the target group attribute <code>stickiness.enabled</code> with the <code>AWSALB</code> or <code>AWSALBAPP</code> cookie), which pins a client to a specific <em>target</em> within a group. They are orthogonal and are often both needed: target group stickiness keeps you on one version, target stickiness keeps you on one server.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Stickiness has costs you must state in an interview. It makes weights <strong>approximate for returning users</strong> — a 1 percent canary with seven-day stickiness accumulates a growing sticky cohort, so actual traffic share drifts above the nominal weight over time. It slows rollback, because sticky clients keep hitting the bad version after you set its weight to zero unless you also shorten or disable stickiness. It only works for clients that store cookies, so mobile apps and API clients are unaffected unless they persist cookies deliberately. And a long duration turns a progressive rollout into a quasi-permanent split. The usual compromise is a duration matched to a realistic session length — minutes to a couple of hours — rather than days.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Blue/green with weights</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two complete environments, each with its own target group: blue at weight 1000, green at 0. You deploy to green, let its targets pass health checks, warm caches and JIT, validate it out-of-band, and then flip — green to 1000, blue to 0 — in a single <code>modify-rule</code> call so the shift is atomic rather than a two-step window where both carry traffic. Rollback is the same call with the weights reversed, which is the entire point: recovery time is one API call and a few seconds, and it does not depend on a rebuild, a redeploy, or an image pull.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The comparison with <strong>DNS-based blue/green</strong> is a favourite interview question. Route 53 weighted or failover records can shift between two load balancers, but the shift is governed by <strong>record TTL plus client resolver behaviour</strong>, so it is not atomic, not fast, and not fully controllable — some clients cache beyond TTL (the JVM problem), so a DNS rollback leaves a long tail of users on the broken version for an unbounded period. Listener weights change behaviour at the load balancer, so they take effect for every client at once regardless of client behaviour, and they support fractions far finer than DNS weighting can reliably deliver. The cases where DNS still wins are genuinely different ones: shifting between <em>regions</em>, between different load balancers, or between fundamentally different stacks, where there is no single ALB to hold both target groups. A sound default is weights for in-region version shifts and DNS (or Global Accelerator, whose traffic dials change without DNS caching) for cross-region shifts.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Keep blue alive at weight zero for a defined bake period — typically one full business cycle — before tearing it down. That is what converts \"roll back\" from a deployment into a routing change. The cost is running two environments, which is the honest trade-off of blue/green and the reason canary exists.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Canary and linear shifting</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Canary sends a small fraction to the new version, evaluates, and either advances or aborts. A typical ladder is 1 percent, 5 percent, 25 percent, 50 percent, 100 percent, with a <strong>bake interval</strong> between steps long enough to accumulate statistically meaningful data. Linear (or progressive) shifting is the same idea with fixed increments on a fixed cadence — 10 percent every minute, say — and no human in the loop; it trades granularity of judgement for simplicity and speed.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>yaml</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\"><i class=s>listener</i> <i <strong>per target group</strong>, and this is where most implementations are quietly broken. The CloudWatch metrics <code>HTTPCode_Target_5XX_Count</code>, <code>TargetResponseTime</code>, <code>RequestCount</code>, <code>HealthyHostCount</code> and <code>UnHealthyHostCount</code> are all published with a <code>TargetGroup</code> dimension alongside <code>LoadBalancer</code>. Alarms must be scoped to the green target group's dimension; an alarm on the load-balancer-level aggregate is nearly useless during a 1 percent canary, for the arithmetic reason below. Beyond ALB metrics, compare application-level and business-level signals per version — error logs tagged with the release, p99 latency, conversion rate, checkout success — which usually means propagating a version label into your logs, traces and custom metrics so the two cohorts are separable.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Use rates, not counts.</strong> A count alarm on the green target group will not fire at 1 percent traffic because the absolute numbers are tiny. Use CloudWatch <strong>metric math</strong> to compute an error <em>ratio</em> — green 5XX count divided by green request count — and alarm on that. Ratios are comparable across steps and across cohorts; counts are not.</li>\n<li><strong>Respect statistical power.</strong> At 1 percent of a service doing 100 requests per second, the canary sees about one request per second. Detecting a change in a 0.1 percent error rate at that volume takes far longer than a five-minute bake. Either start the ladder higher, lengthen the first bake, or accept that step one only catches gross failures (crash loops, 100 percent errors, startup failures) and that subtle regressions will be caught at 25 percent. Saying this out loud is a strong senior signal.</li>\n<li><strong>Warm the green targets.</strong> A brand new fleet has cold caches, cold connection pools and un-JITted code, so its first-minute latency is worse for reasons unrelated to the code change. Target group <strong>slow start</strong> ramps traffic to newly registered targets over a configured duration and prevents this from being misread as a regression.</li>\n<li><strong>Compare like with like.</strong> Green with two targets and blue with twenty have different per-target concurrency, so a latency difference may be a capacity artefact. Scale green proportionally to its weight, or compare per-target load rather than raw latency.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Automated orchestration with CodeDeploy</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Driving weights by hand is fine for a demo and unacceptable as a process, because the rollback decision must not depend on a human watching a dashboard at 02:00. <strong>CodeDeploy</strong> blue/green automates the whole choreography for ECS and for EC2/on-premises, manipulating the ALB listener on your behalf.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Two target groups and (optionally) two listeners.</strong> You register a production listener and, optionally, a <strong>test listener</strong> on a different port. CodeDeploy points the test listener at the green target group first, so the new version is reachable and fully exercised while <em>zero</em> production traffic reaches it. This is the cleanest available answer to \"how do you validate before exposure\".</li>\n<li><strong>Lifecycle hooks as gates.</strong> For ECS the hooks are <code>BeforeInstall</code>, <code>AfterInstall</code>, <code>AfterAllowTestTraffic</code>, <code>BeforeAllowTraffic</code> and <code>AfterAllowTraffic</code>; each invokes a Lambda function that runs whatever validation you like — smoke tests, contract tests, a synthetic checkout — and reports <code>Succeeded</code> or <code>Failed</code> back to CodeDeploy. A failure aborts the deployment and reverts the listener. <code>AfterAllowTestTraffic</code> is the important one: it runs against the test listener before production traffic shifts.</li>\n<li><strong>Deployment configurations express the ladder.</strong> Built-in configurations include all-at-once, canary shapes such as ten percent for five minutes followed by the remainder, and linear shapes such as ten percent every minute; you can define custom configurations. CodeDeploy implements these by adjusting the listener's weighted forward action for you.</li>\n<li><strong>Automatic rollback on CloudWatch alarms.</strong> You attach alarms to the deployment group, and CodeDeploy aborts and reverts the listener if any of them enters ALARM during the deployment. This is where the per-target-group ratio alarms from the previous section belong, and it is the mechanism that makes unattended deployment defensible. Also enable rollback on deployment failure and on stopped deployments.</li>\n<li><strong>Termination wait time.</strong> After a successful shift, CodeDeploy keeps the old task set alive for a configured period, during which a rollback is instant. Set this to a real bake window rather than the default minimum.</li>\n<li><strong>Native alternatives.</strong> ECS has added built-in blue/green deployment support that orchestrates the same listener manipulation without a separate CodeDeploy application, and on Kubernetes, Argo Rollouts and Flagger drive ALB weights through the AWS Load Balancer Controller using the same primitive. The mechanism underneath is always weighted target groups.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Header and query routing for internal testing; the honest truth about shadow traffic</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Before exposing a single real user, you can route <em>yourself</em> to the new version. A rule with an <code>http-header</code> condition — for example <code>X-Release: canary</code> — and a higher priority than the weighted rule sends only requests carrying that header to green, with production traffic untouched. A browser extension or a proxy adds the header, and engineers dogfood the real deployment in the real environment against real data. A <code>query-string</code> condition does the same for shareable links, and a cookie is matchable as a header condition, which lets an internal user opt in once and stay opted in. Combine with a <code>source-ip</code> condition to restrict a rule to the office or VPN range.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two cautions. Anyone who guesses the header value gains access to the unreleased version, so treat this as convenience rather than access control — pair it with authentication or a source-IP condition if the new version must not be publicly reachable. And every such rule consumes part of the 100-rules-per-load-balancer budget and adds a rule evaluation, so clean them up after the release.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Dark launch and shadow traffic</strong> deserve an honest answer: <strong>the ALB cannot mirror or duplicate requests</strong>. There is no traffic-mirroring action, and weighted forwarding <em>moves</em> traffic rather than copying it. VPC Traffic Mirroring operates on ENIs at the packet level and is a network-forensics tool, not an HTTP request replay mechanism. So shadowing must be implemented above or beside the load balancer: a service mesh or Envoy sidecar with a request-mirror filter, a small forwarding shim in the application or at an API layer that fires a fire-and-forget copy to the shadow fleet, or offline replay of access logs and captured traffic against the new version. Whichever you choose, the discipline is the same — the shadow path must never write to production data stores, must never send customer-visible side effects such as emails or payments, must have its errors excluded from production alerting, and must be latency-isolated so a slow shadow cannot slow the real request. Saying plainly that the ALB does not do this, and then proposing the mesh or application-level approach, is a much better answer than inventing a feature.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Pitfalls that sink real rollouts</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Stateful sessions spanning versions.</strong> If session state lives in target memory, a request that lands on the other version finds nothing. Externalise session state (ElastiCache, DynamoDB) with a <em>version-tolerant</em> serialisation format, so v1 can read what v2 wrote. If you cannot, target group stickiness is mandatory, not optional — and you must accept its rollback drag.</li>\n<li><strong>Database schema compatibility.</strong> During any shift, both versions are live against one database, so the schema must satisfy both. The discipline is <strong>expand and contract</strong>: expand (add the nullable column, add the new table, dual-write) in a release that is backward compatible; migrate and backfill; only then contract (drop the old column) in a <em>later</em> release, once no running version reads it. Never combine a destructive migration with a traffic shift — a rollback then means rolling back code onto a schema that no longer supports it, which is how a five-minute incident becomes a restore-from-backup.</li>\n<li><strong>Metrics aggregation hiding a bad canary.</strong> This is the arithmetic every senior candidate should be able to do on the spot. A service at a 0.1 percent baseline error rate shifts 1 percent of traffic to a version that fails 50 percent of requests. Blended error rate becomes roughly 0.99 times 0.001 plus 0.01 times 0.5, about 0.6 percent — a sixfold increase in aggregate that is still well under any sane page threshold, while one in every two hundred users is being handed a hard failure. The mitigations: alarm on per-target-group <em>ratios</em>, use anomaly detection or comparison against the blue cohort rather than static aggregate thresholds, and include composite alarms that fire on divergence between cohorts instead of absolute badness.</li>\n<li><strong>Sticky cohorts distorting weights.</strong> Nominal 1 percent plus multi-day stickiness does not stay at 1 percent; verify actual share with per-target-group <code>RequestCount</code> rather than trusting the configuration.</li>\n<li><strong>Deregistration and in-flight requests.</strong> Dropping a target group's weight to zero does not terminate in-flight requests; and when you do deregister targets, <code>deregistration_delay.timeout_seconds</code> governs the connection draining window. Long-lived requests, WebSockets and uploads need that delay tuned up, or you will cut customers off mid-request during teardown.</li>\n<li><strong>Client-side and cached assets.</strong> If green serves new JavaScript bundles and a client holds an old HTML shell, or a CDN caches version-specific assets, the mixture breaks independently of the ALB. Fingerprinted asset filenames and cache-busting are prerequisites for weighted rollouts of browser applications.</li>\n<li><strong>Forgetting the non-HTTP surface.</strong> Weights only govern traffic through that listener rule. Asynchronous consumers, cron jobs, queue workers and scheduled tasks are not canaried by the ALB at all and need their own rollout strategy.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Interview scenario: design a safe progressive rollout with automated rollback</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A payments API on ECS Fargate behind an ALB, 4,000 requests per second, 99.95 percent availability SLO, and a release that changes both a response field and a database column. Design the rollout.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Schema first, separated from the code shift.</strong> Release one: expand only — add the new nullable column, dual-write to old and new, read from old. Deploy that with the normal process; it is backward compatible and rollback-safe. Release two is the one being canaried: read from the new column, return the new response field additively so existing clients are unaffected. Release three, days later, contracts: stop dual-writing and drop the old column. At no point is a traffic shift concurrent with a destructive migration.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Topology.</strong> Two target groups, <code>pay-blue</code> and <code>pay-green</code>, on one weighted rule. A test listener on port 8443 pointed at green. Slow start of 60 seconds on both target groups so new tasks warm up. Target group stickiness is set to a modest 900 seconds because the API is partly browser-driven; for pure machine clients it would be disabled, since they do not keep cookies and stickiness would only add rollback drag.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Gates.</strong> CodeDeploy blue/green with a canary configuration. <code>AfterAllowTestTraffic</code> runs a Lambda that fires contract tests and a synthetic payment against the test listener; failure aborts before any production exposure. Then the weighted ladder: 5 percent for 10 minutes, 25 percent for 10, 50 percent for 10, 100 percent. I start at 5 rather than 1 deliberately: at 4,000 requests per second, 5 percent is 200 requests per second, which gives enough volume to detect a 0.1 percent error-rate regression within the bake window, whereas 1 percent would only catch catastrophic failure. That is the statistical-power trade-off made explicit.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Automated rollback.</strong> Three CloudWatch alarms attached to the deployment group, all scoped to the <em>green</em> target group dimension and all expressed as metric math ratios: 5XX rate above 0.2 percent over two consecutive one-minute periods; p99 <code>TargetResponseTime</code> more than 1.5 times the blue cohort's p99; and an application-level payment-authorisation-failure rate. Plus <code>UnHealthyHostCount</code> above zero, which catches crash loops immediately. CodeDeploy reverts the listener automatically on any alarm, and termination wait time is set to 60 minutes so a manual rollback after completion is still just a weight flip.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Verification and cleanup.</strong> Before the ladder starts, engineers dogfood green through an <code>X-Release: green</code> header rule. During the ladder, dashboards show blue and green side by side on rates, not counts, and per-target-group <code>RequestCount</code> confirms the actual split matches the intended weight despite stickiness. After 100 percent, blue is parked at weight 0 for a full business cycle — warm, health-checked, one API call from resuming — and only then torn down along with the temporary header rule.
