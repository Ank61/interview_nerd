the trade-off is an extra hop, an extra hourly charge, and the loss of the true client IP at the ALB unless you account for the chain. <strong>API Gateway + private integration to an NLB or ALB</strong> where you want managed throttling and keys in front of existing services. <strong>CloudFront + API Gateway</strong> or <strong>CloudFront + Global Accelerator</strong> exist but are situational, and claiming them casually invites a hard follow-up.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; It also gives two static IPs, which some enterprise clients require. The costs are a fixed hourly charge plus a data transfer premium, and the loss of DNS-level flexibility such as weighted geographic steering, which you can regain by putting Route 53 in front for coarse steering. Remove Global Accelerator and you are back to DNS-speed failover. The hard part of this architecture is never the traffic tier: it is <strong>data</strong>. Active-active means either accepting eventual consistency with conflict resolution (DynamoDB global tables, last-writer-wins or application-level merge), or partitioning writes by Region or tenant so no cell takes conflicting writes, or designating one Region as the writer and accepting cross-Region write latency (Aurora Global with a writer plus fast secondary promotion). State your <strong>RPO and RTO</strong> and pick accordingly, and do not claim active-active without saying what happens to conflicting writes. The other under-discussed requirement: capacity. If each Region normally serves half the traffic, each must be able to serve <em>all</em> of it during failover, which means either running at under fifty percent utilization or accepting a scaling delay — and you should say which you chose.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; put an <strong>ALB behind it as a target</strong> when you also want path routing, WAF and OIDC. Remove PrivateLink and your options are public exposure with allow-lists (a larger attack surface and a CIDR-management burden) or VPC peering and Transit Gateway (bidirectional, requires non-overlapping CIDRs, and couples two organizations' networks — a much worse security posture for a vendor relationship). The operational details that come up: endpoint services must <strong>accept</strong> connections (manually or automatically) and can allow-list principals; the consumer gets <strong>private DNS</strong> so existing hostnames resolve to the endpoint without code changes; the provider must enable the zones the consumer needs; and quotas on endpoints and connections per endpoint service are real at scale. For multi-tenant isolation, per-tenant endpoint services or per-tenant target groups plus tenant-scoped authorization are the usual answers.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cross-Cutting Concerns a Strong Candidate Raises Unprompted</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Health check design.</strong> Shallow and cheap for the load balancer — &quot;can this process serve traffic?&quot; — with dependency checks on a separate endpoint that pages humans but does not control routing, because a deep check turns a two-second database blip into a total outage across all zones. Timeout above the health endpoint's p99, unhealthy threshold of three or more, and target group minimum-healthy settings so the load balancer fails open rather than hammering survivors.</li>\n<li><strong>Zero-downtime deployment.</strong> Deregistration delay longer than your longest request, a preStop or shutdown hook that stops accepting and drains, slow start so cold instances are not flooded, and either weighted target groups for blue/green and canary or ASG instance refresh with a canary step. In Kubernetes, pod readiness gates. Name at least one rollback trigger and how fast it acts.</li>\n<li><strong>Observability.</strong> Error-ratio and p99 alarms per target group, healthy-host minimums per Availability Zone, access logs in Athena with partition projection, and <code>X-Amzn-Trace-Id</code> propagated into application logs and X-Ray. Say that aggregates hide a single bad zone or a canary.</li>\n<li><strong>DDoS and WAF posture.</strong> Shield Standard always; CloudFront or Global Accelerator moving the surface to the edge; private origins; WAF managed groups tuned in count mode; rate-based rules scoped to sensitive paths; and the admission that autoscaling is a bill, not a defense.</li>\n<li><strong>Cost.</strong> Hourly floor charges drive consolidation; LCUs are billed on the maximum dimension; cross-zone is charged on NLB but not ALB; CloudFront cuts egress, processed bytes and WAF request charges together.</li>\n<li><strong>Failure domains per layer.</strong> Be explicit: Route 53 and CloudFront are global; Global Accelerator's anycast is global with Regional endpoints; an ALB or NLB is Regional with zonal nodes; a target group spans zones; an Auto Scaling group spans zones; RDS Multi-AZ is two zones; a single zone is the smallest blast radius you can shed with ARC zonal shift. The question to answer for each layer is &quot;what breaks if this fails, and what do I shed?&quot;</li>\n<li><strong>Quotas and scaling behavior.</strong> Rules, certificates, target groups and targets are bounded; ALB scales over minutes so flash spikes need an edge in front; NLB scales without pre-warming.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Anti-Patterns Worth Naming</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>One load balancer per microservice</strong> with no consolidation strategy — pure hourly cost and quota exhaustion.</li>\n<li><strong>Deep health checks</strong> that check the database, converting dependency blips into fleet-wide outages.</li>\n<li><strong>Public ALB as a CloudFront origin with no origin lock</strong>, making the edge WAF bypassable.</li>\n<li><strong>Trusting the first X-Forwarded-For value</strong>, which silently breaks every IP-based control.</li>\n<li><strong>Raising the idle timeout</strong> to &quot;fix&quot;
