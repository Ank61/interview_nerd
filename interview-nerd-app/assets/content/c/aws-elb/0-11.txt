It can tell you how many requests arrived, how long your application took, which errors came from your code versus from the load balancer itself, and how many servers are currently fit to serve. This topic covers the metrics worth watching, how to read the common HTTP error codes, how to turn on access logs (they are off by default), and how ELB is actually billed — because the pricing model surprises people.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Where the numbers live: CloudWatch</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Amazon CloudWatch</strong> is AWS's monitoring service. It collects numeric measurements over time (<strong>metrics</strong>), lets you graph them, and lets you define <strong>alarms</strong> that watch a metric and notify you or take action when it crosses a threshold you set. Your load balancer publishes metrics to CloudWatch automatically, at no extra charge and with nothing to enable. ALB metrics live in the namespace <code>AWS/ApplicationELB</code>, NLB metrics in <code>AWS/NetworkELB</code>, and Classic in <code>AWS/ELB</code>.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Each metric carries <strong>dimensions</strong> — labels that let you narrow the view. The two that matter are <code>LoadBalancer</code> (the whole load balancer) and <code>TargetGroup</code> (one group of targets behind it), and you can add <code>AvailabilityZone</code>. Narrowing to a target group is how you tell which service is slow when one ALB fronts several.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The metrics a beginner should actually watch</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>RequestCount.</strong> How many requests the load balancer handled. Use the <em>Sum</em> statistic — an average is meaningless here. This is your traffic baseline; a sudden drop to zero usually means a DNS or client-side problem rather than a server one.</li>\n<li><strong>TargetResponseTime.</strong> Seconds from the moment the load balancer sent the request to your target until the target began sending its response. This is <em>your application's</em> latency, with the network and load balancer overhead excluded — so it is where you look when the site feels slow. Watch the <strong>p95</strong> or <strong>p99</strong> percentile rather than the average: the average hides the slow tail that users actually notice.</li>\n<li><strong>HTTPCode_Target_2XX_Count / 3XX / 4XX / 5XX.</strong> Status codes <em>your application</em> returned. 2xx is success, 3xx a redirect, 4xx a client mistake (bad input, not-found, unauthorised), 5xx your code failing.</li>\n<li><strong>HTTPCode_ELB_4XX_Count / HTTPCode_ELB_5XX_Count.</strong> Status codes the <strong>load balancer generated itself</strong> because it could not get a usable answer from a target. <strong>This distinction is the single most useful diagnostic fact in ELB monitoring.</strong> Target 5xx means your application ran and returned an error — go read your application logs. ELB 5xx means the load balancer could not complete the exchange at all: no healthy targets, the connection was refused or closed, or the target timed out. Those are infrastructure problems and your application logs will show nothing.</li>\n<li><strong>HealthyHostCount and UnHealthyHostCount.</strong> How many targets in a target group currently pass and fail health checks. <strong>This pair is the most important thing to alarm on</strong> — everything else degrades gracefully, but healthy hosts reaching zero is a hard outage. Note these are per target group, and with cross-zone load balancing disabled they are reported per Availability Zone.</li>\n<li><strong>ActiveConnectionCount.</strong> Concurrent front-end to back-end connections open right now. A steady climb with flat RequestCount suggests connections are not being closed properly.</li>\n<li><strong>NewConnectionCount.</strong> New connections opened in the period. Compare it with RequestCount: if they are nearly equal, <strong>keep-alive</strong> connection reuse is not happening and you are paying handshake cost on every request.</li>\n<li><strong>RejectedConnectionCount.</strong> Connections the load balancer refused because it hit its own connection capacity limit. Non-zero here means you are being throttled at the load balancer, not at your app — a real red flag.</li>\n<li><strong>TargetConnectionErrorCount.</strong> Times the load balancer failed to <em>establish</em> a connection to a target. Points at security groups, an app that is not listening, or an exhausted backlog queue. Typically rises together with ELB 5xx.</li>\n<li><strong>ConsumedLCUs.</strong> How many <strong>Load Balancer Capacity Units</strong> you are consuming — this is the metric your bill is computed from, covered below. Watching it makes cost concrete instead of a monthly surprise.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two more worth knowing once you are comfortable: <strong>RequestCountPerTarget</strong> (useful as an Auto Scaling target-tracking signal) and <strong>ClientTLSNegotiationErrorCount</strong> (failed HTTPS handshakes, which usually means a certificate or protocol mismatch).</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; Making the app's keep-alive longer than the ALB's idle timeout fixes it. Other causes are a genuine crash while responding, or response headers larger than the ALB permits.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>503</strong> almost always means <strong>zero healthy targets</strong> — either none are registered, or all are failing health checks. Check <code>HealthyHostCount</code> first, then the health check path (does it return 2xx?) and port (is there a security group rule for it?). A <strong>504</strong> means your target was simply too slow; look at <code>TargetResponseTime</code> to see whether it is the whole endpoint or a tail of slow requests, and decide whether to fix the query or raise the idle timeout. For <strong>401 and 403</strong>, remember that the ALB itself can produce 401 when its built-in OIDC/Cognito authentication action is in play, and <strong>WAF</strong> blocks show up as 403 — so check whether the count appears under ELB or Target metrics before blaming your code.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Access logs — off by default, invaluable afterwards</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">CloudWatch metrics are aggregates: they tell you that 40 requests returned 500, not <em>which</em> requests. <strong>Access logs</strong> are the per-request detail — one line for every single request — delivered as gzipped files to an <strong>Amazon S3</strong> bucket (AWS's object storage service) every 5 or 60 minutes.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A line contains, among about thirty fields: the request <strong>timestamp</strong>; the load balancer name; the <strong>client address and port</strong>; the <strong>target address and port</strong> that served it; three timing fields — <code>request_processing_time</code>, <code>target_processing_time</code>, <code>response_processing_time</code> — in seconds; the <strong>elb_status_code</strong> and the <strong>target_status_code</strong> (both, side by side, which is how you see that the ALB returned 502 while the target returned nothing); bytes received and sent; the <strong>request line</strong> (method, URL, HTTP version); the <strong>user agent</strong>; TLS cipher and protocol; the target group ARN; a <strong>trace ID</strong>; the matched listener rule; and error reason codes.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two practical points. First, <strong>they are disabled by default</strong> — you must turn them on with the attribute <code>access_logs.s3.enabled</code> and supply a bucket (and optional prefix). Second, the bucket needs a <strong>bucket policy</strong> that permits the log delivery service to write objects into it, otherwise delivery silently fails. For newer Regions the policy grants <code>s3:PutObject</code> to the service principal <code>logdelivery.elasticloadbalancing.amazonaws.com</code>; in older Regions you grant it to a Region-specific ELB account ID instead. Check the current documentation for your Region rather than copying an old snippet.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Because the volume is large, you query logs rather than read them. <strong>Amazon Athena</strong> lets you run ordinary SQL directly against files in S3: create an external table over the log location, then ask questions like \"which URLs produced 502s in the last hour\", \"which client IPs sent the most requests\", or \"what is the p99 target processing time by URL path\". This is the difference between five minutes and five hours during an incident — and it only works if logs were <em>already</em> on when the incident happened, which is why enabling them is a day-one task, not a post-incident one. Set an S3 <strong>lifecycle rule</strong> to expire or archive old logs so the bucket does not grow forever.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Connection logs and request tracing</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Connection logs</strong> are a separate, newer ALB feature (<code>connection_logs.s3.enabled</code>) that records each <em>connection</em> rather than each request: client address and port, TLS details, and the outcome of the handshake. They are the right tool for debugging failed HTTPS negotiations, which never become requests at all and therefore never appear in access logs.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">For <strong>request tracing</strong>, an ALB adds an <code>X-Amzn-Trace-Id</code> header to every request with a unique identifier, and that same ID appears in the access log line. If your application logs the header value too, you can follow one specific request from the load balancer through every service that handled it. <strong>AWS X-Ray</strong> builds on the same identifier to draw a visual timeline of a request across services. At beginner level the useful habit is simply: log <code>X-Amzn-Trace-Id</code> in your application, and you will be able to correlate a user's complaint with an exact log line.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The handful of alarms every load balancer should have</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>UnHealthyHostCount greater than 0</strong> — and separately <strong>HealthyHostCount below your minimum</strong> (for example less than 2). The second one is the page-someone alarm; losing capacity quietly is how outages start.</li>\n<li><strong>HTTPCode_ELB_5XX_Count</strong> above a small threshold.
