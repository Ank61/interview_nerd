{"title":"SQS Security Architecture","tags":["security","iam","encryption","vpc-endpoint"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Securing a queue is not attaching a policy; it is designing who can put data in, who can take it out, who can change the rules, how the data is protected at rest and in transit, which network paths are acceptable, and how you would know if any of that changed. A queue is frequently the seam between trust domains: a third party publishes into it, an internal service consumes from it, and the message body carries whatever the producer felt like putting there. This topic covers least-privilege role design, queue policies including the confused-deputy patterns, the SSE-SQS versus SSE-KMS decision in operational detail, PrivateLink and network constraints, TLS enforcement, what CloudTrail can and cannot tell you, and message-level data hygiene.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Least-privilege role design</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Start by splitting the roles. A producer and a consumer should never share an identity, because their required permissions are disjoint and combining them means a compromised producer can also drain the queue.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Producer role.</strong> <code>sqs:SendMessage</code> and <code>sqs:SendMessageBatch</code> only, plus <code>sqs:GetQueueUrl</code> if the code resolves the URL by name. Nothing else. A producer does not need <code>sqs:GetQueueAttributes</code> in most designs and certainly does not need to receive.</li>\n<li><strong>Consumer role.</strong> <code>sqs:ReceiveMessage</code>, <code>sqs:DeleteMessage</code>, <code>sqs:DeleteMessageBatch</code>, <code>sqs:ChangeMessageVisibility</code> and <code>sqs:ChangeMessageVisibilityBatch</code>, plus <code>sqs:GetQueueAttributes</code> if it autoscales on queue depth. A consumer never needs SendMessage to the same queue unless it is deliberately requeueing, which is a design smell.</li>\n<li><strong>Resource scoping.</strong> Always name the exact queue ARN. <code>Resource: \"*\"</code> on an SQS statement grants every queue in the account, including other teams and including DLQs holding failed payloads. Wildcards belong at most in a path prefix such as <code>arn:aws:sqs:us-east-1:111122223333:payments-*</code>, and even then be deliberate.</li>\n<li><strong>Explicit deny on management actions.</strong> Application roles must not hold <code>sqs:PurgeQueue</code>, <code>sqs:SetQueueAttributes</code>, <code>sqs:DeleteQueue</code>, <code>sqs:AddPermission</code> or <code>sqs:RemovePermission</code>. <strong>PurgeQueue is a data-destruction primitive</strong>: one call silently and irrecoverably deletes every message in the queue, it is not undoable, and there is no confirmation step. No application principal should ever hold it. Put an explicit Deny in a permissions boundary or SCP rather than relying on nobody adding it later, because an explicit Deny wins over any future Allow.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Remember the two-policy model. Within a single account, either the identity policy or the queue policy can grant access. <strong>Across accounts, both are required</strong>: the calling principal needs an identity policy allowing the action and the queue policy must allow that principal. This is the most common cause of a cross-account AccessDenied where the queue policy looks correct.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Queue policies and the confused deputy</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The queue policy is a resource policy. Three patterns matter.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Cross-account access.</strong> Grant to a specific role ARN in the partner account, not to the account root, unless you genuinely intend every principal in that account to have access. Granting to <code>arn:aws:iam::444455556666:root</code> delegates the decision to the other account administrators.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Service principals and the confused-deputy problem.</strong> When S3, SNS or EventBridge delivers into your queue, the calling principal is the service, for example <code>s3.amazonaws.com</code>. A policy that allows <code>sqs:SendMessage</code> from <code>s3.amazonaws.com</code> with no conditions allows <em>anyone in the world</em> to configure their S3 bucket to publish into your queue, because the service is the deputy and it is confused about whose behalf it is acting on. The fix is conditions that pin the caller: <strong>aws:SourceArn</strong> constrains which specific bucket, topic or rule may invoke, and <strong>aws:SourceAccount</strong> constrains which account owns it. Use SourceArn where the ARN is known and stable, and add SourceAccount as a second belt, particularly for services where the source ARN can be predicted or where you want to allow a family of resources in one account.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Organization-wide grants.</strong> <code>aws:PrincipalOrgID</code> lets you allow any principal in your AWS Organization without enumerating accounts, which is the right tool for a shared internal event queue. It is safe because it is evaluated on the calling principal and cannot be forged from outside the organization. Prefer it over long lists of account IDs that drift as accounts are created.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Encryption at rest: SSE-SQS versus SSE-KMS</h4>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; Choose SSE-KMS when you need <strong>key control, an audit trail of key usage, the ability to revoke, or an independent control point for cross-account access</strong> such as a queue that a partner account writes to.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Three SSE-KMS details that bite people. First, <strong>both producer and consumer need KMS permissions on the key</strong>, not just SQS permissions: the sender needs <code>kms:GenerateDataKey</code> (and per the documentation <code>kms:Decrypt</code> as well) and the receiver needs <code>kms:Decrypt</code>. When this is missed the failure is an AccessDenied on <code>SendMessage</code> or <code>ReceiveMessage</code> even though the IAM policy plainly allows the SQS action, which sends people hunting in the wrong policy for hours. The tell is that the error message mentions KMS. Second, SQS supplies an <strong>encryption context</strong> of <code>aws:sqs:arn</code> mapped to the queue ARN, which you can pin in the key policy with a condition so the key can only be used for that specific queue. Third, the <strong>data key reuse period</strong> (60 seconds to 24 hours, default 300 seconds) is both a cost lever and a security lever: longer means far fewer KMS requests but a plaintext data key cached longer in the service and slower effect when you disable a key or revoke a grant.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">If the key is <strong>disabled or scheduled for deletion</strong>, sends and receives start failing with KMS errors such as KMSDisabled or KMSInvalidState; messages already in the queue are not lost but become unreadable until the key is re-enabled, and because of the data key cache some operations may keep succeeding until the reuse window expires, which makes the failure look intermittent. This is worth rehearsing: it is both a real outage mode and a deliberate containment action.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Network controls: PrivateLink and aws:SourceVpce</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SQS is reachable at a public regional endpoint, so by default a valid credential works from anywhere on the internet. An <strong>interface VPC endpoint</strong> puts SQS on an ENI inside your VPC, keeps traffic off the internet, and removes the NAT hop. But the endpoint alone does not stop anyone from reaching the public endpoint; it only provides a private path. The control that matters is the <strong>queue policy condition</strong>: a Deny for any request where <code>aws:SourceVpce</code> does not equal your endpoint ID. That single statement is what makes a leaked access key useless outside your network, which is the actual threat model people care about. Combine it with an <strong>endpoint policy</strong> on the VPC endpoint itself, which restricts which SQS actions and which queue ARNs can be reached through that path, giving you a second, network-anchored control point.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">When applying a SourceVpce Deny, sequence it carefully. Any legitimate caller that is not in the VPC, including Lambda functions not attached to the VPC, S3 event notifications, SNS subscriptions and EventBridge targets, will start failing. Service principals do not come through your endpoint, so scope the Deny with a <code>NotPrincipal</code> carve-out or restrict it to the specific IAM roles it is meant to constrain.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Enforcing TLS in transit</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SQS supports HTTPS and the SDKs use it, but nothing structurally prevents an HTTP call. Add a Deny on <code>aws:SecureTransport</code> being false to every queue policy. It is a two-line statement, it never breaks a correctly configured client, and it turns an assumption into an enforced control that an auditor can verify. Make it part of the queue module in your infrastructure code so no queue is created without it.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Auditing: what CloudTrail actually gives you</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">CloudTrail records SQS <strong>management events</strong> by default: <code>CreateQueue</code>, <code>DeleteQueue</code>, <code>SetQueueAttributes</code>, <code>AddPermission</code>, <code>RemovePermission</code>, <code>TagQueue</code> and, critically, <code>PurgeQueue</code>. What it does <strong>not</strong> record is the data plane: SendMessage, SendMessageBatch, ReceiveMessage, DeleteMessage, DeleteMessageBatch and ChangeMessageVisibility are not logged to CloudTrail. This is a genuinely important limitation to state in an interview, because it means <strong>you cannot reconstruct who read or wrote a particular message from CloudTrail</strong>. Per-message auditability has to come from your own application logging, with the message ID and the calling identity recorded by the producer and consumer.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The detections worth building on top of the management events, wired through EventBridge rules to a security channel or a ticket:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>PurgeQueue called on any production queue.</strong> Page on this. It is data destruction and it is almost never legitimate outside a controlled runbook.</li>\n<li><strong>SetQueueAttributes changing the Policy, KmsMasterKeyId or SqsManagedSseEnabled.</strong> This covers both a policy being widened and encryption being turned off.</li>\n<li><strong>A queue policy gaining a principal outside the organization.</strong> IAM Access Analyzer does this natively for SQS and is the least effort option; back it with a Config rule.</li>\n<li><strong>AddPermission calls</strong>, which is the legacy one-shot grant API and a common way for a broad grant to sneak in.</li>\n<li><strong>KMS key disable, schedule-deletion or key-policy change</strong> on any key backing a production queue.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Message-level concerns</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Treat the message body as something that will end up in a log, a DLQ, a screenshot in a ticket and a developer laptop during debugging. <strong>Never put secrets in a message body</strong>: pass a reference to Secrets Manager or Parameter Store and let the consumer resolve it under its own identity. <strong>Minimise personal data</strong>: send a customer ID and let the consumer look up the record rather than shipping name, address and payment details through the pipeline, which drags the queue, its DLQ and every log line into the compliance scope. Where sensitive payloads are unavoidable, encrypt the payload at the <strong>application level</strong> with envelope encryption, so that SQS, the DLQ, and anyone with queue read access sees ciphertext and only holders of the KMS key can decrypt. That also protects the data across a cross-account hop. For payloads over 256 KB, the Extended Client Library stores the body in S3 and puts a pointer in the message, which moves the security question to the S3 bucket policy and its encryption.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Security-review scenario: a queue receiving third-party data</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A partner company will publish webhook events into a queue you own. The review checklist I would run:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Identity.</strong> Grant to one specific partner role ARN, not the partner account root, in the queue policy. Action list is SendMessage and SendMessageBatch only. Never grant ReceiveMessage, DeleteMessage, GetQueueAttributes or any management action to an external principal.</li>\n<li><strong>Confused deputy.</strong> If the partner publishes through SNS or EventBridge rather than directly, pin <strong>aws:SourceArn</strong> and <strong>aws:SourceAccount</strong> so only their specific topic or bus can deliver.</li>\n<li><strong>Transport.</strong> Deny when <code>aws:SecureTransport</code> is false.</li>\n<li><strong>Encryption.</strong> SSE-KMS with a customer managed key, so the key policy is a second control and CloudTrail shows partner key usage. Grant the partner role only <code>kms:GenerateDataKey</code> and <code>kms:Decrypt</code> on that key, conditioned on the <code>kms:EncryptionContext:aws:sqs:arn</code> matching this queue. Keep the data key reuse period short enough that disabling the key is an effective kill switch.</li>\n<li><strong>Blast radius.</strong> A dedicated queue per partner, never a shared one, so a compromised partner cannot flood or poison another partner data path, and so revocation is per-partner.</li>\n<li><strong>Input validation.</strong> The consumer must treat every body as hostile: strict schema validation, size limits, no deserialisation into arbitrary types, and rejection to a quarantine queue rather than a crash loop.</li>\n<li><strong>Abuse controls.</strong> Alarm on an unusual sending rate from the partner, since there is no per-principal rate limit on a queue, and consider a separate throttling layer if the partner can outrun your consumers.</li>\n<li><strong>Detection.</strong> EventBridge rules for policy changes and PurgeQueue on this queue; IAM Access Analyzer flagging the external grant so it is reviewed rather than forgotten.</li>\n</ul>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"I design queue security in four layers.
