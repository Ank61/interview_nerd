FIFO is a system-design problem, and the design surface is small but sharp: one identifier choice determines both your correctness and your maximum throughput, one failure mode (head-of-line blocking) can stall a customer indefinitely, and one five-minute window is the entire extent of the \"exactly-once\" guarantee you are being sold.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Central Insight: MessageGroupId Is Both Ordering Key and Parallelism Key</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">On a FIFO queue, SQS guarantees that messages within a single <code>MessageGroupId</code> are delivered in the order they were sent, and it enforces this by refusing to deliver message N+1 of a group until message N has been deleted or has returned to visibility. Different groups are completely independent — they can be delivered concurrently to different consumers in any relative order.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">So the group id is doing two jobs at once. It is the <strong>ordering key</strong>: everything sharing a group is serialized. Even a good key can be badly distributed: if one enterprise customer produces 40% of your events, that customer's group is a serial bottleneck while everyone else is idle, and your effective throughput is set by the hottest group, not the average. Measure group cardinality <em>and</em> the distribution of messages per group before committing.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">High Throughput Mode for FIFO</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A standard FIFO queue has a documented base limit of roughly 300 transactions per second per API action, which batching (10 messages per call) lifts to roughly 3,000 messages per second per action. <strong>High throughput mode for FIFO</strong> raises these limits by an order of magnitude or more. You enable it by setting the queue attributes <code>DeduplicationScope=messageGroup</code> and <code>FifoThroughputLimit=perMessageGroupId</code>.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Those two attributes describe exactly what changes. <code>FifoThroughputLimit=perMessageGroupId</code> means the throughput quota is applied per message group rather than to the queue as a whole, so total capacity scales with the number of distinct active groups. <code>DeduplicationScope=messageGroup</code> means the 5-minute deduplication window is scoped per group rather than queue-wide — necessary for the per-group quota to be meaningful, but a behavioral change you must accept: two identical deduplication IDs in <em>different</em> groups will both be delivered. The specific TPS figures differ by region and are documented per region, so cite them from the docs rather than from memory, and remember that some quotas are adjustable through AWS Support.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The critical caveat: <strong>high throughput mode does not help a queue with few message groups.</strong> If everything is in one group, per-group limits give you exactly one group's worth of throughput. font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Head-of-Line Blocking: FIFO's Real Operational Danger</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The ordering guarantee has a dark side. If message N in a group cannot be processed successfully, SQS will not release message N+1 until N is deleted or exhausts its retries. A <strong>poison message</strong> — malformed payload, a reference to a deleted record, a bug triggered by one specific input — therefore <strong>stalls its entire group indefinitely</strong>. With <code>MessageGroupId = customerId</code>, one bad event means that one customer's entire event stream stops. Everything still looks healthy at the queue level: total depth may be small, most groups drain fine, and no alarm fires. Meanwhile one customer has had no updates for six hours.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\"><i class=k>group</i> c<i class=n>-1001</i>:  [POISON m7] [m8] [m9] [m10] [m11] ...   <i class=o>&lt;</i><i class=c>-- m8..m11 never delivered</i>\n<i class=k>group</i> c<i class=n>-1002</i>:  [m1] [m2] ...  draining normally\n<i class=k>group</i> c<i class=n>-1003</i>:  [m1] ...       draining normally\n\nQueue depth: <i class=n>37</i> (looks fine). Combine with a short visibility timeout so retries burn quickly rather than blocking for minutes each.</li>\n<li><strong>Distinguish retryable from permanent failures.</strong> A validation error or a 400 from a downstream should go straight to a dead-letter path on the first attempt — retrying it 3 times just extends the stall. A 503 or a timeout is worth retrying. Encode that distinction in the consumer, not just in <code>maxReceiveCount</code>.</li>\n<li><strong>Record-and-skip design.</strong> The strongest architectural answer: design the handler so a failure writes a failure record (to a table, an error topic, a DLQ of your own) and then <em>deletes</em> the message, so the group advances. You trade \"this event will eventually be processed\" for \"this stream keeps moving\", which for most customer-facing streams is the right trade.</li>\n<li><strong>Per-group circuit breaker.</strong> Track consecutive failures per group in a small state store; after K failures, quarantine the group (route subsequent messages to a side channel and alert) rather than retrying forever.</li>\n<li><strong>Alarm on the right metric.</strong> <code>ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage</code> is the detector for head-of-line blocking, because depth stays low while age climbs. Any DLQ message on a FIFO queue should page — it means a group was stalled.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Deduplication Mechanics — and Why It Is a Bounded Guarantee</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">FIFO deduplication works on a <strong>5-minute window</strong>. When a message arrives, SQS computes or reads its <code>MessageDeduplicationId</code>; if a message with the same id was accepted within the last 5 minutes (within the queue, or within the message group under <code>DeduplicationScope=messageGroup</code>), the new message is <strong>accepted and silently discarded</strong>. You get a successful <code>SendMessage</code> response with a <code>MessageId</code>, and the message is never delivered.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">That last detail is the one that burns teams. The send succeeded. The producer logged success. The consumer never saw it. It looks exactly like message loss, and engineers spend days chasing it. Two frequent causes: content-based deduplication on messages that are legitimately identical (a \"heartbeat\" or \"refresh account 42\" event sent twice in five minutes is a genuine duplicate by content but two distinct business events), and a deduplication id derived from too-coarse a value.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The two modes:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Content-based deduplication</strong> (<code>ContentBasedDeduplication=true</code>): SQS SHA-256 hashes the message <em>body</em> to derive the id. Convenient, and wrong whenever two distinct events can have identical bodies. Note it hashes the body — not message attributes — so varying only an attribute does not make two sends distinct.</li>\n<li><strong>Explicit <code>MessageDeduplicationId</code></strong>: you supply it (up to 128 characters), and it always wins over content-based. This is almost always the right choice, because you can derive it from a <strong>domain identity</strong> — the upstream event id, or a composite like <code>order-9931-status-SHIPPED-attempt-key</code> — which is what you actually mean by \"the same message\".</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">And the honest framing for an interview: <strong>the 5-minute window makes FIFO's \"exactly-once processing\" a bounded guarantee, not an absolute one.</strong> A producer retry 6 minutes after the original send produces a genuine second delivery. A message redelivered after a visibility timeout is a second delivery regardless of dedup. FIFO dedup narrows the duplicate window; it does not close it. Application-level idempotency on a domain key is the only unbounded protection, and it is the thing you should build whether or not you use FIFO.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">FIFO with Lambda</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A Lambda event source mapping on a FIFO queue preserves per-group ordering: Lambda processes one batch per message group at a time, and the number of concurrent invocations is bounded by the number of active message groups. A batch delivered to your function contains records from a <strong>single message group</strong>, in order.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Failure behavior is the part people get wrong. If your function returns an error for a batch, the whole batch is retried, and Lambda will not advance past that group until it succeeds or the messages reach <code>maxReceiveCount</code> — the same head-of-line blocking, now inside Lambda. With <code>FunctionResponseTypes: [\"ReportBatchItemFailures\"]</code>, you return the failing <code>itemIdentifier</code>s and Lambda retries from the <strong>first failure onward within that group</strong>, which preserves ordering rather than reprocessing the earlier successes. Because of this, for FIFO you should report the first failure and everything after it, not just the individual failing record — otherwise you risk processing later messages before an earlier one succeeds.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Practical guidance: keep <code>maxReceiveCount</code> low, keep per-message processing fast (a slow handler on a hot group is a throughput ceiling you cannot scale out of), do not expect <code>MaximumConcurrency</code> to buy you parallelism beyond your group count, and remember that FIFO's ~20,000 in-flight cap is much tighter than standard's.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">When FIFO Is Genuinely Required — and When It Is Not</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">FIFO is right when <strong>out-of-order processing produces a wrong final state that the application cannot detect or repair</strong>. Classic cases: a stream of incremental deltas where order determines the result, a command stream where \"create\" must precede \"update\", or a regulated audit path where ordering is contractual.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">It is often <em>not</em> required, and the alternatives are cheaper and more robust:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Version numbers / sequence numbers.</strong> Put a monotonically increasing version on each event and have the consumer conditionally write only if the incoming version exceeds the stored one. Out-of-order messages become no-ops. This works on a standard queue, scales without bound, and is immune to head-of-line blocking.</li>\n<li><strong>Timestamp-based last-writer-wins.</strong> For state-snapshot events (as opposed to deltas), apply only if the event timestamp is newer than the stored one. Requires trustworthy clocks or upstream-assigned sequence values.</li>\n<li><strong>Send full state, not deltas.</strong> If each message carries the complete desired state, order stops mattering except for recency — which is a much weaker requirement.</li>\n<li><strong>Reorder buffer at the consumer.</strong> Buffer briefly and sort by sequence. Adds latency and complexity; use only when you truly need contiguous ordering and cannot use FIFO.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The senior instinct is to ask whether the ordering requirement is really about ordering or about <em>correctness under concurrency</em> — the latter is usually better solved with conditional writes than with a serialized queue.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Interview Scenario: Group IDs for a Multi-Tenant Platform</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A B2B SaaS processes account events: <code>account.created</code>, <code>subscription.upgraded</code>, <code>user.invited</code>, <code>usage.recorded</code>, <code>invoice.finalized</code>. 4,000 tenants; the largest produces 35% of all traffic.
