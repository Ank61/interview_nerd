{"title":"Troubleshooting Production SQS — A Symptom-Driven Playbook","tags":["troubleshooting","incident-response","visibility-timeout","dlq"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Almost every SQS incident presents as one of eight symptoms, and each symptom has a short, bounded list of causes that can be discriminated with one or two specific metrics or attributes. The value of a playbook is that it replaces guessing with a decision procedure: you name the symptom, you check the discriminating signal, and you land on the cause in minutes rather than restarting things and hoping. This topic works through all eight, names the exact metric, log field, attribute or command to inspect first for each, gives a general triage ordering, and closes with one incident worked from alarm to root cause.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">General triage ordering</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Before diving into a symptom, spend ninety seconds establishing the shape of the problem. Half of all SQS incidents are explained by one of these values.</li>\n<li><strong>2. Is work arriving, and is work completing?</strong> Compare <code>NumberOfMessagesSent</code> with <code>NumberOfMessagesDeleted</code>. This single comparison splits the world into producer problems, consumer problems, and capacity problems.</li>\n<li><strong>3. Timeout tuning and partial-batch reporting reduce how often they happen; only <strong>idempotency</strong> makes them harmless.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Symptom 2: messages disappearing</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Check first:</strong> DLQ depth, then MessageRetentionPeriod against the historical ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage curve, then CloudTrail for PurgeQueue.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Retention expiry.</strong> Signature: age climbed toward MessageRetentionPeriod and then dropped to near zero without a matching rise in NumberOfMessagesDeleted. Messages were deleted by the service, silently. Fix: 14-day retention (it costs nothing) and an alarm on age well before the ceiling.</li>\n<li><strong>The consumer deletes on failure.</strong> The most common self-inflicted cause: a <code>finally</code> block or a catch-all that calls DeleteMessage regardless of outcome. Signature: Deleted tracks Received exactly, DLQ is empty, yet the downstream effect never happened. Fix: delete only after the side effect is durable.</li>\n<li><strong>A DLQ is silently collecting them.</strong> Signature: DLQ depth greater than zero and nobody alarmed on it. This is why DLQ-depth-above-zero is a non-negotiable alarm.</li>\n<li><strong>FIFO deduplication discarding a resend.</strong> If a message is re-sent with the same MessageDeduplicationId, or content-based deduplication is on and the body is byte-identical, within the five-minute deduplication window, SQS accepts the send and returns success while <strong>discarding the message</strong>. Signature: the producer logged a successful send with a message ID but the message never appeared. Fix: use a genuinely unique deduplication ID per logical message, and be careful with content-based deduplication on idempotent-looking payloads such as heartbeats.</li>\n<li><strong>PurgeQueue.</strong> Everything gone at once, no data-plane trace, because SQS does not log message operations to CloudTrail. The management event is the only evidence.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Symptom 3: backlog growing</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Check first:</strong> completion rate (NumberOfMessagesDeleted per second) against arrival rate (NumberOfMessagesSent per second), then consumer concurrency and downstream latency.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The discrimination is whether completion rate <em>fell</em> or arrival rate <em>rose</em>. If arrival rose and completion is flat at its ceiling, this is a capacity problem: scale consumers, and check whether you can. If completion fell while arrival is unchanged, something broke, and the candidates are: a <strong>throttled or failing downstream</strong> (per-message processing duration will have risen, which is the tell), <strong>Lambda concurrency exhausted or throttled</strong> (Throttles greater than zero, ConcurrentExecutions pinned at the reserved or account limit), the <strong>in-flight limit reached</strong> (NotVisible pinned, receives returning OverLimit), or a <strong>poison message consuming capacity</strong> (age climbing faster than depth, high receive counts). Watch for the feedback loop where retries from a slow downstream multiply the effective arrival rate and make the backlog grow faster than the traffic did.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Symptom 4: nothing is being consumed</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Check first:</strong> is <code>NumberOfMessagesReceived</code> zero, or non-zero with Deleted at zero? Those are different problems.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Received is zero.</strong> Nobody is even polling. Candidates: the <strong>Lambda event source mapping is disabled</strong> (check its State, which is a single describe call and an extremely common cause after a deploy or a manual mitigation that was never undone); consumers <strong>crashed on startup</strong> so they never reach the poll loop, which looks identical from the queue side; <strong>permissions</strong>, where the consumer role lost sqs:ReceiveMessage or, on an SSE-KMS queue, kms:Decrypt, producing AccessDenied that many poll loops swallow; or the <strong>wrong queue URL</strong>, typically a stale environment variable pointing at another environment or region, where the consumer is happily polling an empty queue.</li>\n<li><strong>Received is non-zero but Deleted is zero.</strong> Consumers are polling and failing. Look at consumer error logs and receive counts. Also consider a <strong>long visibility timeout hiding everything</strong>: if the timeout is twelve hours and processing fails, messages vanish from the visible count for twelve hours at a time, the queue looks empty, and the backlog is invisible in NotVisible. That mismatch between Visible near zero and NotVisible large is the tell.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Symptom 5: DLQ filling up</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Check first:</strong> the <code>ApproximateReceiveCount</code> on the DLQ messages and the error classes in consumer logs at the time of the last attempt.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">There are two fundamentally different stories and they need opposite responses. A <strong>real failure</strong> looks like: a logged exception with a consistent error class, receive counts hitting exactly maxReceiveCount, and a clustering by payload shape or by a specific downstream. A <strong>mis-sized visibility timeout</strong> looks like: no logged terminal errors at all, processing duration that overlaps the timeout, work that actually succeeded on one of the attempts, and messages arriving in the DLQ despite the side effect having happened. The discriminator is whether the consumer ever logged a completion for the message: if it did and the message still landed in the DLQ, the timeout expired before the delete, and redriving the message would duplicate the work rather than fix anything. Fix the timeout first, then redrive. Note also that <strong>DLQ messages retain the original enqueue timestamp</strong>, so a message moved to the DLQ near the end of its retention has very little life left, which argues for 14-day retention on the DLQ specifically.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Symptom 6: high cost</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Check first:</strong> <code>NumberOfEmptyReceives</code> versus <code>NumberOfMessagesReceived</code>, then requests per delivered message.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">In order of likelihood: <strong>empty receives</strong> from short polling (fix: ReceiveMessageWaitTimeSeconds of 20), <strong>no batching</strong> (fix: SendMessageBatch, MaxNumberOfMessages 10, DeleteMessageBatch, for a tenfold reduction), <strong>KMS requests</strong> from a short data key reuse period on an SSE-KMS queue, and an <strong>idle over-provisioned polling fleet</strong> whose compute cost usually exceeds its SQS cost. Then check the adjacent bill: NAT gateway data processing for consumers in private subnets without a VPC endpoint is a line item that never appears under SQS.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Symptom 7: FIFO throughput collapse</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Check first:</strong> the cardinality of MessageGroupId in recent messages, and whether high throughput mode is enabled on the queue.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">FIFO parallelism is bounded by the number of distinct <strong>message groups</strong>, because ordering is guaranteed within a group and only one message per group is in flight at a time. Three failure modes: <strong>too few groups</strong>, the classic being every message sent with the same group ID such as the tenant name or a constant, which serialises the entire queue no matter how many consumers you add; <strong>one group blocking</strong>, where a slow or poisoned message stalls its whole group while other groups flow, showing as age climbing with normal overall throughput; and <strong>high throughput mode not enabled</strong>, leaving the queue on the lower per-queue transaction ceiling. The fix for the first is a high-cardinality group key such as the entity ID rather than the tenant ID, with the group count comfortably above the consumer count. The fix for the second is a redrive policy so the blocking message is parked. The third is a queue attribute change to enable high throughput mode, which also changes the deduplication scope to the message group level.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Symptom 8: intermittent OverLimit or throttling errors</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Check first:</strong> <code>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesNotVisible</code> against the in-flight limit, and the SDK retry metrics or error codes.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two distinct limits produce similar-looking errors. It almost always means messages are being held too long, so the real causes are a visibility timeout far longer than processing time, consumers that received work and stalled, or a consumer that never deletes. The <strong>request-rate quota</strong> is per-API and per-region and produces throttling responses; the fix is batching, which reduces request count directly, plus exponential backoff with jitter, which every AWS SDK implements by default but which is often defeated by a custom retry loop wrapped around the SDK.
