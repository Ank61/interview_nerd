Designing an error handling architecture is a design exercise, and the difference shows up the first time something downstream degrades for twenty minutes. A well-designed failure path decides, for every message, whether the failure is worth retrying, how long to wait before the next attempt, when to give up, where the message goes when you give up, who finds out, and how the message gets replayed once the cause is fixed. Most production queue incidents are not caused by the original failure — they are caused by the system's reaction to it.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The taxonomy of failures drives everything</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Before any retry policy makes sense, you must classify failures. all you bought was five times the compute, five times the log volume, and five times the delay before a human sees it. Conversely, dead-lettering on the first downstream timeout turns a thirty-second blip into a manual replay exercise across thousands of messages. Classification is usually implemented as an exception hierarchy — a <code>PermanentError</code> versus a <code>RetryableError</code> — with the rule that <strong>unknown exceptions default to retryable but bounded</strong>, because assuming a new failure is permanent silently drops work.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Retry strategy: in-process versus back to the queue</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">There are two places a retry can happen and they suit different durations.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>In-process retry with exponential backoff and jitter.</strong> Appropriate only for very short blips — a single retry after 50 ms, maybe two, totalling well under a second. Jitter is mandatory: without it, a thousand consumers that all failed at the same instant retry at the same instant and reproduce the thundering herd that caused the failure. Full jitter (sleep a random duration between zero and the computed backoff) is the standard choice.</li>\n<li><strong>Return the message to the queue.</strong> For anything longer, this is almost always better. In-process waiting <strong>occupies a worker or an invocation for the entire sleep</strong>, so you are paying for and holding capacity to do nothing; with Lambda you are billed for the sleep, and with a fixed worker fleet you have reduced effective concurrency. Returning the message frees the worker to do useful work, spreads retries across the fleet and across time, and lets natural queue dynamics absorb the delay.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The rule of thumb to state in an interview: <strong>retry in-process for milliseconds, retry through the queue for seconds or minutes.</strong> The one exception is when a retry is cheap and the alternative is losing ordering — on a FIFO queue, returning a message to the queue stalls its whole message group, so a short in-process retry can be preferable there.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Visibility-timeout-based backoff: the idiomatic SQS mechanism</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SQS has no native per-message exponential backoff, but it gives you the two primitives to build one: <code>ApproximateReceiveCount</code> tells you which attempt this is, and <code>ChangeMessageVisibility</code> lets you extend the invisibility of a specific message up to 12 hours. Redrive also becomes impossible to target. A DLQ per queue costs nothing extra.</li>\n<li><strong>The DLQ must match the source queue type.</strong> A FIFO queue's DLQ must be FIFO; a standard queue's must be standard.</li>\n<li><strong>Set DLQ retention to the maximum of 14 days.</strong> The source queue might be four hours; the DLQ should be as long as SQS allows, because a dead letter that expires before a human looks at it is data loss with extra steps. Note SQS uses the <em>original enqueue timestamp</em> for retention, so a message that sat in the source queue for days has already spent part of that budget.</li>\n<li><strong>Alarming on DLQ depth is a required control, not a nice-to-have.</strong> The standard alarm is <code>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible &gt; 0</code> on the DLQ, evaluated over a short period, paging a human. A DLQ nobody is alarmed on is a queue where work goes to die silently. Pair it with <code>ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage</code> on the source queue, which catches a stalled consumer that is not producing dead letters at all.</li>\n<li><strong>Enrich before dead-lettering where you can.</strong> SQS does not let you modify the message on its way to the DLQ, so the durable pattern is to log a structured record (messageId, receive count, exception, stack, correlation id, consumer version) at the moment of final failure so the DLQ message can be joined back to diagnostics.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Redrive: use the managed message move task</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Historically every team wrote the same buggy script: receive from the DLQ, send to the source queue, delete from the DLQ — with no rate control, no resumability, and a window where a crash duplicates or loses messages. SQS now provides a managed <strong>message move task</strong> (<code>StartMessageMoveTask</code>, <code>ListMessageMoveTasks</code>, <code>CancelMessageMoveTask</code>, also exposed as DLQ redrive in the console). It moves messages from the DLQ back to the original source queue (or a specified destination), it is resumable and cancellable, and critically it accepts <strong>MaxNumberOfMessagesPerSecond</strong>, which is your rate control. Only one active task per source DLQ at a time.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The rate parameter is the whole point. <strong>Blind mass redrive is the classic self-inflicted second outage:</strong> you redrive 400,000 dead letters at full speed, the consumer scales hard, the downstream that was already fragile falls over again, and in minutes every one of those messages is back in the DLQ — now with a fresh receive count and a second incident. Redrive slowly, watch the DLQ drain rate and the downstream error rate, and be prepared to cancel.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">DLQ triage as an operational practice</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A DLQ is an inbox, not an archive. The discipline has four steps and the order matters.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Categorize.</strong> Sample the DLQ and group the failures. In practice you almost always find a small number of buckets: one deploy-related bug accounting for 90%, a handful of genuinely malformed producer messages, and a tail of transient failures that exhausted retries during an outage.</li>\n<li><strong>Fix the cause first.</strong> Redriving before the bug is fixed guarantees the messages die again. If the failure was transient, confirm the downstream is genuinely healthy — not merely no longer alarming.</li>\n<li><strong>Replay selectively.</strong> Move only the category you fixed, slowly. The malformed messages usually should never be replayed at all; they should be exported for analysis and deleted, with the producer fixed.</li>\n<li><strong>Close the loop.</strong> Every dead letter is a defect in either the producer, the consumer, or the retry policy. A DLQ with a permanent baseline of thousands of messages means the team has stopped treating it as an inbox, and the alarm has been normalized away.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Circuit breakers and deliberate consumer pausing</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">When a downstream dependency is hard down, continuing to pull messages is actively harmful: every message consumed is a message failed, receive counts climb, and you convert a temporary outage into a permanent dead-letter pile. The correct response is to <strong>stop consuming</strong>.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Mechanisms, from most to least managed: <strong>disable the Lambda event source mapping</strong> (or drop its <code>MaximumConcurrency</code> to 2) — messages accumulate untouched and, crucially, receive counts do not increment; <strong>scale an ECS or EC2 consumer service to zero</strong> or to a trickle; or implement an in-process circuit breaker that, once open, stops calling <code>ReceiveMessage</code> and sleeps, with half-open probes that let a single message through to test recovery. The SQS-specific nuance is that opening the breaker must mean <em>stop receiving</em>, not <em>receive and immediately fail</em> — the latter burns receive counts and dead-letters healthy work. Pair the breaker with an automatic reopen guard: never reopen straight to full concurrency, ramp it.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Poison-message quarantine</strong> is the complement. When a consumer detects a definitively permanent failure, it should not wait for <code>maxReceiveCount</code>: it should write the message and its diagnostic context to a quarantine store (a dedicated quarantine queue, or S3 plus a DynamoDB index) and delete it from the source queue immediately. This gets a known-bad message out of the retry cycle on attempt one instead of attempt five, keeps the DLQ meaningful, and makes analysis much easier because the quarantined record carries the reason.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The interaction that catches everyone: maxReceiveCount and visibility timeout</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">These two settings are coupled and the coupling is not obvious. If the visibility timeout is shorter than the actual processing time, a message becomes visible again <em>while it is still being processed successfully</em>. A second consumer picks it up, and the receive count increments on a message that is not failing. With a <code>maxReceiveCount</code> of 3 and a processing time twice the visibility timeout, <strong>healthy messages reach the DLQ on schedule</strong> while the work is also being done two or three times downstream.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The diagnostic signature is distinctive and worth memorizing: dead letters that process perfectly when replayed by hand, duplicate side effects downstream, no errors in the consumer logs, and <code>NumberOfMessagesDeleted</code> lower than <code>NumberOfMessagesReceived</code>. The remedies are to size the visibility timeout from the p99 processing time (not the mean), extend it with <code>ChangeMessageVisibility</code> heartbeats for genuinely long jobs, and set <code>maxReceiveCount</code> high enough — typically 3 to 5 — to tolerate ordinary transient failures without dead-lettering.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Interview scenario: the full failure path for a payment consumer</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A consumer reads <code>payment-capture</code> messages and calls a third-party payment gateway. A declined card, an invalid account, or a validation error is permanent — declines go to a business-outcome topic, not a retry loop. Anything unrecognized is treated as transient but bounded.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Retry shape.</strong> One in-process retry at 100 ms with full jitter for a single flaky call, then return the message with <code>ChangeMessageVisibility</code> backoff of 30s, 2m, 10m, 30m across attempts.
