{"title":"SQS vs Kafka — A Deep Architectural Comparison","tags":["kafka","architecture","comparison","event-streaming"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">\"SQS or Kafka\" is the most common architecture question in senior interviews and the one most often answered badly, because candidates recite a feature table instead of the model that generates the features. The honest senior answer starts from a single structural difference and derives everything else from it. <strong>SQS is a queue: a message is consumed and deleted.</strong> <strong>Kafka is a distributed, append-only, partitioned log: a record is written once, retained for a period, and each consumer group independently tracks its own position.</strong> Almost every other difference — replay, fan-out, ordering mechanics, scaling model, operational burden, cost shape — follows from that. If you can derive the table rather than memorise it, you can also answer the follow-ups, which is what the interviewer is actually testing.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Fundamental Model Difference</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">In SQS the <strong>unit of progress is a message</strong>. A consumer receives it, the message becomes invisible, and on success the consumer calls <code>DeleteMessage</code> with a receipt handle. The delete is the acknowledgement, it applies to exactly that one message, and after it the message is gone forever. This is a <strong>destructive read</strong>, and its purpose is <strong>work distribution</strong>: N workers share a pool of tasks, each task is done once, and no worker needs to know anything about the others.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">In Kafka the <strong>unit of progress is a position</strong>. A topic is split into partitions; each partition is an ordered, immutable sequence of records with monotonically increasing offsets. A consumer group is assigned partitions and commits an offset per partition meaning \"my group has processed up to here\". The records themselves are untouched by reading — they expire on a <strong>retention policy</strong> (time or size based), not on consumption. This is a <strong>non-destructive read</strong>, and its purpose is <strong>shared access to a durable history of events</strong>.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">SQS — destructive read, progress <i class=o>=</i> a message\n\n  producers <i class=o>-</i><i class=o>&gt;</i> [ m7 m6 m5 m4 m3 ] <i class=o>-</i><i class=o>&gt;</i> receive(m3) <i class=o>-</i><i class=o>&gt;</i> invisible <i class=o>-</i><i class=o>&gt;</i> <i class=k>delete</i>(m3) <i class=o>-</i><i class=o>&gt;</i> GONE\n                                            <i class=o>^</i>\n                                  visibility timeout expiry <i class=o>=</i> redeliver\n\nKafka — non<i class=o>-</i>destructive read, progress <i class=o>=</i> a position\n\n  <i class=k>partition</i> <i class=n>0</i>:  [ r0 r1 r2 r3 r4 r5 r6 r7 r8 r9 ... ]  (retained <i class=n>7</i> days)\n                               <i class=o>^</i><i class=k>group</i><i class=o>-</i>A committed<i class=o>=</i><i class=n>4</i>    <i class=o>^</i><i class=k>group</i><i class=o>-</i>B committed<i class=o>=</i><i class=n>9</i>\n                               <i class=o>^</i><i class=k>group</i><i class=o>-</i>C (<i class=k>new</i>) can <i class=k>start</i> <i class=k>at</i> <i class=n>0</i> <i class=k>and</i> read <i class=k>ALL</i> history\n                     <i class=k>seek</i>(<i class=n>2</i>) <i class=c>--&gt; reprocess r2..r9 without touching A or B</i></code></pre></div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Everything below is a consequence. Kafka can replay because the data is still there. Kafka supports many independent consumers because each keeps its own pointer into shared data. Kafka's parallelism is capped by partition count because a partition is the atomic unit of assignment. SQS has no replay because the data is deleted, needs SNS or EventBridge for fan-out because a delete is final, and scales without planning because there is nothing to assign.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">What the Log Model Buys You</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Replay and reprocessing.</strong> You ship a bug that miscomputes a field for six hours. With Kafka you fix the code, reset the consumer group's offsets to a timestamp, and reprocess — the events were never destroyed. This is the single most cited reason teams choose Kafka, and it is a genuine capability gap, not a preference.</li>\n<li><strong>Multiple independent consumer groups.</strong> The same <code>orders</code> topic feeds fulfilment, fraud scoring, the analytics warehouse loader and a search indexer, each at its own pace, each with its own lag, each able to fall behind and catch up without affecting the others. Adding a fifth consumer is a config change, not new infrastructure. With SQS each consumer needs its own queue fed by SNS or EventBridge, which works well but means the fan-out topology is something you provision and maintain.</li>\n<li><strong>Event sourcing and new read models from history.</strong> If the log is the system of record, you can build a brand-new materialised view six months later by replaying from the beginning — a new database, a new aggregation, a new ML feature store. That is impossible when the transport deletes on read; the best you can do is rebuild from an archive you thought to create in advance.</li>\n<li><strong>Stream processing with state, windowing and joins.</strong> Kafka Streams and Apache Flink do windowed aggregations, stream-stream and stream-table joins, and exactly-once state updates on top of the log, with the log itself providing the durability for recovery. SQS has no equivalent: a queue gives you one message at a time with no notion of position, so a stateful operator cannot recover its place after a crash.</li>\n<li><strong>Log compaction.</strong> A compacted topic retains at least the latest record per key indefinitely, discarding superseded ones. That turns a topic into a durable <strong>changelog</strong> — a replayable snapshot of current state per key — which is how Kafka Streams state stores, connector offsets and CDC topics work. There is no queue analogue at all.</li>\n<li><strong>Ordered history for auditing.</strong> A partition is an immutable, ordered record of what happened, which is useful for debugging and compliance in a way that a queue whose contents vanish is not.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">What the Queue Model Buys You</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Trivial per-message retry and DLQ semantics.</strong> Because progress is per message, a single poison message is naturally isolated: it is redelivered, its <code>ApproximateReceiveCount</code> increments, and at <code>maxReceiveCount</code> the redrive policy moves it to a DLQ while every other message flows on untouched. In Kafka progress is a single offset per partition, so one poison record blocks the partition until you skip it — and skipping means committing past it, which you must implement yourself (a dead-letter topic plus explicit error handling). Kafka's most common production incident is a stuck partition; SQS structurally cannot have one.</li>\n<li><strong>No partitions and no rebalancing to reason about.</strong> There is no assignment protocol, no group coordinator, no <code>max.poll.interval.ms</code> causing an eviction cascade, no stop-the-world pause while partitions move. Consumers are completely independent pollers. This removes an entire category of production incident and an entire chapter of tuning.</li>\n<li><strong>Elastic scaling with zero capacity planning.</strong> Standard SQS has effectively unlimited throughput; you add consumers and throughput rises, with no quantity to provision in advance and no ceiling imposed by a number you chose months ago. A traffic spike of 50x needs more compute, not a queue change. Kafka's consumer parallelism is capped by partition count, and partition count is a decision you make up front and change awkwardly.</li>\n<li><strong>Far smaller conceptual and operational surface.</strong> A new engineer can be productive against SQS in an hour: send, receive, delete, visibility timeout, DLQ. Kafka requires understanding partitions, keys, consumer groups, rebalance protocols, offset commit semantics, ISR and replication, retention and compaction, producer acks and idempotence, and serialization with a schema registry. That difference in cognitive load is a real engineering cost that experienced people weigh heavily.</li>\n<li><strong>Native per-message controls.</strong> Per-message visibility extension, <code>DelaySeconds</code>, per-message attributes and (in FIFO) a five-minute deduplication window are all first-class. Kafka has no per-record delivery controls of this kind.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Ordering and Parallelism, Side by Side</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Both systems reach the same insight — <strong>ordering and parallelism are the same knob</strong> — by different routes. You get order within a key and concurrency across keys, and the number of distinct keys is your maximum useful parallelism.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Kafka:</strong> the record key is hashed to a partition. A partition is assigned to exactly one consumer in a group, so <strong>consumer parallelism is capped at the partition count</strong>. Twelve partitions and thirteen consumers means one consumer sits idle. You can add partitions, but that rehashes future keys, so a key that used to land on partition 3 may now land on partition 7, and records for that key can be processed out of order across the boundary. In practice you over-provision partitions at design time and live with the cost (more open files, more replication traffic, longer rebalances, higher end-to-end latency at very high counts).</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>SQS FIFO:</strong> <code>MessageGroupId</code> is an arbitrary string with no configured cardinality. Messages in one group are delivered in order and the next is not released until the current one is deleted or its visibility expires; different groups proceed in parallel. There is no number to pick and no rehashing — you can use <code>customerId</code> with millions of distinct values and SQS simply parallelises across whatever groups are active. The cost is throughput limits: default FIFO quotas are far lower than standard SQS (historically 300 API calls per second per action, or 3,000 messages per second with batches of 10), and <strong>high throughput mode for FIFO</strong> raises this substantially — check the current service quotas page for the numbers in your region — plus a lower in-flight message limit than standard queues.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Rebalancing has no SQS equivalent.</strong> When a Kafka consumer joins, leaves or is deemed dead, the group re-assigns partitions. With the classic eager protocol this is stop-the-world: all consumers revoke everything and pause. Cooperative sticky assignment (and the newer consumer group protocol from KIP-848, which moves reconciliation server-side and became the default path in Kafka 4.0) reduce the pain considerably, but rebalancing still exists as a concept, a latency spike and a source of duplicate processing when a consumer is evicted mid-batch for exceeding <code>max.poll.interval.ms</code>. In SQS a consumer that dies simply stops polling; its in-flight messages become visible again after the timeout and any other consumer picks them up. Nothing coordinates, nothing pauses.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Delivery Semantics, Honestly</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>SQS Standard is at-least-once</strong> with best-effort ordering; duplicates occur because the delete may not land, or a visibility timeout may expire while work is still in progress. <strong>SQS FIFO</strong> adds exactly-once <em>processing</em> in the specific sense of a <strong>five-minute deduplication window</strong> keyed by <code>MessageDeduplicationId</code> (explicit or content-based): a resend inside that window is accepted and discarded. That is bounded deduplication, not a general exactly-once guarantee — a retry six minutes later produces a second message, and a consumer whose visibility expires mid-work can still see the same message twice.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Kafka is at-least-once by default</strong> (with <code>enable.idempotence</code> on the producer preventing duplicates from producer retries within a session). Kafka additionally offers <strong>transactional exactly-once</strong>: a producer can atomically write records to multiple partitions and commit consumer offsets in the same transaction, and a consumer with <code>isolation.level=read_committed</code> sees only committed records. This is genuinely exactly-once for <strong>read-process-write pipelines whose input, output and offsets all live in Kafka</strong> — which is what Kafka Streams uses. The crucial caveat, and the one interviewers listen for: <strong>it does not extend to external side effects</strong>. If your consumer charges a credit card, writes to DynamoDB or calls a partner API, that action is outside the Kafka transaction and can happen twice if the process dies after the side effect but before the commit. So in both systems, for any real-world side effect, <strong>the consumer must be idempotent</strong>. The right framing is: exactly-once is a property of your consumer design, not a checkbox on a broker.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Operational Reality</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This is where the comparison becomes concrete, and where the queue model earns its keep. Running Kafka — self-managed or on <strong>MSK provisioned</strong> — means owning a list of ongoing responsibilities:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Partition count planning</strong> per topic, a decision that is easy to increase and effectively impossible to decrease, and that determines your parallelism ceiling for years.</li>\n<li><strong>Consumer lag monitoring</strong> as a primary SLI, with alerting per group per partition, because lag is the only early warning that something is stuck.</li>\n<li><strong>Broker sizing and storage management</strong>: instance types, EBS volumes, retention policy versus disk growth, and the classic outage where a topic's retention was raised and a broker filled its disk. Tiered storage moves older segments to cheaper storage and eases this, but it is another thing to configure.</li>\n<li><strong>Replication health</strong>: replication factor, <code>min.insync.replicas</code>, under-replicated partitions, leader skew and preferred-leader election after a broker replacement.</li>\n<li><strong>Rebalancing incidents</strong>: slow consumers evicted for exceeding <code>max.poll.interval.ms</code>, which triggers a rebalance, which slows other consumers, which get evicted — a real failure mode with a real pager.</li>\n<li><strong>Version and metadata management</strong>: modern Kafka uses <strong>KRaft</strong> for metadata, with ZooKeeper removed in Kafka 4.0, so older clusters carry a migration in their future.</li>\n<li><strong>Client tuning and schema governance</strong>: batch size, linger, compression, acks, plus a schema registry and a compatibility policy if you want producers and consumers to evolve independently.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SQS has essentially none of this. There is no cluster, no version, no partition count, no lag-per-partition, no replication setting, no disk. The operational surface is: pick a visibility timeout, configure a DLQ and <code>maxReceiveCount</code>, set retention, and alarm on <code>ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage</code> and DLQ depth. <strong>MSK Serverless</strong> is the middle ground: it removes broker sizing, storage management and capacity provisioning while keeping the Kafka API, partitions, consumer groups and rebalancing — so it eliminates roughly half the list above, at the cost of its own quotas and some configuration restrictions relative to a provisioned cluster. If a team wants Kafka's model without broker operations, that is the honest recommendation.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cost Shape and the Crossover</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The shapes are fundamentally different, which is why \"which is cheaper\" has no answer without a volume. <strong>SQS is pure per-request</strong>: you pay per API call (roughly &#36;0.40 per million requests for standard and &#36;0.50 per million for FIFO in us-east-1 — verify on the pricing page), with a perpetual free tier, and <strong>zero cost at zero traffic</strong>. Batching up to 10 messages per call and long polling are the main levers; a naive short-polling consumer can spend more on empty receives than on real work. <strong>Kafka is provisioned capacity</strong>: MSK charges per broker-hour plus storage (plus data transfer), so you pay the same at 3 a.m. on a Sunday as at peak, and there is a substantial floor for a production-grade multi-AZ cluster before you send a single message. MSK Serverless shifts this partly toward usage-based pricing.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The consequence: at low and moderate volume SQS is dramatically cheaper because the Kafka floor dominates. As volume grows, SQS cost scales linearly with request count while the Kafka cluster's cost is roughly flat until you add brokers — so there is a <strong>crossover</strong>, typically somewhere in the high hundreds of millions to billions of messages per month for a small cluster, after which sustained high-volume streaming favours Kafka. Two caveats keep this honest. First, batching moves the crossover a long way to the right: ten messages per API call cuts SQS request cost by an order of magnitude. Second, and more important, <strong>infrastructure cost is usually not the dominant term</strong> — the engineering time to operate Kafka, and the incidents it generates, routinely exceed the line item. Choosing Kafka to save money at moderate volume is almost always a mistake; choosing it for replay, fan-out or stream processing is a good reason that happens to also pay off at scale.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem;
