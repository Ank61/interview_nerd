{"title":"Observability for Queue-Based Systems","tags":["observability","cloudwatch","distributed-tracing","slo"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">A synchronous service is easy to observe: a request arrives, something happens, a response goes back, and one trace covers the whole thing. A queue deliberately breaks that. The producer returns successfully long before the work happens, the consumer runs in a different process at a different time, and the causal link between the two exists only if you deliberately carry it. Observability for queue-based systems is therefore about three things: choosing metrics that describe <em>health</em> rather than <em>volume</em>, defining objectives on end-to-end latency rather than on queue internals, and preserving causality across the queue boundary so that one message can be followed from enqueue to completion.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The metric model</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SQS publishes metrics to CloudWatch automatically at one-minute granularity at no additional charge. The ones that matter:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible.</strong> The backlog: messages available to be received right now. Gauge, not counter.</li>\n<li><strong>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesNotVisible.</strong> Messages currently in flight, that is, received but neither deleted nor expired back to visible. This is your concurrency observation: if it is pinned near the in-flight limit, receives will start failing.</li>\n<li><strong>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesDelayed.</strong> Messages held by a queue delay or a per-message DelaySeconds. A surprising value here usually means someone set a queue-level delay and forgot.</li>\n<li><strong>ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage.</strong> How long the oldest undeleted message has been in the queue, in seconds. This is the single best health indicator.</li>\n<li><strong>NumberOfMessagesSent, NumberOfMessagesReceived, NumberOfMessagesDeleted.</strong> Counters. Sent is arrival rate, Deleted is the true completion rate, and Received includes retries so it exceeds Deleted whenever redelivery is happening.</li>\n<li><strong>NumberOfEmptyReceives.</strong> Receives that returned nothing. Purely a cost and configuration signal.</li>\n<li><strong>SentMessageSize.</strong> Useful for cost modelling and for catching a producer that started shipping large payloads.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Why age beats depth for alerting.</strong> Depth is meaningless without knowing throughput. A queue holding 50,000 messages that drains at 5,000 per second is healthy; a queue holding 200 messages that has not moved in an hour is an outage. Depth also alarms constantly on bursty workloads, so teams raise the threshold until the alarm is useless. Age answers the question the business actually asks, which is how stale the work is, it is unit-comparable to an SLO expressed in seconds, and it catches the two failure modes depth misses: a slow trickle that never accumulates but never completes, and a single poison message that is redelivered forever while the rest of the queue flows normally. That last case is diagnostic: if age keeps climbing while depth stays near zero and NumberOfMessagesReceived greatly exceeds NumberOfMessagesDeleted, you are looking at a message that cannot be processed and cannot be deleted.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Derive the rates you need with metric math rather than instrumenting them yourself. Arrival rate is <code>NumberOfMessagesSent</code> Sum over the period divided by the period in seconds. Completion rate is <code>NumberOfMessagesDeleted</code> Sum divided by the period. The <strong>retry ratio</strong> is Received divided by Deleted, which should sit near 1.0; at 1.5 you are reprocessing half your messages. The <strong>empty-receive ratio</strong> is EmptyReceives divided by EmptyReceives plus Received, which points straight at short polling or an over-provisioned fleet.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">SLO thinking for queues</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The mistake teams make is writing an SLO like \"queue depth below 1,000\". That is an implementation detail, not a user-facing promise, and it is unstable under traffic growth. The right objective is on <strong>end-to-end latency</strong>: the time from the business event being enqueued to the work being completed, for example \"99 percent of order confirmations complete within 60 seconds of order placement\". That objective survives refactors, is meaningful to the business, and maps naturally onto the age metric as a leading indicator.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The operator-facing number that predicts recovery is <strong>drain time</strong>. Given a backlog <code>B</code>, a completion rate <code>C</code> and an arrival rate <code>A</code>, the queue drains in roughly <code>B / (C - A)</code> seconds, and if <code>C</code> is less than or equal to <code>A</code> it never drains without intervention. This is the number to put on the dashboard and in the incident channel, because \"backlog 4.2 million\" tells nobody anything, whereas \"draining in 38 minutes at current rate\" tells everybody whether to scale up, page the downstream team, or wait. You can compute it entirely in CloudWatch metric math and alarm on it directly, which makes the alarm robust to traffic growth in a way that a fixed depth threshold never is.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>yaml</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\"><i class=s>arrival</i> <i class=s>rate</i> <i class=s>A</i> <i class=s>----&gt;</i> [  <i class=s>QUEUE</i>  <i class=s>backlog</i> <i class=s>B</i>  ] <i class=s>----&gt;</i> <i class=s>completion</i> <i class=s>rate</i> <i class=s>C</i>\n                        <i class=s>|</i>                              <i class=s>|\n                  Visible + NotVisible            NumberOfMessagesDeleted / 60\n                        |\n</i>               <i class=s>AgeOfOldestMessage</i>  <i class=s>&lt;--</i> <i class=s>alert</i> <i class=s>on</i> <i class=s>THIS,</i> <i class=s>not</i> <i class=s>on</i> <i class=s>B</i>\n\n <i class=s>drain_time</i> <i class=s>=</i> <i class=s>B</i> <i class=s>/</i> <i class=s>(C</i> <i class=y>-</i> <i class=s>A)</i>        <i class=s>C</i> <i class=s>&gt;</i> <i class=a>A :</i> <i class=s>recovering,</i> <i class=s>report</i> <i class=s>the</i> <i class=s>ETA</i>\n                                 <i class=s>C</i> <i class=s>=</i> <i class=a>A :</i> <i class=s>stable</i> <i class=s>but</i> <i class=s>never</i> <i class=s>catching</i> <i class=s>up</i>\n                                 <i class=s>C</i> <i class=s>&lt;</i> <i class=a>A :</i> <i class=s>diverging,</i> <i class=s>backlog</i> <i class=s>grows</i> <i class=s>without</i> <i class=s>bound</i></code></pre></div>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; Set <code>TreatMissingData</code> deliberately: SQS stops publishing some metrics when a queue is completely idle, so an alarm set to treat missing data as breaching will page at 3am on a quiet queue, while one set to notBreaching can hide a total stall. For the stalled-consumer alarm, use <strong>notBreaching on the age metric but treat the missing Deleted metric explicitly</strong>, and prefer M-of-N datapoints over a single period to avoid flapping on one-minute noise. Note also that Lambda <code>IteratorAge</code> does not apply to SQS event source mappings; that metric is for stream sources. For SQS-backed Lambda the backlog indicator is the queue age metric, not an Lambda metric.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Distributed tracing through a queue</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This is the hard part. A naive trace breaks at the queue because the producer segment closes when SendMessage returns; the consumer starts a brand new trace minutes later with no knowledge of its cause. You end up with two unrelated traces and no way to answer \"what happened to the order the customer placed at 14:02\".</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">There are two mechanisms, and mature systems use both:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>The AWSTraceHeader system attribute.</strong> SQS provides a reserved message system attribute named <code>AWSTraceHeader</code> that carries an X-Ray trace header through the queue. Set it with <code>MessageSystemAttributes</code> on send and request it with <code>MessageSystemAttributeNames</code> on receive. It does not count against your ten user-defined message attributes and the Lambda event source mapping understands it, so the consumer segment joins the producer trace. This is the lowest-effort path if you are on X-Ray.</li>\n<li><strong>A correlation ID in a user message attribute.</strong> Independently of any tracing vendor, carry a stable business correlation identifier (order ID, request ID, W3C traceparent) as a message attribute and log it on both sides. This survives vendor changes, works when a message crosses an account or a third party, and is what you will actually grep at 3am. Note that message attributes count toward the 256 KB message size limit and you are limited to ten user-defined attributes, so keep the set small and disciplined.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Conceptually, the causal chain you are reconstructing is producer segment, then a link across the queue, then consumer segment, with the queue wait time as the gap. Modern OpenTelemetry semantics model this as a <strong>span link</strong> rather than a parent-child relationship, precisely because the consumer may process a batch of ten messages originating from ten different traces. If you make the batch span a child of one of them you get a misleading tree; if you use links you get an honest one. Expect this nuance to come up with a strong interviewer.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Structured logging conventions</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Agree a fixed field set across every consumer so queries work across services: <code>queue_name</code>, <code>message_id</code>, <code>correlation_id</code>, <code>receive_count</code>, <code>enqueue_ts</code>, <code>queue_wait_ms</code>, <code>processing_ms</code>, <code>outcome</code> (success, retryable_failure, permanent_failure) and <code>error_class</code>. Emit exactly one such line per message, not per stage, so counting is trivial.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The highest-value field is <strong>ApproximateReceiveCount</strong>, available as a system attribute on every received message. Logging it makes retries visible, which otherwise are completely invisible: without it a message processed four times looks like four unrelated successes. A histogram of receive count instantly separates \"everything is being retried once\" (visibility timeout slightly too short) from \"one message is on attempt 47\" (poison message) from \"healthy\". Also log <code>ApproximateFirstReceiveTimestamp</code> if you care about distinguishing queue wait from retry wait.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Measuring true end-to-end latency</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">CloudWatch cannot tell you end-to-end latency, because it does not know when your processing finished. Measure it yourself. The cheap version needs no producer change at all: request the <code>SentTimestamp</code> system attribute on receive and subtract it from the current time to get exact queue wait time in milliseconds. The complete version stamps a user message attribute with the <strong>original business event time</strong> at the edge, before the message was enqueued, and the consumer emits <code>now - event_time</code> as a custom metric when the work completes. That number, not queue depth, is what your SLO is written against; publish it as a percentile metric with the Embedded Metric Format so you get percentiles without a separate PutMetricData call per message.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Investigation walkthrough: one lost-looking message</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Support reports that order <code>ORD-88421</code>, placed at 14:02, never produced a confirmation email.
