{"title":"SQS Cost Optimization at Scale","tags":["cost-optimization","batching","long-polling","kms"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">SQS looks almost free until it is not. The bill is driven by a unit most engineers never think about: the <strong>API request</strong>, not the message. A queue moving a billion messages a month can cost tens of dollars or several thousand dollars depending entirely on how the client code is written. At SDE-3 and SRE level you are expected to be able to open a cost report, point at the three or four levers that matter, and do the arithmetic out loud. This topic covers the billing model precisely, the levers in priority order, the hidden costs that show up on adjacent line items, and how to attribute and monitor messaging spend.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The billing model, precisely</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Amazon SQS charges per API request. Every <code>SendMessage</code>, <code>SendMessageBatch</code>, <code>ReceiveMessage</code>, <code>DeleteMessage</code>, <code>DeleteMessageBatch</code>, <code>ChangeMessageVisibility</code> and every other call is a billable request. Three details change everything:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>A request can carry up to ten messages.</strong> A batch send of ten messages is one request. A <code>ReceiveMessage</code> that returns ten messages is one request. A batch delete of ten receipt handles is one request. So the unit that matters for cost is requests, and the naive one-message-per-call pattern pays ten times more than it needs to.</li>\n<li><strong>Payload is billed in 64 KB chunks.</strong> Each 64 KB chunk of a request payload counts as one billable request. A single call carrying a 256 KB payload is billed as four requests. This is why batching ten large messages does not give a full tenfold saving, and why it is worth knowing your average message size before you promise a cost reduction.</li>\n<li><strong>FIFO requests cost more than standard requests.</strong> At US-region list price, standard requests have historically been priced around &#36;0.40 per million and FIFO around &#36;0.50 per million, with tiered discounts as monthly volume grows, and a perpetual free tier of one million requests per month. Always re-check the pricing page before quoting numbers, but the structural point is stable: FIFO carries roughly a 25 percent per-request premium.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Equally important is what you are <em>not</em> charged for. There is <strong>no storage charge</strong> for messages and no charge that scales with retention duration. Setting <code>MessageRetentionPeriod</code> to fourteen days instead of four days costs nothing extra. Engineers frequently shorten retention hoping to save money; it saves nothing and destroys your ability to replay after an incident.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The two dimensions that sit outside the request meter are <strong>data transfer</strong> and <strong>KMS requests</strong>. Data transfer into SQS is free; data transfer out to the internet or across regions is charged at normal AWS rates. In-region traffic to the SQS endpoint is not charged by SQS itself, but the network path you choose to reach that public endpoint absolutely is (see the NAT gateway discussion below). If the queue uses <strong>SSE-KMS</strong> with a customer managed key, every data key operation is a charged KMS request, and on a busy queue that can become a larger line item than SQS itself.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Lever 1: batching sends and deletes</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Take a workload of one billion messages per month. The unbatched path costs one send, one receive and one delete per message: three billion requests. At &#36;0.40 per million that is roughly &#36;1,200 per month before volume tiering. Switch to <code>SendMessageBatch</code> with ten messages per call, let <code>ReceiveMessage</code> return its maximum of ten, and use <code>DeleteMessageBatch</code>: one hundred million sends, one hundred million receives, one hundred million deletes, or three hundred million requests, roughly &#36;120. Same message volume, one tenth the bill.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The engineering cost of batching is real and you should name it in an interview: batching on the send side requires an accumulator with a size trigger and a time trigger (flush at ten messages or at fifty milliseconds, whichever comes first), and it introduces partial-failure handling because <code>SendMessageBatch</code> returns per-entry <code>Successful</code> and <code>Failed</code> arrays. A batch send is not atomic. On the delete side, batching is nearly free: you already have the receipt handles from a batched receive, so deleting them together requires no extra buffering.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Lever 2: long polling and the empty-receive tax</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">With short polling (<code>WaitTimeSeconds</code> of zero), a <code>ReceiveMessage</code> call returns immediately, frequently with zero messages. That empty response is a billable request. A consumer loop that polls as fast as it can on an idle queue is a machine for converting nothing into money.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Worked comparison. One hundred worker threads, each issuing ten short-poll receives per second against a mostly idle queue: 1,000 requests per second, about 2.59 billion requests in a thirty-day month, roughly &#36;1,037 per month of pure empty receives, on a queue carrying almost no traffic. Now set <code>WaitTimeSeconds</code> to 20. An idle worker makes at most three calls per minute: 100 workers times 3 times 60 times 24 times 30 is about 13 million requests, roughly &#36;5. The idle fleet just became two hundred times cheaper, and latency improved rather than degraded, because long polling returns as soon as a message arrives rather than waiting for the next poll tick.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Enable long polling in two places: set <code>ReceiveMessageWaitTimeSeconds</code> on the queue so it is the default even for clients you do not control, and pass <code>WaitTimeSeconds=20</code> explicitly in the receive call. Make sure the SDK socket timeout exceeds the wait time, or you will get spurious client-side timeouts that look like a service problem.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Lever 3: right-sizing the poll loop</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The classic waste is a fleet sized for peak that polls at peak rate all day. Two hundred containers each running eight polling threads is 1,600 concurrent long polls; even at 20 seconds each that is 80 requests per second of pure overhead, around 207 million requests per month, before a single message is processed. The fix is not micro-tuning the sleep: it is decoupling fleet size from the poll rate. Scale the consumer fleet on <strong>ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage</strong> or on backlog per worker so the idle fleet shrinks, and cap concurrent pollers per instance rather than per thread. A queue that is empty most of the day should have a handful of pollers, not sixteen hundred.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Lever 4: the KMS data key reuse period</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">When a queue uses SSE-KMS, SQS calls <code>GenerateDataKey</code> to get a data key for encryption and <code>Decrypt</code> to unwrap it for reads. It caches that data key for the <strong>KmsDataKeyReusePeriodSeconds</strong> window, which ranges from 60 seconds to 86,400 seconds (24 hours) and defaults to 300 seconds. Roughly speaking you pay one KMS request per reuse window per queue per calling context, rather than one per message. Moving from the 60-second minimum to the 24-hour maximum reduces KMS request volume by up to about 1,440 times. At the usual KMS rate of a few cents per ten thousand requests this is the difference between a rounding error and a four-figure monthly line item on a very busy multi-tenant queue.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The trade-off is not purely financial and you should say so: a longer reuse period means a plaintext data key lives in the SQS service cache longer, and a KMS key disable or grant revocation takes longer to take effect on the data path. Most teams land on the maximum for high-volume internal queues and something much shorter for queues carrying regulated data where fast revocation matters.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Lever 5: consolidating chatty small messages</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">If producers emit one tiny message per row, per click or per metric sample, the request count is dominated by message count rather than by data volume. Coalescing at the producer, for example one message carrying fifty row identifiers instead of fifty messages, cuts requests by another order of magnitude on top of batching. Watch the 64 KB chunk rule and the 256 KB maximum message size: the sweet spot is messages comfortably under 64 KB, batched ten at a time. Above 256 KB you need the Extended Client Library with an S3 payload offload, which trades SQS requests for S3 requests and storage.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Hidden costs people miss</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>The Lambda event source mapping polls on your dime.</strong> Lambda runs its own pollers against the queue. That polling is not billed as Lambda invocations, but the <code>ReceiveMessage</code> calls the poller makes are part of your SQS request picture. A Lambda-backed queue with almost no traffic still shows a steady baseline of receives. You do not control the poll loop directly, so the levers are queue-level long polling, a sensible batch size, and batching window.</li>\n<li><strong>DLQ traffic is real traffic.</strong> Every redrive attempt is a receive plus a send to the DLQ, and a poison-message storm can multiply request volume. A message with <code>maxReceiveCount</code> of 100 costs a hundred receives before it lands in the DLQ. Low single-digit or low double-digit redrive limits are cheaper as well as more operationally sane.</li>\n<li><strong>Over-provisioned consumer fleets.</strong> The compute bill for idle pollers usually dwarfs the SQS bill for their empty receives. Count both.</li>\n<li><strong>NAT gateway data processing.</strong> This is the genuinely common surprise. SQS is a regional public endpoint. Consumers in private subnets reach it through a NAT gateway, which charges an hourly rate per gateway plus a per-GB processing charge on every byte in both directions. A workload pushing a terabyte a month through NAT pays tens of dollars in processing plus roughly thirty dollars per gateway per month, per availability zone. An <strong>interface VPC endpoint</strong> for SQS keeps the traffic on the AWS network, removes the NAT hop entirely, and is typically far cheaper per GB, while also improving the security posture. Teams discover this when the NAT line item, not the SQS line item, is what grew.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">FIFO versus standard as a cost decision</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">FIFO costs more per request and constrains throughput in ways that raise the effective cost further: ordering is preserved per <strong>message group</strong>, so a queue with few groups cannot use parallelism well, and without high-throughput mode enabled the per-queue transaction ceiling is low enough that you may need multiple queues. If your real requirement is merely that two updates to the same entity do not land out of order, a standard queue with a per-entity version number, an idempotency key and a last-writer-wins check in the datastore is usually cheaper, faster and more elastic. Reserve FIFO for cases where the business genuinely cannot tolerate reordering or duplicates and you are unwilling to build idempotency.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cost attribution and anomaly detection</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Tag every queue at creation with owner, service, environment and cost-center, activate those as cost allocation tags in Billing, and you can slice messaging spend per team in Cost Explorer. For per-queue precision, use the Cost and Usage Report: the usage-type dimension distinguishes standard from FIFO requests and separates data transfer, and combining it with resource-level detail plus the CloudWatch <code>NumberOfMessagesSent</code> and <code>NumberOfEmptyReceives</code> metrics per queue lets you rank queues by requests per useful message. That ratio, requests divided by messages delivered, is the single best cost-efficiency KPI for a queue: a well-batched, long-polling queue sits near 0.3; an unbatched short-polling queue can sit in the hundreds. Add an AWS Cost Anomaly Detection monitor scoped to the SQS and KMS services so a deploy that accidentally disables batching pages someone the next day rather than at month end.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem;
