{"title":"Migration and Interoperability — Moving Between SQS, RabbitMQ, and Kafka","tags":["migration","rabbitmq","kafka","amazon-mq"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Messaging migrations are among the highest-risk projects an infrastructure team takes on, because the queue is usually the one component that every service touches and the one place where losing a message means losing a customer order. Interviewers probe this area because it separates people who have read a feature comparison from people who have actually moved traffic: the hard part is never the SDK call, it is the <strong>semantic gap</strong> between two brokers that look superficially similar and behave very differently under failure. This topic walks through why migrations happen in both directions, which semantics do not translate, the four strategies that actually work in production, how to prove parity, and how to plan a rollback you will hopefully never use.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Why Migrations Happen — In Both Directions</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">There is no single arrow of progress here. Teams move toward SQS and away from SQS for good reasons, and a strong answer names both.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Self-managed broker to SQS — shedding operational burden.</strong> A three-node RabbitMQ cluster with mirrored or quorum queues means someone owns disk alarms, memory watermarks, partition handling, version upgrades, TLS certificate rotation and a 3 a.m. page when a node splits. SQS has no cluster, no version, no capacity to provision and no patching window. Teams making this move are almost always trading features they do not use for on-call sleep.</li>\n<li><strong>SQS to Kafka — replay, multiple consumers, sustained throughput.</strong> Three requirements reliably push a team off SQS. First, <strong>replay</strong>: a bug corrupts a downstream store and you need to reprocess last Tuesday, which SQS simply cannot do because consumed messages are deleted. Second, <strong>multiple independent consumers</strong> of the same stream: with SQS you need SNS or EventBridge fan-out to N queues, and every new consumer is a new piece of infrastructure, whereas in Kafka it is a new consumer group reading an existing topic. Third, <strong>sustained very high throughput</strong> where per-request pricing and per-message API overhead start to dominate cost and latency compared to batched sequential log reads.</li>\n<li><strong>Kafka to SQS — right-sizing.</strong> Plenty of teams adopted Kafka for a workload that is really a work queue: one producer, one consumer group, no replay, modest volume. They are paying for partition planning, rebalancing incidents, consumer lag dashboards and broker sizing to get behavior SQS gives away. Migrating down is a legitimate and underrated move.</li>\n<li><strong>Multi-cloud and portability pressure.</strong> Kafka and RabbitMQ are open protocols you can run anywhere; SQS is an AWS API. Organizations with a hard multi-cloud mandate, or an ISV shipping software that customers self-host, often cannot take the AWS-only dependency regardless of the operational cost.</li>\n<li><strong>Compliance and data residency.</strong> Occasionally the driver is that a regulator requires the message store to sit inside a specific boundary the team controls, which argues for self-managed or Amazon MQ in a specific region rather than a service abstraction.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Semantic Gaps You Must Bridge</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This is where candidates get caught. Each broker has a different model of what \"the consumer got the message\" means, and a naive port silently changes your failure behavior.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>RabbitMQ acknowledgements and prefetch versus SQS visibility timeout and delete.</strong> In RabbitMQ the broker pushes messages over a long-lived AMQP channel. Delivery is bounded by <code>basic.qos</code> prefetch — the maximum number of unacknowledged messages the broker will push to one channel. A message stays unacknowledged indefinitely while the consumer works; there is no timer. If the TCP connection drops, every unacknowledged message on that channel is immediately requeued. In SQS the consumer pulls, and redelivery is driven by a <strong>clock</strong>: the visibility timeout. Exceed it and the message is redelivered even though your consumer is still happily working on it. The port therefore has two traps: (1) a long-running handler that was safe under AMQP now produces duplicate concurrent processing unless you extend visibility with <code>ChangeMessageVisibility</code>; (2) prefetch-based backpressure disappears — SQS concurrency is controlled by how many messages you request and how many threads you run, so you must rebuild the bound explicitly. Conversely a crashed SQS consumer does not release its message instantly; you wait out the visibility timeout, so failover latency gets worse, not better.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Exchanges and routing keys have no SQS equivalent.</strong> RabbitMQ's real power is the exchange: direct, topic, fanout and headers exchanges with bindings evaluated broker-side, so <code>order.created.us-west</code> can land in three queues by pattern. SQS has exactly one primitive — a queue — and no routing at all. Routing must move somewhere:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>SNS</strong> replaces fanout and simple direct exchanges, with subscription filter policies giving attribute-based matching that covers many topic-exchange cases.</li>\n<li><strong>EventBridge</strong> replaces topic and headers exchanges properly: content-based rules match on the message body, not just attributes, which is the closest managed analogue to a topic exchange, plus archive and replay.</li>\n<li><strong>The application</strong> takes the rest — a dispatcher consuming one queue and re-publishing, which is the fallback when rules get genuinely complex, at the cost of an extra hop.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Kafka offsets and consumer groups versus SQS per-message deletion.</strong> This is the gap that cannot be bridged, only compensated for. Kafka reads are non-destructive: a consumer group holds a position in an immutable log, and resetting that position re-reads history. SQS reads are destructive: <code>DeleteMessage</code> removes the message and nothing brings it back. So a Kafka-to-SQS migration must <strong>rebuild replay as an explicit archive</strong> — typically the producer also writes every event to S3 (partitioned by date and event type, via Kinesis Data Firehose or a direct put), and \"replay\" becomes a batch job that reads S3 and re-publishes to the queue. State that plainly in an interview: you are not replicating the feature, you are re-implementing it, and the replay path needs its own idempotency story because re-publishing creates duplicates by construction.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Partitions versus FIFO message groups.</strong> Both give you \"ordering within a key, parallelism across keys\", but the mechanics differ. A Kafka topic has a fixed partition count; the key hashes to a partition; a consumer group assigns whole partitions to members, so <strong>consumer parallelism is capped at the partition count</strong> and adding partitions rehashes future keys. SQS FIFO has no fixed count: <code>MessageGroupId</code> is an arbitrary string, there is no configured limit you plan for, and parallelism scales with the number of distinct active groups. Migrating Kafka to FIFO is usually the easy direction — the partition key becomes the group id and the cap disappears. Migrating FIFO to Kafka is the hard direction: you must pick a partition count up front, and if your group cardinality is millions of customer ids, that is fine (they hash down), but if it is four tenants, you have created a permanent hot-partition problem.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Priorities, TTL and delay.</strong> RabbitMQ supports per-queue max-priority with priority-ordered delivery, per-message and per-queue TTL, dead-letter exchanges with routing, and delayed delivery via a plugin. SQS has none of the priority machinery: you emulate priority with separate queues (a high queue and a low queue, polled with weighted attention), which is coarser but operationally simpler. TTL maps only partially — SQS has a queue-wide retention period (up to 14 days) and no per-message expiry, so per-message TTL becomes an application check on a timestamp attribute at consume time. Delay maps to <code>DelaySeconds</code> but caps at 15 minutes, so longer scheduling moves to EventBridge Scheduler or Step Functions <code>Wait</code> states.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Message size.</strong> SQS carries small messages (256 KiB has been the long-standing documented maximum — check the current service quotas page before you design to it), and the Extended Client Library transparently stores larger payloads in S3 with a pointer in the message. Kafka's <code>message.max.bytes</code> is configurable and commonly raised to 1 MB or more; RabbitMQ tolerates large messages but suffers for it. A Kafka topic carrying 2 MB records cannot be lifted to SQS without adopting the claim-check pattern, which changes the consumer contract (the consumer now needs S3 read permission and must handle a missing object).</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Migration Strategies</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Four patterns cover essentially every real migration, and they compose.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>1. Dual-write with dual-read and a cutover.</strong> Producers publish every message to both the old and new system. Initially only the old consumer acts; the new consumer runs in <strong>shadow mode</strong>, doing everything except the side effects (or writing to a shadow table). You compare outputs, then flip a feature flag so the new consumer becomes authoritative and the old one goes into shadow. Finally you stop dual-writing. The strength is a clean, fast rollback at every stage. The weakness is that dual-write is not atomic: a process that publishes to Kafka and then crashes before publishing to SQS has diverged the two systems. Mitigate with the <strong>outbox pattern</strong> — write the event to a database table in the same transaction as the business change, and let a relay publish to both — or accept divergence and reconcile.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>2. Strangler pattern — one consumer at a time.</strong> Rather than moving the whole topology, you move consumers individually. Both brokers stay live; each consumer is cut over independently and can be reverted independently. This is the right default when many teams consume the same stream, because it lets each team schedule its own risk. It requires the message to be available in both systems, which means either dual-write or a bridge.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>3. A bridge (forwarder) during transition.</strong> A small stateless service consumes from A and publishes to B, preserving ordering key and message id. This is the cheapest option when you cannot change producers — legacy services, third-party publishers, or a very large fleet. Bridges are attractive because producers are untouched, and dangerous because the bridge is now a single point of failure, adds latency, and is itself an at-least-once component that will duplicate on retry. Make the bridge stateless, run it with at least two instances, propagate the original message id as a deduplication key, and monitor its lag as a first-class SLI. Bridges should be explicitly temporary, with a deletion date in the plan.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">Phase <i class=n>1</i> — shadow            Phase <i class=n>2</i> — dual<i class=o>-</i>read         Phase <i class=n>3</i> — cutover\nProducer                    Producer                    Producer\n   <i class=o>|</i>                           <i class=o>|</i>                          <i class=o>|</i>\n   v                           v   v                       v\nRabbitMQ <i class=c>--bridge--&gt; SQS    RabbitMQ   SQS              SQS only</i>\n   <i class=o>|</i>                  <i class=o>|</i>        <i class=o>|</i>        <i class=o>|</i>                  <i class=o>|</i>\n <i class=k>old</i> consumer    shadow     <i class=k>old</i> (auth) <i class=k>new</i> (shadow)    <i class=k>new</i> consumer\n (authoritative) (<i class=k>no</i> side     <i class=o>|</i>         <i class=o>|</i>              (authoritative)\n                  effects)  compare outputs            <i class=k>old</i> stack deleted</code></pre></div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>4. Big-bang cutover.</strong> Drain the old system to zero depth during a maintenance window, then point producers at the new one. Honest assessment: this is acceptable only for low-volume, non-critical, tolerant-of-downtime workloads, and it is the answer interviewers expect you to reject for anything important. Its one virtue is that it has no dual-live window, and therefore none of the duplicate and reordering problems described below.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Verifying Parity</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">\"We switched and nothing looked broken\" is not verification. Build four checks before you move any traffic:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Counts over a window.</strong> Messages produced, consumed and dead-lettered per minute on both sides, from the same clock source. Any sustained delta is a bug, not noise; an intermittent delta is usually a boundary effect at the window edge, which you fix by comparing over overlapping five-minute buckets rather than instants.</li>\n<li><strong>Per-message checksums.</strong> Have both consumers emit a hash of the canonical business payload keyed by message id to a comparison store (a DynamoDB table with a short TTL works well). A row that sees only one side within the TTL is a lost or extra message; a row where the two hashes differ is a serialization bug — extremely common when moving between JSON conventions, Avro, and protobuf, especially around numeric precision, null versus absent fields, and timestamp formats.</li>\n<li><strong>End-to-end latency comparison.</strong> Stamp <code>publishedAt</code> on the producer and measure publish-to-processed p50/p95/p99 on both paths. Expect SQS to show a latency floor from polling and visibility semantics and Kafka to show lower per-message latency but sharper tail spikes during rebalances. You want the numbers before cutover so that the post-cutover graph does not surprise anyone.</li>\n<li><strong>Business-outcome reconciliation.</strong> The strongest check: compare the resulting state, not the messages. Orders shipped, invoices generated, rows written. If the shadow consumer writes to a shadow table, a nightly diff of shadow versus production is the most convincing artifact you can bring to a go/no-go review.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Idempotency and Ordering During Cutover</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The dual-live window is precisely when correctness breaks, and saying so unprompted is a strong signal in an interview. Three specific hazards:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Duplicates across systems.</strong> If both old and new consumers act on the same logical message during the flip, you charge twice. Deduplication must therefore be keyed on a <strong>broker-independent business idempotency key</strong> that the producer generates and both paths carry, not on the SQS message id or the Kafka offset. Store it in a shared table with a conditional write so that the first writer wins regardless of which system delivered the message.</li>\n<li><strong>Reordering across systems.</strong> Even if each broker preserves order internally, two brokers draining in parallel have no mutual ordering. Message 5 can arrive on the new path while message 4 is still in flight on the old. For ordered workloads the only clean answer is a <strong>per-key cutover with a drain</strong>: stop producing key K to the old system, wait until the old system reports zero in-flight for K (or simply wait longer than the maximum end-to-end latency plus visibility timeout), then start producing K to the new system. Combine with a version guard in the consumer so a late straggler is discarded rather than applied.</li>\n<li><strong>Redelivery amplification.</strong> A bridge that retries plus an at-least-once source plus an at-least-once destination multiply: your duplicate rate during migration is higher than either system alone. Size your idempotency store and its TTL for that, and make the TTL longer than the old system's maximum retention.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Amazon MQ and MSK — The Lift-and-Shift Paths</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Amazon MQ</strong> runs managed <strong>RabbitMQ</strong> and <strong>ActiveMQ</strong> brokers. Crucially it speaks the real protocols — AMQP 0-9-1, and for ActiveMQ also JMS, MQTT, STOMP and OpenWire — so existing clients connect with a URL change rather than a rewrite. Exchanges, routing keys, priorities, per-message TTL and dead-letter exchanges all keep working. That makes it the correct answer when <strong>protocol compatibility matters more than re-architecting</strong>: a large legacy estate, a vendor application you cannot modify, or a deadline that forbids touching consumer code. The trade-off is that you still think about broker instance sizes, single-instance versus cluster deployment modes, engine version upgrades and maintenance windows — you have outsourced the operations, not the architecture, and you do not get SQS's unlimited elastic scaling. A common and sensible sequence is: self-managed RabbitMQ to Amazon MQ (de-risk operations now, no code change), then selectively move individual queues to SQS/SNS/EventBridge as those services are touched anyway. Note that Amazon MQ for RabbitMQ supports a specific set of engine versions and that RabbitMQ 4.x removed classic mirrored queues in favour of <strong>quorum queues</strong> (Raft-replicated) and <strong>streams</strong> (an append-only, replayable log) — check the supported-versions page, because a self-managed cluster still using mirrored queues needs a queue-type migration as part of the move.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Amazon MSK</strong> is managed Apache Kafka: real brokers, real protocol, so any Kafka client, Kafka Connect connector, Kafka Streams app or Flink job works unchanged. AWS handles provisioning, patching and broker replacement; you still own <strong>topic and partition design, consumer lag, storage growth and client tuning</strong>. Modern Kafka uses <strong>KRaft</strong> for metadata — ZooKeeper was deprecated over the 3.x line and removed in Kafka 4.0 — so a migration from an old self-managed cluster may also be a ZooKeeper-to-KRaft migration, which is a separate, well-documented procedure you should not conflate with the move to managed infrastructure. <strong>MSK Serverless</strong> removes broker sizing and scales capacity automatically, which is the middle ground when you want the Kafka API without capacity planning; it has its own quotas and does not support every configuration a provisioned cluster does. MSK Replicator handles cluster-to-cluster replication for the migration itself, and MirrorMaker 2 is the open-source equivalent; both replicate topics but you must plan <strong>consumer offset translation</strong>, since offsets are not identical across clusters.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Rollback Planning and Shadow Operation</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A migration plan without a rollback plan is a gamble. Concretely: keep the old system <strong>running and consuming in shadow</strong> for at least one full business cycle after cutover — a week covers weekly batch jobs, a month covers billing. Keep dual-write on during that period so the old path stays warm and has current data; a cold rollback target that has not seen a message in ten days will itself fail when you need it. Make the switch a <strong>runtime configuration flag</strong>, not a deployment, so reverting takes seconds rather than a build. Define rollback triggers numerically before you start (error rate above X, p99 latency above Y, DLQ depth above Z, any checksum mismatch) so nobody debates during an incident. Decide in advance what happens to <strong>in-flight messages</strong> at rollback time: usually you let the new system drain what it already has while producers revert, which requires both consumers to be idempotent against the same key. Finally, write down the <strong>decommission criteria</strong> — otherwise the old cluster runs for two more years and the migration never actually finishes.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Worked Migration Plan — RabbitMQ to SQS for an Order Pipeline</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Starting point: a self-managed three-node RabbitMQ cluster, a topic exchange <code>orders</code>, routing keys like <code>order.created</code>, <code>order.paid</code> and <code>order.cancelled</code>, six bound queues consumed by six services, roughly 2,000 messages per second at peak, ordering required per order id for the fulfilment consumer only.
