Real systems need one event to reach several independent consumers, need consumers to receive only the subset they care about, and need those consumers to fail independently. That is a composition problem, and the answer is almost never a second consumer on the same queue — it is a fan-out or routing layer in front of the queues. This topic covers SNS-to-SQS fan-out, EventBridge as the richer alternative, multi-queue topologies, the classic routing patterns, and the delivery guarantees at each hop.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Why not just add another consumer to the queue?</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Because SQS delivers each message to exactly one receiver. No producer change, no redeploy of existing consumers.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Making SNS-to-SQS actually work: the queue policy</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The most common first-time failure is that the subscription exists, SNS reports successful publishes, and the queue stays empty. The cause is the <strong>SQS queue resource policy</strong>: SNS delivers using its own service principal, so the queue must have a policy allowing <code>sns.amazonaws.com</code> to <code>sqs:SendMessage</code>, conditioned on the source topic ARN. Subscribing does not create that policy unless your tooling does it for you. Condition on <code>aws:SourceArn</code> (and, for cross-account, <code>aws:SourceAccount</code>) rather than leaving it open — an unconditioned policy lets any SNS topic in any account write to your queue. If the queue is encrypted with SSE-KMS, it must use a <strong>customer managed key</strong> whose key policy allows the SNS service principal to <code>kms:GenerateDataKey</code> and <code>kms:Decrypt</code>; the AWS-managed SQS key does not permit this, which is the second most common silent failure.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Raw message delivery</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">By default SNS wraps your payload in a JSON envelope. The message your consumer reads from SQS is a JSON document with <code>Type</code>, <code>MessageId</code>, <code>TopicArn</code>, <code>Subject</code>, <code>Timestamp</code>, <code>SignatureVersion</code>, <code>Signature</code>, <code>SigningCertURL</code>, <code>UnsubscribeURL</code>, <code>MessageAttributes</code>, and your actual payload as a <em>string</em> in the <code>Message</code> field. Consumers therefore have to parse twice: parse the body, then parse <code>Message</code>.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Setting <code>RawMessageDelivery</code> to true on the subscription strips the envelope — the queue receives your payload verbatim and SNS message attributes are mapped onto SQS message attributes. The trade-offs: raw delivery makes the consumer simpler, makes Lambda event-source filtering on <code>body</code> straightforward, and reduces message size (which matters against the 256 KB limit and for cost, since SQS bills in 64 KB chunks). What you lose is the topic ARN, the SNS message ID and the timestamp, so if a queue is subscribed to several topics and needs to know which one delivered, either keep the envelope or put the source in a message attribute. Note raw delivery changes the shape consumers see, so flipping it on a live subscription is a breaking change.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Subscription filter policies</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Without filtering, every queue receives every message and each consumer discards what it does not want — paying SQS request charges, Lambda invocations and log volume for work it throws away. A <strong>filter policy</strong> on the subscription moves that decision into SNS: non-matching messages are simply not delivered to that queue, and you are not charged for the delivery.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Filter policies are JSON. A key must match for the message to be delivered; values within a key are OR-ed, keys are AND-ed. Operators include exact string match, <code>anything-but</code>, <code>prefix</code> and <code>suffix</code>, <code>exists</code>, numeric comparisons and ranges, and IP address matching. Body-based filtering removes that coupling at the cost of requiring JSON and of coupling your routing to your payload schema, so a field rename becomes a routing outage.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">EventBridge-to-SQS: the richer alternative</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">EventBridge is an event bus with rules rather than a topic with subscriptions, and the differences are real.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Richer content-based rules.</strong> Event patterns match on any field of the event, with numeric operators, prefix and suffix matching, <code>anything-but</code>, wildcards, and <code>&#36;or</code> — and unlike SNS, routing is a property of the <em>bus</em>, so rules are managed centrally rather than per subscriber.</li>\n<li><strong>Input transformation.</strong> A rule can reshape the event before delivery, so each target receives exactly the structure it expects without a translation Lambda.</li>\n<li><strong>Archive and replay.</strong> You can archive all events matching a pattern for a defined retention and later replay a time range onto the bus. This is the killer feature: rebuilding a read model, backfilling a new consumer that did not exist when the events happened, or recovering from a consumer bug that silently dropped a day of events. SNS has no equivalent — once published, an SNS message is gone.</li>\n<li><strong>Cross-account buses and schema registry.</strong> Rules can forward events to a bus in another account, making a central event backbone across an organization straightforward, and the schema registry with discovery gives consumers typed bindings.</li>\n<li><strong>Native AWS service events and third-party SaaS partner event sources</strong> arrive on the bus with no integration code.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The honest comparison: <strong>SNS wins on simplicity, throughput, latency and cost</strong> for high-volume, straightforward fan-out where every subscriber wants roughly the same event and routing is a single attribute. Its per-message cost is materially lower, which matters at hundreds of millions of messages, and it supports very high publish rates. <strong>EventBridge earns its keep</strong> when routing is genuinely content-based and changes often, when you need replay or archival, when you are building a cross-account or cross-domain backbone, or when you want the routing logic to be centrally owned and auditable rather than scattered across subscriptions. EventBridge has a lower default throughput quota (raisable) and higher per-event latency, so it is the wrong choice for a very hot, latency-sensitive path. A common mature architecture uses both: EventBridge as the organization-wide backbone, and SNS for high-volume intra-service fan-out.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Multi-queue topologies</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Priority queues.</strong> SQS has no priority within a queue, so priority means separate queues — for example <code>jobs-high</code> and <code>jobs-low</code> — with a consumer strategy that prefers high. The naive strategy (drain high completely, then poll low) causes <strong>starvation</strong>: sustained high-priority volume means low-priority work never runs and ages until retention expires. Mitigations are a weighted strategy (poll high, and every Nth cycle poll low regardless), dedicated capacity per tier (separate consumer fleets or separate event source mappings with their own concurrency ceilings, which is simplest and most predictable), or an aging rule that promotes low-priority work once <code>ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage</code> crosses a threshold. Two tiers is almost always enough; five tiers is an operational trap.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Per-tenant queues.</strong> Giving each tenant its own queue gives perfect noisy-neighbour isolation — one tenant's million-message batch job cannot delay another tenant — plus per-tenant metrics, per-tenant rate limiting and per-tenant pausing. The costs are real: thousands of queues means thousands of event source mappings or a dynamic poller that discovers queues, CloudWatch cardinality, IaC sprawl, and pollers sitting mostly idle on quiet tenants. The usual compromise is a <strong>tiered topology</strong>: dedicated queues for the handful of large enterprise tenants, a shared queue for the long tail, and a mechanism to promote a tenant that starts misbehaving. Sharded queues (hash tenant id into N queues, N in the tens) is the middle ground that captures most of the isolation at a fraction of the operational cost.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Queue-per-stage pipelines.</strong> Decomposing a long workflow into stages, each with its own queue, lets each stage scale, retry and fail independently — validate, enrich, score, persist, notify. The benefit is that a failure in stage four does not re-run stages one to three, and each stage's resource profile can differ. The costs are end-to-end latency accumulating per hop, harder distributed tracing (carry a correlation id in message attributes religiously), and the fact that nothing gives you a workflow-level view. Past about four stages, or when you need timeouts, branching and compensation, <strong>Step Functions is the better tool</strong> and saying so is a mark of seniority.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Routing patterns worth naming</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Content-based routing.</strong> The router inspects the message and sends it to the right queue — implemented declaratively as EventBridge rules or SNS filter policies rather than as a router Lambda, because a code router becomes a single point of failure and a deployment bottleneck.</li>\n<li><strong>Scatter-gather.</strong> Fan a request out to N workers, each writing its partial result keyed by a <strong>correlation identifier</strong>, with an aggregator that completes when all parts arrive or a timeout fires. The hard parts are the aggregator's state store (DynamoDB with a conditional update and a TTL), deciding what a partial result set means, and duplicate handling — at-least-once delivery means the aggregator must be idempotent per part.</li>\n<li><strong>Claim check.</strong> The payload goes to S3 and the message carries only a pointer plus metadata. This sidesteps the 256 KB message limit, keeps messages small enough to batch efficiently, reduces SQS cost (billed in 64 KB chunks), and keeps large blobs out of logs. The trade-offs are an extra round trip per message, and lifecycle coupling — the S3 object must outlive the message including its full DLQ retention, so set S3 lifecycle rules longer than 14 days plus your replay window.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cross-account, cross-Region, and delivery guarantees per hop</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SNS can deliver to a queue in another account provided the queue policy allows the topic ARN and the subscription is confirmed by the queue owner; EventBridge forwards to a bus in another account via a rule plus a resource policy on the target bus. Cross-Region is supported by both (EventBridge has native cross-Region targets; with SNS the common pattern is a regional topic plus replication at the producer, or a cross-Region subscription), and the design consideration is that the cross-Region hop adds latency and a new failure domain — for disaster recovery you usually want independent regional stacks rather than one stack reaching across.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Guarantees hop by hop. <strong>Publish to SNS</strong> is durable once it returns a MessageId; a failed publish is the producer's problem, which is why producers should retry and why a critical producer sometimes writes to a local queue first. <strong>SNS to SQS</strong> uses the AWS-managed retry policy — SNS retries deliveries to AWS endpoints many times over a period of days (on the order of 100,000 attempts across 23 days), so a transiently unavailable or throttled queue does not lose messages. If delivery ultimately fails, the message is dropped <em>unless the subscription has a redrive policy pointing at an SNS DLQ</em> — <strong>configure an SNS subscription DLQ; it is the single most commonly missed control in fan-out designs</strong>, and without it you find out about lost events from a customer. Enable <code>DeliveryStatusLogging</code> and alarm on <code>NumberOfNotificationsFailed</code>.
