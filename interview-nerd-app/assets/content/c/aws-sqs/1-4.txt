The event source mapping (ESM) is a managed poller fleet that lives inside the Lambda service, reads from your queue, and invokes your function synchronously with a batch of messages. Almost every production incident involving SQS and Lambda traces back to a misunderstanding of one of five things: how the poller fleet scales, how that scaling interacts with account concurrency, how partial batch failures are reported, how the visibility timeout relates to the function timeout, and how deletion of messages actually happens. This topic works through each mechanism at the level a senior engineer is expected to reason about without looking anything up.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The polling architecture: who actually calls ReceiveMessage</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">You never write a receive loop. The Lambda service runs a fleet of internal pollers on your behalf. Each poller issues long-polling <code>ReceiveMessage</code> calls against your queue, assembles a batch, and calls <code>Invoke</code> on your function with an event containing a <code>Records</code> array. When your function returns successfully, the ESM calls <code>DeleteMessageBatch</code> for the messages in that batch. If the function throws, times out, or is throttled, the ESM deletes nothing, the visibility timeout lapses, and the messages become visible again.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two consequences matter commercially and operationally. First, <strong>you are not billed for the pollers</strong> as Lambda invocations, and they do not appear in your function's <code>Invocations</code> metric or its concurrency accounting. You are, however, billed for the SQS API requests the pollers make — including empty receives. This is why a very low-traffic queue attached to a Lambda still produces a nonzero SQS bill and why <code>ReceiveMessageWaitTimeSeconds</code> on the queue still matters even though you do not write the receive call. Second, <strong>the poller fleet is not visible to you</strong>: you cannot log into it, you cannot see its concurrency directly, and the only observable proxies are your function's concurrency metrics and the queue's <code>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesNotVisible</code>.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Because the ESM deletes messages only after a successful return, <strong>the delete is at the batch granularity by default</strong>. This single design decision is the root of the partial batch failure problem discussed below.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Scaling behaviour and the documented ramp</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">For a standard queue, the ESM begins with a small number of concurrent batch invocations — five — and then evaluates the backlog. If messages are still available and invocations are succeeding, it increases concurrency aggressively: Lambda adds up to <strong>300 additional concurrent invocations per minute</strong> for an SQS event source (this was raised from 60 per minute in late 2023). This tells the poller fleet itself not to exceed that many concurrent invocations. The pollers simply stop asking for more work. Messages stay in the queue, visible, untouched — their <code>ApproximateReceiveCount</code> does not move. This is the modern, correct control.</li>\n<li><strong>Reserved concurrency on the function.</strong> This caps how many concurrent executions the function may have and carves that share out of the account pool. It was the pre-2023 workaround, and it has a sharp edge: the pollers do not know about it. They keep scaling, keep invoking, and Lambda returns <code>TooManyRequestsException</code>. Every throttled invocation is a failed delivery: the batch returns to the queue and <code>ApproximateReceiveCount</code> increments. With a <code>maxReceiveCount</code> of, say, 5, a sustained burst can push <strong>perfectly healthy messages into the DLQ purely because they were throttled</strong>, never because anything was wrong with them.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Reserved concurrency is still useful — it guarantees a floor of capacity for a critical function and isolates it from the shared pool — but for controlling how hard an SQS consumer pulls, use ESM maximum concurrency. A good production pattern is both: reserved concurrency to fence off the function's share of the account, and ESM maximum concurrency at or below that number to control the pull rate without generating throttles. Note that the ESM maximum concurrency is per mapping, so a function with two queues attached can still reach the sum of both ceilings.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Batch size, batching window, and the payload ceiling</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><code>BatchSize</code> for a standard queue is up to 10 by default; setting <code>MaximumBatchingWindowInSeconds</code> to any value greater than zero raises the allowed batch size to 10,000. The batching window (0 to 300 seconds) tells the poller to keep accumulating until either the batch is full, the window expires, or the accumulated payload approaches the 6 MB synchronous invocation payload limit.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The trade-offs are exact. A larger batch amortises cold starts, connection setup, and per-invocation billing overhead across more messages, and it lets you do bulk writes downstream (one <code>BatchWriteItem</code> instead of ten <code>PutItem</code> calls). It costs you latency — with a 20-second window, a message that arrives one second after a batch closes waits 20 seconds — and it increases blast radius: one bad message in a batch of 1,000 without partial failure reporting redelivers 999 good ones. The 6 MB payload limit is the invisible cap: with 256 KB messages you will never get more than roughly 23 per batch regardless of what <code>BatchSize</code> says, so tuning <code>BatchSize</code> to 500 for a queue of large messages does nothing except mislead the next engineer.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Partial batch failures, rigorously</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">By default the ESM treats the invocation result as binary. Return normally and every message in the batch is deleted. Throw and <strong>nothing</strong> is deleted — the entire batch, including the messages you successfully processed, returns to the queue when the visibility timeout expires. Those successful messages are then redelivered, their receive counts climb, your handler must be idempotent to survive it, and after <code>maxReceiveCount</code> receives they land in the DLQ having never actually failed.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The fix is <code>FunctionResponseTypes: [\"ReportBatchItemFailures\"]</code> on the mapping. Your handler then returns an object with a <code>batchItemFailures</code> array of <code>{ itemIdentifier: messageId }</code> entries. The ESM deletes everything not named in that list and returns only the named messages to the queue. Three sharp edges to state in an interview:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>The handler must not throw.</strong> If it throws, the response is never produced and the whole batch fails regardless of the setting. Wrap each record in its own try/catch.</li>\n<li><strong>A malformed response is treated as a full batch failure.</strong> A misspelled key, a null <code>itemIdentifier</code>, or an identifier that does not correspond to a message in the batch causes Lambda to fall back to failing the entire batch. Silently. You discover it as mysterious reprocessing.</li>\n<li><strong>Returning an empty list means total success</strong>, and so does returning <code>null</code> or an empty object. That is a trap if your error accumulator is accidentally scoped wrong — you will delete messages that failed.</li>\n<li><strong>For FIFO queues</strong>, you must stop processing a message group at its first failure and report that message plus every subsequent message in the same group as failed, otherwise you break ordering.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Event filtering on the mapping</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><code>FilterCriteria</code> lets the poller evaluate a pattern against each message and discard non-matching ones <strong>before any invocation happens</strong>. Up to five filter patterns per ESM by default; a message matching any one of them is delivered. For SQS the message body is available under the <code>body</code> key, and because SQS bodies are strings, you use the JSON-shaped pattern form when the body is JSON.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The economics are the point: a queue carrying 100 million events a month of which your function cares about 2% costs you 100 million invocations without filtering and 2 million with it — plus the compute duration of 98 million no-op invocations, plus the log volume. There is one behavioural detail worth knowing: <strong>filtered-out messages are deleted from the queue</strong>, not left behind. The ESM discards them, so you cannot use a filter as a peek mechanism, and every consumer of a shared queue needs its own copy of the data (which is exactly why SNS fan-out to per-consumer queues exists).</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Visibility timeout and function timeout</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">AWS recommends setting the queue's visibility timeout to at least <strong>six times the function timeout</strong>. The factor exists because a single message may be attempted several times within one visibility window: the ESM may retry a throttled invocation, batches are delivered with the same visibility clock, and there is poller and invocation overhead beyond the function's own duration budget.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">If the visibility timeout is shorter than the function timeout, the failure is pathological rather than obvious: the function is still running when the message becomes visible again, another poller picks it up, and now two invocations are processing the same message concurrently. You get duplicate side effects, duplicate downstream load, and receive counts that climb even though nothing failed — eventually pushing healthy messages into the DLQ. The observable symptom is <code>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesNotVisible</code> far exceeding what your concurrency should explain, together with duplicate-key errors downstream. The ESM also refuses to be created if the queue visibility timeout is below the function timeout, which catches the naive case but not the case where somebody later raises the function timeout.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">FIFO queues with Lambda</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">With a FIFO source, the ESM guarantees that messages within a <code>MessageGroupId</code> are delivered in order and that only one batch per group is in flight at a time. Concurrency therefore scales with the number of <strong>active message groups</strong>, not with the backlog. Ten thousand messages in one group process strictly serially no matter how much concurrency is available; the same ten thousand across two thousand groups can use two thousand concurrent invocations. Choosing the group key is therefore a throughput decision, not just a correctness decision: per-customer or per-entity keys usually give both ordering where it matters and parallelism where it does not. A stuck group blocks only itself, which is a feature — but a poison message at the head of a hot group stalls that entire group until it exhausts <code>maxReceiveCount</code> and moves to the DLQ.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Production scenario: the mapping that is killing the database</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">An order-enrichment function reads from a standard queue and writes to an Aurora PostgreSQL writer with <code>max_connections</code> of 400. A backfill pushes 3 million messages. Within four minutes the ESM is running roughly 900 concurrent invocations, each opening a connection; the writer hits connection exhaustion, query latency goes from 8 ms to 4 seconds, function duration climbs from 200 ms to the 30-second timeout, invocations start failing, batches return to the queue, receive counts climb, and the DLQ starts filling with messages that were never malformed. Meanwhile the account's remaining concurrency is gone and an unrelated API-backed function is throttling.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The correct remediation, in order. <strong>Immediately:</strong> set <code>MaximumConcurrency</code> on the ESM to a number derived from the database, not from the backlog — if the writer tolerates 100 concurrent connections and you want headroom for other clients, set it to roughly 50 and let the queue hold the rest. <strong>Then:</strong> put RDS Proxy in front of the database so connections are pooled and reused across invocations rather than created per execution environment. <strong>Then:</strong> raise <code>BatchSize</code> with a batching window so each invocation does one multi-row write instead of ten single-row writes — this raises throughput per unit of database load, which is the opposite direction from raising concurrency. <strong>Then:</strong> enable <code>ReportBatchItemFailures</code> so a single bad row does not redeliver 499 good ones. <strong>Finally:</strong> raise the visibility timeout to six times the function timeout and raise <code>maxReceiveCount</code> so that a transient database wobble does not dead-letter valid work.
