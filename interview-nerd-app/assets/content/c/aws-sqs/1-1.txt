{"title":"Scaling SQS — Throughput, Quotas, and Consumer Fleets","tags":["scaling","capacity-planning","auto-scaling","throttling"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">The single most common mistake in scaling an SQS system is scaling the wrong thing. SQS standard queues are effectively unlimited in throughput — AWS operates them as a horizontally sharded fleet and there is no per-queue TPS ceiling to design around. In practice, the bottleneck is always one of three things: your consumer fleet, your downstream dependency, or a quota you did not know existed (most often the in-flight message limit). Senior interviews probe whether you can do the arithmetic, pick the right Auto Scaling signal, and reason about what breaks when a backlog appears all at once.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Standard Throughput vs FIFO Limits</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A standard queue supports a nearly unlimited number of API calls per second per action. You do not need to pre-warm it, request a limit increase, or partition it for throughput reasons. If you are sending a million messages a second, SQS is not your problem — your producers, your consumers and your database are.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">FIFO is different because ordering requires coordination. A FIFO queue has a documented base limit of roughly 300 transactions per second per API action (send, receive, delete), and because a batch call carries up to 10 messages, batching lifts that to roughly 3,000 messages per second per action. <strong>High throughput mode for FIFO</strong> raises these limits substantially, with the increase expressed per API action and per message group, and the headline numbers vary by region — the documented per-region figures are the authority here. The architectural point that matters more than any number: <strong>FIFO throughput scales with the number of distinct active message groups</strong>, so a high-throughput FIFO queue with one message group is still slow. Always state that quotas are documented and some are adjustable through AWS Support rather than reciting figures you are not certain of.</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; margin-top: 0.75rem\">\n<table style=\"width: 100%; Batching cuts your API call count — and therefore your SQS bill — by up to 10x, and removes per-call latency from the critical path. The cost is coarser failure granularity: with partial batch failure reporting (<code>ReportBatchItemFailures</code> on a Lambda event source mapping) you can fail only the offending items, but without it a single bad message causes the whole batch to be retried, re-delivering the nine that already succeeded. That is a direct argument for idempotency, not against batching.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Long polling and utilization</strong>: with short polling, an idle consumer burns CPU and API calls in a spin loop, and your \"consumer count\" overstates real capacity because workers spend time on empty receives. With <code>WaitTimeSeconds=20</code>, an idle worker makes three calls a minute and a busy worker gets messages immediately, so utilization is essentially all useful work. Long polling does not add latency when messages are available — the call returns as soon as the front end finds something.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The In-Flight Limit as a Fleet-Size Ceiling</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Because in-flight messages are counted per queue, <em>consumers × batch size × concurrency</em> is bounded by roughly 120,000 on standard and 20,000 on FIFO. Long processing times make this bite much sooner, because each message occupies an in-flight slot for its whole duration. Blowing through it produces <code>OverLimit</code> errors on receive: throughput stalls, backlog grows, and the symptom looks like SQS \"throttling\" when it is really your own design.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Shrink the batch.</strong> Batch of 1 with 10,000 workers is 10,000 in flight; batch of 10 is 100,000.</li>\n<li><strong>Shorten time in flight.</strong> Delete the message once the work is durably handed off (to a state machine, a job table) instead of holding it for the whole multi-minute job.</li>\n<li><strong>Shard queues.</strong> The limit is per queue, so N queues give you N times the ceiling — plus blast-radius isolation.</li>\n<li><strong>Cap concurrency deliberately.</strong> A downstream database usually falls over well before SQS does; the limit that should bind is the one you chose.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Horizontal Scaling: Backlog per Consumer Is the Right Signal</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Multiple consumers on one queue form a competing-consumers pattern: each message goes to exactly one of them (modulo duplicates), so adding workers adds throughput with no coordination. The question is what signal drives the scaling.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>CPU is the wrong signal for queue workers.</strong> A worker that spends 95% of its time waiting on a downstream HTTP call or a database query sits at 5% CPU while a million-message backlog piles up — CPU-based scaling will never fire, and may even scale you <em>in</em> during the worst of it. Queue workers are usually I/O bound, so CPU measures the wrong resource entirely.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The correct signal is <strong>backlog per consumer</strong>, published as a custom CloudWatch metric:</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">backlog_per_consumer <i class=o>=</i> ApproximateNumberOfMessages <i class=o>/</i> RunningCapacity\n\nacceptable_backlog_per_consumer <i class=o>=</i> acceptable_latency_seconds <i class=o>/</i> avg_processing_seconds\n     e.g. SLA <i class=n>60</i>s latency, <i class=n>0.5</i>s <i class=k>per</i> message  <i class=o>-</i><i class=o>&gt;</i>  target <i class=o>=</i> <i class=n>120</i> messages <i class=k>per</i> consumer\n\n<i class=k>Then</i>: TargetTrackingScaling <i class=k>on</i> the custom metric, TargetValue <i class=o>=</i> <i class=n>120</i></code></pre></div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The target is derived from your latency SLA rather than guessed, which is exactly the reasoning an interviewer wants to see. Target tracking then adds or removes capacity to hold the ratio. Two operational caveats: use <strong>step scaling or a short cooldown</strong> if your instances take minutes to become useful, and set a <strong>minimum capacity above zero</strong> for latency-sensitive queues so the first message after a quiet period does not wait for a cold instance. <code>ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage</code> is an excellent complementary alarm because it directly measures the SLA you derived the target from.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Scale-in safety</strong> is where naive setups lose messages' worth of time. A terminating worker holding in-flight messages must be allowed to finish: use EC2 Auto Scaling <strong>lifecycle hooks</strong> or ECS <code>stopTimeout</code> with a SIGTERM handler that stops polling, finishes and deletes what it holds, then exits. If it is killed mid-flight the messages are not lost — they become visible again after the visibility timeout — but you have just added a full timeout of latency and a guaranteed duplicate delivery. Also enable <strong>instance scale-in protection</strong> for long jobs, and drain the ALB/ECS deregistration before the hook expires.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Multi-Queue Sharding</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">One queue is one unit of fairness, isolation and limits. When those need to differ, split:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Per-priority queues.</strong> SQS has no priority field. The standard pattern is a high and a low queue with consumers that poll high first and fall back to low, or a dedicated fleet per priority. Poll-high-first risks starving the low queue, so cap the ratio.</li>\n<li><strong>Per-tenant queues.</strong> Prevents one noisy tenant's burst from adding latency to everyone else — a real \"noisy neighbor\" fix that no amount of consumer scaling achieves on a shared queue.</li>\n<li><strong>Per-workload queues.</strong> Separate a 50 ms job from a 5-minute job so the slow one does not consume the in-flight budget and workers of the fast one. Different visibility timeouts and DLQ policies also become possible.</li>\n<li><strong>Per-downstream queues.</strong> If one dependency is fragile, isolating its traffic means its backlog does not block unrelated work — bulkheading.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The operational cost is real: per-queue CloudWatch alarms and dashboards, IAM policies, Terraform sprawl, and the routing logic itself. Thousands of per-tenant queues become a management problem, and consumers polling many queues waste calls on empty ones. A common compromise is sharding only the top talkers (dedicated queues for the largest tenants, one shared queue for the long tail) or fanning out from SNS/EventBridge so the routing lives in a managed service rather than your code.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">API Quotas, Throttling, and Backoff</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Even on standard queues you can be throttled — by account-level API request rates, by an unusually aggressive burst, or by FIFO's per-action TPS. Throttling surfaces as <code>RequestThrottled</code> / <code>ThrottlingException</code> (HTTP 403 or 5xx depending on the error), and the AWS SDKs retry these automatically with <strong>exponential backoff and jitter</strong> under the <code>standard</code> or <code>adaptive</code> retry mode. Jitter is the important word: without randomization, every throttled client retries at the same instant and re-creates the spike that caused the throttle — a synchronized retry storm. Configure <code>max_attempts</code> and retry mode explicitly rather than relying on defaults, and emit a metric on retry counts so throttling is visible before it becomes latency. Batch APIs are also a throttling mitigation, since ten messages in one call is one request against the rate.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cold Start and Thundering Herd</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The dangerous moment in a queue system is not steady state — it is the recovery. A downstream outage produces a two-million-message backlog; the downstream comes back; your autoscaler sees an enormous backlog-per-consumer and scales to maximum capacity; every one of those workers hammers a database that has cold caches, empty connection pools and no warmed query plans. You knock the dependency over again within seconds, the backlog grows further, and you loop.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Cap maximum capacity</strong> to what the downstream can survive, not to what drains fastest. This is the single most effective control.</li>\n<li><strong>Ramp, do not jump.</strong> Step scaling with modest steps and a cooldown, or reserved/provisioned concurrency limits on Lambda event source mappings, so capacity rises over minutes rather than seconds.</li>\n<li><strong>Client-side rate limiting</strong> in the worker (a token bucket around the downstream call) turns an unbounded fleet into a bounded request rate.</li>\n<li><strong>Circuit breaker.</strong> When the downstream error rate spikes, stop consuming and let the queue hold the load — that is what it is for. Returning messages to the queue via <code>ChangeMessageVisibility</code> with a backoff beats burning <code>maxReceiveCount</code> into the DLQ during an outage.</li>\n<li><strong>Watch maxReceiveCount during incidents.</strong> A downstream outage plus an aggressive redrive policy silently dumps your entire backlog into the DLQ. Prefer retrying with backoff and reserve the DLQ for genuine poison messages.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Worked Capacity-Planning Scenario</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Image-thumbnail service.
