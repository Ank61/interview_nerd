{"title":"Dead-Letter Queues and Redrive","tags":["sqs","dead-letter-queue","redrive","error-handling"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Queues retry. That is one of their best features — a message that fails because a database was briefly unreachable will be delivered again and probably succeed. But retrying forever is a disaster, and the mechanism that saves you from it is the <strong>dead-letter queue</strong>. If you learn one operational practice for SQS, make it this one.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The poison pill problem</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>poison pill</strong> is a message that will <em>never</em> succeed no matter how many times you try. Someone published a message with a null customer id, or a body that is not valid JSON, or an order referring to a product that was deleted. Your consumer throws, the message is not deleted, and after the visibility timeout expires it becomes available again — so it is delivered again, throws again, and the cycle repeats.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Concretely, here is what a single poison pill costs you:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>It never leaves.</strong> Until the retention period expires — by default four days — it keeps coming back.</li>\n<li><strong>It blocks progress.</strong> On a FIFO queue this is fatal: messages in the same message group are delivered in order, so a stuck message halts every message behind it in that group. On a standard queue other messages still flow, but workers waste capacity on the repeat offender.</li>\n<li><strong>It drowns your logs.</strong> The same stack trace every thirty seconds for four days buries every other error you needed to see, and inflates your CloudWatch Logs bill.</li>\n<li><strong>It costs money.</strong> Every redelivery is a billable receive, plus the compute your worker burns failing.</li>\n<li><strong>It can wake people up.</strong> A perpetual error rate triggers alarms and turns into an on-call page for one bad message.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">What a dead-letter queue actually is</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>dead-letter queue</strong>, or DLQ, is nothing special — it is an ordinary SQS queue that you designate as the destination for messages that have failed processing too many times. There is no \"DLQ\" checkbox when you create it; a queue becomes a DLQ purely because another queue's configuration points at it.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The idea is quarantine. Instead of a bad message circulating forever, SQS moves it aside after N attempts. font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The redrive policy: how you wire it up</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The connection lives on the <em>source</em> queue as an attribute called <strong>RedrivePolicy</strong>, a small JSON document with exactly two fields:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>deadLetterTargetArn.</strong> The ARN (the unique AWS identifier) of the queue that should receive the failures.</li>\n<li><strong>maxReceiveCount.</strong> The number of receives after which SQS moves the message to that queue. Set it to 5 and the sixth delivery attempt does not happen — on reaching 5 receives without a delete, the message goes to the DLQ instead.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">SQS tracks this with <strong>ApproximateReceiveCount</strong>, a per-message counter of how many times it has been delivered. Every time the count would exceed <code>maxReceiveCount</code>, SQS moves the message instead of redelivering it. The word \"Approximate\" is honest: in a massively distributed system the count is not guaranteed to be exact, so treat it as a strong signal rather than a precise ledger. You can read it on receive by requesting it in <code>MessageSystemAttributeNames</code>, which is how you log \"attempt 3 of 5\".</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Choosing maxReceiveCount</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This is a balance. Too low and a two-minute blip in a downstream API dumps thousands of perfectly good messages into the DLQ. Too high and a genuinely broken message burns compute and log volume for hours before it is quarantined.</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; margin-top: 0.75rem\">\n<table style=\"width: 100%; border-collapse: collapse; Size the pair together.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The trap: every receive counts, including ones you did not intend</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">This is the detail that produces the most confusing DLQ incidents. <code>ApproximateReceiveCount</code> increments on <strong>every delivery</strong>, not on every failure. SQS has no idea whether your worker succeeded — it only knows whether the message was deleted.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">So imagine your job takes 90 seconds and your <strong>visibility timeout</strong> — the window during which a received message is hidden from other consumers — is 30 seconds. At 30 seconds the message becomes visible again while your worker is still happily working. Another worker receives it (count 2) and starts the same job. At 60 seconds, again (count 3). Nobody has failed; the work is succeeding, repeatedly and wastefully — and after five such deliveries a perfectly healthy message lands in the DLQ.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The lesson: <strong>a DLQ full of messages that look fine usually means the visibility timeout is too short, not that the messages are bad.</strong> The fix is to set the visibility timeout comfortably above your p99 processing time, or to call <code>ChangeMessageVisibility</code> to extend it (a heartbeat) during long work. Worker crashes and deployments that kill workers mid-message have the same effect — each one burns an attempt.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Queue type must match</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A rule you will be asked about: the DLQ must be the <strong>same type</strong> as the source queue. A <strong>FIFO</strong> queue's dead-letter queue must also be FIFO, and a <strong>standard</strong> queue's DLQ must be standard. SQS rejects the redrive policy otherwise. It makes sense — a FIFO queue's ordering and deduplication semantics cannot be preserved by a standard queue.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Related: both queues must be in the <strong>same AWS account and region</strong>, and if the source queue is encrypted with a customer managed KMS key, make sure the DLQ's key configuration lets the same principals read the quarantined messages.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Operating a DLQ: alarm, inspect, fix, redrive</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>1. Alarm on it being non-empty.</strong> This is the single most important SQS alarm you will ever configure. A DLQ nobody watches is invisible data loss — orders never shipped, emails never sent, payments never recorded — and because the main queue looks perfectly healthy, nothing else tells you. Create a CloudWatch alarm on <code>ApproximateNumberOfMessagesVisible</code> for the DLQ with a threshold of 0 and treat missing data as not breaching, so any message at all pages someone.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>2. Inspect.</strong> Use the console's \"Send and receive messages\" page, or <code>receive-message</code> with a visibility timeout of 0 so you can peek without hiding the message from anyone else. Request <code>ApproximateReceiveCount</code> and <code>SentTimestamp</code>. You are answering: is this one bad message or ten thousand? Do they share a shape? Is the failure in the data or in my code?</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>3. Fix the cause.</strong> Deploy the code fix, restore the downstream dependency, or correct the bad records. Redriving before the fix simply refills the DLQ.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>4. Redrive.</strong> <strong>Redrive</strong> means moving messages from the DLQ back to the source queue so they are processed again. SQS has this built in — the <strong>DLQ redrive</strong> feature, exposed as a \"Start DLQ redrive\" button in the console and as the <code>start-message-move-task</code> API. It moves messages server-side, lets you cap the rate with <code>MaxNumberOfMessagesPerSecond</code>, and can be monitored and cancelled. Prefer it over a hand-written receive-send-delete script: your script has to handle partial failure, duplicates and throttling, and it is very easy to lose messages with a bug in the delete step.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Give the DLQ a long retention</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A DLQ is evidence. If its <strong>MessageRetentionPeriod</strong> — how long a message survives in a queue before SQS deletes it — is the default four days, a failure that happens on a Friday evening can be gone before anyone finishes investigating on Tuesday. Set DLQ retention to the maximum, <strong>14 days</strong> (1,209,600 seconds), essentially always. The messages are few and the peace of mind is large.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">One DLQ per queue</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">It is tempting to create a single <code>app-dlq</code> and point everything at it. Resist. With a shared DLQ you cannot tell from an alarm which pipeline broke, redrive becomes dangerous because <code>start-message-move-task</code> sends messages back to their original source queue but you lose the clean \"this queue's failures\" view, retention and access policies cannot differ per workload, and different teams end up staring at each other's failures. The convention is simple: <code>orders-queue</code> gets <code>orders-dlq</code>, <code>emails-queue</code> gets <code>emails-dlq</code>. One alarm each, one owner each.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Common beginner misconceptions</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>\"A DLQ is a special type of queue.\"</strong> It is an ordinary SQS queue. It becomes a DLQ only because another queue's <code>RedrivePolicy</code> names it.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Messages in a DLQ retry automatically.\"</strong> They do not. A DLQ is a parking lot, not a retry loop — nothing happens until you redrive them deliberately.</li>\n<li><strong>\"A DLQ prevents data loss by itself.\"</strong> Only if someone is watching. Without an alarm and a retention long enough to survive the weekend, a DLQ is a silent hole where your data goes to expire.</li>\n<li><strong>\"One DLQ for everything is fine.\"</strong> It destroys your ability to alarm, attribute and redrive per workload.</li>\n<li><strong>\"Messages only reach the DLQ when my code throws.\"</strong> No — <code>ApproximateReceiveCount</code> counts <em>receives</em>. A too-short visibility timeout, a crashed worker, or a deployment can dead-letter healthy messages.</li>\n<li><strong>\"I can use a standard DLQ for my FIFO queue.\"</strong> SQS rejects it. The types must match, in the same account and region.</li>\n</ul>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"A dead-letter queue is an ordinary SQS queue that catches messages which failed processing too many times, so one poison-pill message cannot be retried forever, block the queue, flood the logs and cost money.
