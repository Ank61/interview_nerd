{"title":"Storage Performance and I/O Optimization on RDS","tags":["EBS","gp3","Provisioned IOPS","I/O Tuning"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">Storage on RDS is a <strong>performance system, not a capacity choice</strong>. Every read that misses the buffer cache and every commit that flushes the log becomes a network round trip to Amazon EBS, so the volume type, the provisioned IOPS, the throughput ceiling and — the frequently missed part — the <strong>instance's own EBS bandwidth limit</strong> collectively set the floor on database latency. Many \"the database is slow\" incidents are storage incidents wearing a database costume, and the diagnosis is precise if you know which metrics to read.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">gp2 vs gp3: The Burst-Credit Cliff</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>gp2</strong> derives performance from volume size: <strong>3 IOPS per GiB</strong>, minimum 100 IOPS, maximum 16,000 IOPS (reached at about 5,334 GiB). Volumes below 1,000 GiB can <em>burst</em> to 3,000 IOPS by spending <strong>I/O credits</strong> from a bucket that refills at the baseline rate. This coupling of size to performance is the whole problem: a 200 GiB gp2 volume has a baseline of only <strong>600 IOPS</strong>, so to get 6,000 IOPS you must buy 2 TiB of storage you do not need.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The burst bucket produces the classic incident. A 200 GiB gp2 volume starts a batch job needing 3,000 IOPS. For the first stretch it performs beautifully, drawing on accumulated credits. Then <code>BurstBalance</code> reaches zero and throughput collapses to the 600 IOPS baseline — an instantaneous <strong>5× drop</strong>. Query latency multiplies, <code>DiskQueueDepth</code> climbs, connections pile up waiting on I/O, and the application tips over. To the team it looks like \"it was fast for twenty minutes and then fell off a cliff\", with no code change and no traffic change. The tell is <strong>BurstBalance</strong> descending to 0 exactly when latency spikes — and it is a metric nobody thinks to chart until they have been burned once.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>gp3</strong> decouples performance from capacity. Every gp3 volume gets a baseline of <strong>3,000 IOPS and 125 MiB/s included</strong> regardless of size, and you provision more independently of how much space you need. On RDS, the provisioning rules are size-gated: for MySQL, MariaDB and PostgreSQL, volumes <strong>under 400 GiB</strong> are fixed at the 3,000 IOPS / 125 MiB/s baseline and cannot be increased, while volumes of <strong>400 GiB or more</strong> can be provisioned up to <strong>64,000 IOPS and 4,000 MiB/s</strong>. There is no burst-credit model at all — performance is flat and predictable, which is exactly what you want for a database. For most workloads gp3 is both faster and cheaper than the equivalent gp2, and migrating gp2 to gp3 is a modification performed in place with no downtime.</p>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; margin-top: 0.75rem\">\n<table style=\"width: 100%; border-collapse: collapse; font-size: 0.92rem\">\n<thead><tr style=\"text-align: left\" data-box=\"1\"><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Type</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">IOPS model</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Ceiling</th><th style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Use when</th></tr></thead>\n<tbody>\n<tr><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>gp2</strong></td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">3 IOPS/GiB baseline + burst credits below 1 TiB</td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">16,000 IOPS</td><td style=\"padding: 0.6rem; border: 1px solid #e2e8f0\" data-box=\"1\">Legacy only. If you simply need more IOPS and tolerate normal variance, more gp3 IOPS is cheaper.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Instance EBS Limit — The Bottleneck Everyone Misses</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Provisioning 40,000 IOPS on the volume does not mean the database can perform 40,000 IOPS. The <strong>instance class</strong> has its own EBS bandwidth and IOPS ceiling, enforced at the Nitro level, and the effective limit is the <em>minimum</em> of the volume's provisioning and the instance's capability.</p>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>bash</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">effective I/O = MIN( volume provisioned IOPS/throughput ,\n                     instance class EBS IOPS/bandwidth <i class=b>limit</i> )\n\nExample of the <i class=b>trap</i>:\n  volume:   gp3, 2 TiB, 32,000 IOPS provisioned, 1,000 MiB/s          (you paid <i class=k>for</i> this)\n  instance: db.m6i.large -- EBS baseline ~ 10,000 IOPS / ~ 80 MiB/s   (what you actually get)\n  result:   ~80 MiB/s. The diagnostic is to compare achieved <code>ReadIOPS + WriteIOPS</code> and <code>ReadThroughput + WriteThroughput</code> against <em>both</em> the volume's provisioning and the instance class's documented EBS limits, and to check <code>EBSIOBalance%</code> / <code>EBSByteBalance%</code> on classes that burst. The fix is a larger or more EBS-capable instance class, not more IOPS. The reverse trap exists too: a large instance with a small gp2 volume is throttled by the volume. Storage and compute must be sized together.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The Metrics That Diagnose I/O Precisely</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>ReadIOPS / WriteIOPS.</strong> Achieved operations per second. Compare against provisioning: sitting flat at exactly your provisioned number is the signature of throttling, not of coincidence.</li>\n<li><strong>ReadThroughput / WriteThroughput.</strong> Bytes per second. A workload can hit the throughput ceiling long before the IOPS ceiling if it does large sequential reads — a full table scan is a bandwidth problem, not an IOPS problem.</li>\n<li><strong>ReadLatency / WriteLatency.</strong> Seconds per operation. Healthy gp3 and io2 read latency is roughly 0.5–2 ms. Sustained latency above about 10–20 ms means you are queueing, throttled, or the volume is unhealthy.</li>\n<li><strong>DiskQueueDepth.</strong> Outstanding requests waiting on the volume. A consistently high queue depth with latency rising in proportion is the textbook signature of a saturated volume. Low queue depth with high latency points instead at per-operation latency — a different problem.</li>\n<li><strong>BurstBalance.</strong> gp2 only. Percentage of I/O credits remaining. Any downward trend toward zero is an incident in progress. Alarm on it if you still run gp2.</li>\n<li><strong>EBSIOBalance% / EBSByteBalance%.</strong> Instance-level burst credit for EBS operations and bytes on smaller and burstable classes. Draining to zero means the <em>instance</em> is throttling you. These are the metrics that explain \"we provisioned more IOPS and nothing changed\".</li>\n<li><strong>FreeStorageSpace.</strong> Running out of storage puts the instance into <code>storage-full</code> state, which is an outage. Alarm well above zero.</li>\n<li><strong>FreeableMemory and BufferCacheHitRatio.</strong> Indirect but central: I/O demand is a <em>function</em> of how much of the working set fits in memory, so memory metrics are always part of an I/O diagnosis.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Reducing I/O at the Database Level</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Buying IOPS is the last resort, not the first. The cheapest I/O is the one you never issue.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Fit the working set in memory.</strong> This dominates everything else. On MySQL, <code>innodb_buffer_pool_size</code> at ~75% of memory, with the buffer pool hit ratio (derived from <code>Innodb_buffer_pool_reads</code> vs <code>Innodb_buffer_pool_read_requests</code>) above 99% for OLTP. On PostgreSQL, <code>shared_buffers</code> at ~25% plus the OS page cache; check <code>pg_statio_user_tables</code> for heap hit ratios. Moving from a 95% to a 99.5% hit rate is a <strong>10× reduction in physical reads</strong> — more than any storage upgrade you can buy at that price point. Often the right answer to an I/O problem is a memory-optimised instance class, not more IOPS.</li>\n<li><strong>Index to avoid scans.</strong> A sequential scan of a 40-million-row table is pure wasted I/O when a selective index exists. Conversely, over-indexing <em>adds</em> write I/O, so index design is a two-sided optimisation.</li>\n<li><strong>Smooth write bursts with checkpoint tuning.</strong> In PostgreSQL, raise <code>max_wal_size</code> and <code>checkpoint_timeout</code> and keep <code>checkpoint_completion_target</code> at 0.9 so dirty pages are written gradually rather than in a spike that saturates the volume and stalls queries. In MySQL, raise <code>innodb_io_capacity</code> and <code>innodb_io_capacity_max</code> to match the volume so the page cleaner keeps up, and set <code>innodb_flush_neighbors=0</code> because neighbour flushing is a rotational-disk optimisation that wastes SSD I/O.</li>\n<li><strong>Batch commits.</strong> Each commit is a log flush. One transaction per row turns an ingest job into a million fsyncs.</li>\n<li><strong>Control vacuum and bloat.</strong> A bloated table means every scan reads dead space. Vacuum itself also consumes I/O, so <code>autovacuum_vacuum_cost_limit</code> is effectively an I/O budget allocation between maintenance and queries.</li>\n<li><strong>Move scans off the writer.</strong> Analytics, exports and reporting generate huge read volume that evicts the OLTP working set from cache, causing a second wave of physical reads. Send them to a replica or export a snapshot to S3.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Aurora's Different Model</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Aurora does not use a conventional EBS volume, and you <strong>cannot provision IOPS</strong> for it. Storage is a distributed, log-structured service: six copies of every 10 GB segment across three Availability Zones, with the database instance shipping <em>redo log records</em> rather than full pages to storage, and the storage layer materialising pages itself. This removes the double-write buffer and full-page-write overhead, and it is why Aurora write amplification is much lower than a traditional engine on EBS. Storage grows automatically in 10 GB increments up to <strong>128 TiB</strong> and you pay only for what is used.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The commercial consequence is that under the <strong>Aurora Standard</strong> configuration, I/O is a separate, usage-based line item billed per million read and write requests. For an I/O-heavy workload this can become a large and unpredictable fraction of the bill. <strong>Aurora I/O-Optimized</strong> is the alternative configuration: instance and storage rates are higher (roughly 30% more), but <strong>I/O requests are not charged at all</strong>, making spend predictable. The rule of thumb AWS publishes and that interviewers like to hear: <strong>when I/O costs exceed roughly 25% of your total Aurora spend, I/O-Optimized is the cheaper option</strong> — and it also removes the incentive to under-provision reads. You can switch a cluster between configurations, so the correct approach is to measure the actual I/O line on your bill rather than guess.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Storage Autoscaling and Modification Cooldowns</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>RDS storage autoscaling</strong> raises allocated storage automatically when you set a <code>MaxAllocatedStorage</code> above the current allocation. It triggers when free space drops below <strong>10% of allocated storage</strong>, that condition persists for at least <strong>5 minutes</strong>, and at least <strong>6 hours</strong> have passed since the last storage modification. Each increase is the larger of 10 GiB or 10% of current allocated storage, adjusted by the observed growth rate, up to the ceiling you set and the engine maximum (64 TiB for MySQL, MariaDB and PostgreSQL).</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The limits are what matter operationally. Autoscaling <strong>never shrinks storage</strong> — allocated storage on RDS is a one-way ratchet, and the only way to reduce it is to dump and reload into a new, smaller instance. It does not protect you from a <em>fast</em> fill: a runaway process writing 500 GB in ten minutes will hit <code>storage-full</code> before the 5-minute-plus-6-hour machinery can react, so you still need a <code>FreeStorageSpace</code> alarm. And the 6-hour cooldown is the general rule for storage changes, not just autoscaling: after modifying allocated storage, IOPS or throughput, the volume enters an <strong>optimizing</strong> state during which the database remains fully available but <em>no further storage modification is permitted for about six hours</em>. Plan capacity changes accordingly — discovering mid-incident that you cannot raise IOPS again for six hours is an unpleasant surprise. Also note that increasing gp2 volume size increases its baseline IOPS as a side effect, which is why \"just grow the volume\" sometimes fixes a gp2 performance problem, and why it does nothing at all on gp3 below 400 GiB.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Interview Scenario: Diagnosing an I/O-Bound Database</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><em>\"An RDS PostgreSQL instance was fine for months. This week, every weekday at about 09:30, query latency multiplies for roughly 40 minutes, then recovers. CPU is only 35%. Diagnose it.\"</em></p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>1. Establish that it is I/O.</strong> CPU at 35% with high latency immediately points away from compute. In Performance Insights, check the wait breakdown for the incident window: if <code>IO:DataFileRead</code> dominates DB load, the database is waiting on storage. Cross-check CloudWatch <code>ReadLatency</code> — if it jumps from 1 ms to 25 ms, storage is confirmed as the proximate cause.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>2. Determine whether you are throttled or merely busy.</strong> This is the key branch. Chart <code>ReadIOPS + WriteIOPS</code> against the provisioned value. A curve that rises and then goes perfectly <em>flat</em> at the provisioned number is throttling; a curve that fluctuates below it means the volume is not the limit. Check <code>DiskQueueDepth</code> — high and rising confirms queueing. If the volume is gp2, chart <strong>BurstBalance</strong>: a decline to zero coinciding exactly with the latency spike is a diagnosis on its own. If the instance class is a burstable or smaller class, chart <strong>EBSIOBalance%</strong> and <strong>EBSByteBalance%</strong> — draining to zero means the <em>instance</em> is the limit and adding volume IOPS would change nothing.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>3. Find the source of the new demand.</strong> A daily 09:30 pattern is a schedule, not an accident. Use Performance Insights top SQL and top hosts for the window and compare against a quiet window. Typical culprits: a new BI dashboard that refreshes on the hour, a batch export that began running against production, a backup window that moved, a report whose plan flipped to a sequential scan as a table crossed a size threshold, or business-hours traffic that finally pushed the working set past what memory holds.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>4. Check memory before buying IOPS.</strong> Compare <code>FreeableMemory</code> and the buffer cache hit ratio in the window against baseline. If the working set has grown past <code>shared_buffers</code> plus the OS page cache, the database is doing physical reads for data it used to serve from memory — and the surge in read IOPS is a <em>symptom</em> of a memory shortfall. In that case a memory-optimised instance class is both cheaper and more effective than more IOPS, because it eliminates the reads rather than serving them faster.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>5. Fix at the right layer, in order.</strong> (a) If a single query is doing a large sequential scan, index it or rewrite it — this removes the I/O entirely and is free. (b) If it is analytics, move it to a read replica or export a snapshot to S3 for Athena, so scan traffic stops evicting the OLTP working set. (c) If the working set genuinely outgrew memory, scale to a memory-optimised class. (d) If write bursts are the issue, tune checkpoints (<code>max_wal_size</code>, <code>checkpoint_timeout</code>) to spread the flush. (e) Only then, change storage: migrate gp2 to <strong>gp3</strong> to eliminate the burst cliff and get flat, predictable performance, sizing the volume at 400 GiB or above if you need to provision beyond the 3,000 IOPS baseline. (f) If the instance's own EBS limit is the binding constraint, change the instance class.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>6. Prevent recurrence.</strong> Alarm on <code>ReadLatency</code> and <code>WriteLatency</code>, on <code>BurstBalance</code> and <code>EBSIOBalance%</code> approaching zero, on <code>DiskQueueDepth</code>, and on <code>FreeStorageSpace</code>. Enable storage autoscaling with a sane <code>MaxAllocatedStorage</code>. And remember the six-hour cooldown when planning any storage change — if you raise IOPS as an emergency measure, you cannot raise them again that day.</p>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"gp2 ties IOPS to volume size at 3 per GiB and lets small volumes burst on credits, which produces the classic incident where everything is fast until BurstBalance hits zero and throughput collapses to baseline. gp3 decouples them — 3,000 IOPS and 125 MiB/s included, provisionable independently once the volume is 400 GiB or more — with no credits, so performance is predictable.
