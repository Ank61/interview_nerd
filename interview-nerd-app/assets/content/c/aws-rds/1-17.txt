For RDS the commitment instrument is a <strong>Reserved Instance</strong>. Also note that Multi-AZ replication traffic between the writer and its standby is not charged as cross-AZ transfer, but <em>your application's</em> traffic to a database in another AZ is.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Lever 1 — Right-sizing from data, not intuition</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Right-sizing is first because it is usually the largest single line and because every subsequent lever (RIs especially) multiplies whatever size you land on. Committing to three years of an over-provisioned instance locks in the waste. Use evidence:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Performance Insights DBLoad versus vCPU count.</strong> Average Active Sessions sustained well below the vCPU count over a representative period (include month-end and peak season) is the strongest signal that compute is oversized. AAS is a better right-sizing metric than CPUUtilization because it tells you whether sessions are waiting on CPU or on I/O and locks.</li>\n<li><strong>CloudWatch p95, not average.</strong> Look at <code>CPUUtilization</code>, <code>FreeableMemory</code>, <code>ReadIOPS</code>/<code>WriteIOPS</code>, <code>ReadThroughput</code>/<code>WriteThroughput</code> and <code>DatabaseConnections</code> at p95 over 4 to 8 weeks.</li>\n<li><strong>Memory is the real constraint.</strong> Dropping a class halves the buffer pool or shared buffers; a working set that no longer fits turns into read IOPS and latency. Check the buffer cache hit ratio before you shrink, not after.</li>\n<li><strong>Compute Optimizer</strong> produces RDS instance and storage recommendations with projected utilisation, which is a useful cross-check but should not be applied blindly to spiky workloads.</li>\n<li><strong>Change one dimension at a time</strong> and use the Multi-AZ resize path (modify applies to the standby, then fails over) to keep downtime to a failover.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Lever 2 — Reserved Instances</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">RDS RIs come in <strong>1-year and 3-year</strong> terms with <strong>No Upfront, Partial Upfront and All Upfront</strong> payment options; discounts run roughly from the low 20s percent up to around 60 to 70 percent for 3-year All Upfront depending on engine and family. They are a <strong>billing construct</strong>: you do not attach an RI to an instance, you buy coverage for an instance class, Region, engine and deployment type, and matching usage is discounted automatically.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Size flexibility</strong> is the nuance to know. For RDS MySQL, MariaDB, PostgreSQL and Aurora, RI benefits apply across sizes within the same instance family and Region — a reservation for <code>db.r6g.4xlarge</code> normalises and can cover two <code>db.r6g.2xlarge</code> instances. This does <strong>not</strong> apply to the license-included commercial engines (SQL Server, Oracle with LI), where the reservation is pinned to the specific size. Size flexibility is what makes a 3-year commitment tolerable, because it survives right-sizing.</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>Good candidates.</strong> Steady production databases that have existed for a year, are already right-sized, and are not scheduled for a re-platform. Cover the <em>baseline</em> — typically 70 to 80 percent of steady usage — and leave the top on demand.</li>\n<li><strong>Bad candidates.</strong> Anything experimental, anything mid-migration, and anything a team is actively talking about moving to Aurora or DynamoDB. RDS RIs cannot be sold on a marketplace the way EC2 Standard RIs can, so a wrong commitment is sunk cost.</li>\n<li><strong>Watch the deployment-type dimension.</strong> Single-AZ and Multi-AZ reservations are distinct; converting a covered Single-AZ instance to Multi-AZ leaves half the usage on demand.</li>\n<li><strong>Ladder your purchases</strong> in quarterly tranches rather than one large annual buy, so expiry is staggered and you are never renegotiating the whole estate at once.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Lever 3 — Graviton instance classes</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Moving from Intel-based <code>db.m5</code>/<code>db.r5</code> to Graviton-based <code>db.m6g</code>/<code>db.r6g</code> or <code>db.m7g</code>/<code>db.r7g</code> delivers a meaningful price/performance gain — AWS quotes up to around 35 percent better price/performance for RDS on Graviton, and the hourly rate itself is typically about 5 to 10 percent lower for the same nominal size. Because the database engine is the only thing running on the instance and AWS compiles it for ARM, <strong>your application code is unaffected</strong> — this is the rare change where the risk is genuinely low. Availability covers MySQL, MariaDB, PostgreSQL and Aurora; Oracle and SQL Server remain on x86. The change is an instance-class modify, ideally applied in a maintenance window or via the Multi-AZ failover path. Caveats: verify that any engine extensions or plugins you rely on are supported, and benchmark rather than assume for workloads with unusual instruction-level behaviour.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Lever 4 — Aurora I/O-Optimized versus Standard</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Aurora Standard bills instances, storage, and <strong>every I/O request</strong> (on the order of $0.20 per million in us-east-1). trap</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Serverless v2 bills per <strong>ACU-hour</strong>, where one ACU is roughly 2 GiB of memory with matched CPU and networking, scaling in 0.5-ACU increments between a minimum and maximum you configure. It is excellent for genuinely variable load: development and test, multi-tenant estates with many mostly-idle databases, workloads with a sharp daily or weekly peak, and new services whose load you cannot predict.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>The trap:</strong> you pay the minimum ACU continuously, and an ACU-hour costs meaningfully more than the equivalent slice of a provisioned instance. A cluster with <code>min_capacity</code> set defensively high — say 8 ACUs so that cold starts never hurt — that idles most of the day is <em>more expensive</em> than a right-sized provisioned <code>db.r6g.large</code>. The rule of thumb: Serverless v2 wins when <strong>peak-to-trough ratio is large and the trough is genuinely near the minimum</strong>; provisioned wins when utilisation is flat, because you are then paying a premium for elasticity you never use. Newer capability to scale to <strong>0 ACUs with automatic pause</strong> makes the idle case far better for dev/test, at the cost of a resume delay on the first connection — great for a staging database, unacceptable for a latency-sensitive production endpoint. A common hybrid that reads well in interviews: provisioned writer sized for the baseline, Serverless v2 readers that scale with query load.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Lever 6 — Idle non-production databases, and the stop/start gotcha</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Non-production is where most estates leak money, because dev and staging databases run 168 hours a week to serve maybe 45. You can <code>stop-db-instance</code> to halt instance-hour charges, but there are two things that make naive stop/start automation fail in practice:</p>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>A stopped instance still bills storage, backups and snapshots.</strong> You save compute only, which on a small dev instance with a large volume can be a minority of the cost.</li>\n<li><strong>RDS automatically starts a stopped instance after 7 days.</strong> This exists so maintenance can be applied, and it silently defeats \"stop it and forget it\". Your automation must re-stop instances on a schedule, or you will discover a fleet quietly running again.</li>\n</ul>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Better patterns for truly intermittent environments: <strong>snapshot and delete</strong>, restoring on demand — snapshot storage is far cheaper than a live volume and there is no 7-day resurrection; <strong>Aurora clones</strong>, which are copy-on-write against the source cluster's storage so an ephemeral test environment costs almost nothing in storage until it diverges; and <strong>ephemeral environments created per pull request</strong> from a masked snapshot and destroyed with the branch. Note also that Aurora Serverless v2 with a 0-ACU minimum gets you much of the benefit with none of the automation. For the many environments that only need business-hours availability, an EventBridge Scheduler rule invoking a small Lambda to stop at 19:00 and start at 07:00 on weekdays removes roughly 70 percent of non-production instance hours.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Lever 7 — Storage, backups and the long tail</h4>\n<ul style=\"margin-left: 1.5rem; list-style: disc; line-height: 2\">\n<li><strong>gp2 to gp3.</strong> gp3 is roughly 20 percent cheaper per GB and decouples IOPS and throughput from capacity. gp2 gave you 3 IOPS per GB, so teams over-provisioned <em>capacity</em> purely to buy IOPS; gp3 provides a 3,000 IOPS / 125 MBps baseline independent of size, with additional IOPS purchasable above it (and higher baselines available on larger volumes). Migrating is an online modify with no downtime. It is the highest ratio of savings to risk on this entire list.</li>\n<li><strong>io1 to io2 Block Express or gp3.</strong> Most io1 volumes were provisioned for a peak that no longer exists. Compare actual p99 IOPS from CloudWatch against what you pay for.</li>\n<li><strong>Backup retention.</strong> Free allowance equals 100 percent of provisioned storage; a 35-day retention on a high-write database generates far more than that in change data. Match retention to the actual RPO requirement per environment — 35 days for production if the policy demands it, 1 to 7 for non-production.</li>\n<li><strong>Orphaned manual snapshots.</strong> Automated snapshots die with the instance (unless retained as a final snapshot); manual ones live forever. Sweep for snapshots whose source instance no longer exists and whose creation date is older than policy. This is routinely a five-figure annual line in a large estate.</li>\n<li><strong>Performance Insights retention.</strong> 7 days is free. Long-term retention is billed per vCPU-month, so enabling it fleet-wide on large instances is expensive. Keep it on the handful of databases where historical comparison genuinely matters.</li>\n<li><strong>Cross-AZ chatter.</strong> Ensure application nodes and the database writer are AZ-aligned where the architecture permits, and prefer reader endpoints local to the caller. Cross-Region replication (Global Database, cross-Region read replicas, cross-Region snapshot copies) is billed per GB and is often the largest transfer line — make sure the DR capability it buys is actually required.</li>\n<li><strong>Offload analytics.</strong> Reporting queries on OLTP hardware force you to size the most expensive instance in the estate for the least latency-sensitive workload. Move them to a read replica, or better, to S3 plus Athena, Redshift, or Aurora zero-ETL integration with Redshift.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The architectural lever: consolidation versus isolation</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Fifty microservices with fifty <code>db.t3.medium</code> instances pay fifty minimum instance charges, fifty 20 GB volumes, and fifty backup baselines — and most sit at 3 percent CPU. Consolidating onto a few larger instances with one database or schema per service is dramatically cheaper and improves utilisation, because a larger instance absorbs uncorrelated peaks. What you surrender is real and must be priced honestly: <strong>blast radius</strong> (one bad query or one failover affects every tenant service), <strong>noisy neighbours</strong> within a shared buffer pool and connection limit, <strong>independent scaling and version upgrade schedules</strong>, <strong>security isolation</strong> becoming a grants problem rather than a network problem, and <strong>organisational coupling</strong> where teams must now coordinate maintenance windows. The defensible middle ground is to tier: consolidate non-production and low-criticality services aggressively, keep tier-1 services on dedicated clusters, and use Aurora Serverless v2 for the long tail where consolidation is politically impossible.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Interview scenario: cut a $180k/month RDS bill by 30 percent</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Estate: 120 instances and clusters.
