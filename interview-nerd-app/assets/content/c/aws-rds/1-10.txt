{"title":"High Availability and Disaster Recovery Design for RDS","tags":["rds","high availability","disaster recovery","multi-region"],"html":"<div>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75; margin-top: 1.5rem\">High availability and disaster recovery are two different engineering problems that share some tooling, and conflating them is the single most common failure in a database design interview. <strong>High availability (HA)</strong> protects against the loss of a component inside one Region: a failed instance, a failed storage volume, a degraded or lost Availability Zone. It is largely automatic, measured in seconds to a couple of minutes, and costs roughly 2x the compute. <strong>Disaster recovery (DR)</strong> protects against events that HA cannot touch: the loss of an entire Region, logical corruption from a bad deployment, a <code>DELETE</code> with a missing <code>WHERE</code> clause, a compromised IAM principal that drops your databases. DR is never automatic. It is a plan, a set of copies with independent blast radii, and a rehearsed runbook.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The clarifying test: <strong>if the bad thing replicates, HA will not save you.</strong> Multi-AZ replication is synchronous and byte-faithful. A corrupted page, a dropped table, or a truncated row is faithfully applied to the standby in milliseconds. Every HA mechanism in RDS is a replication mechanism, and replication is an amplifier of correctness and of corruption alike. Only a point-in-time copy — a backup, a snapshot, a backtrack window — protects you from something that was correctly committed but wrong.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">RPO and RTO — the two numbers that drive every decision</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Recovery Point Objective (RPO)</strong> is the maximum amount of <em>data</em>, measured in time, that the business accepts losing. An RPO of five minutes means that after a disaster, the surviving copy may be missing up to the last five minutes of committed writes. <strong>Recovery Time Objective (RTO)</strong> is the maximum <em>duration of unavailability</em> the business accepts: from the moment of impact until the application is serving traffic again from a healthy database.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two disciplines separate seniors from juniors here. First, RPO and RTO are business inputs, not engineering outputs — you elicit them (\"what does one hour of lost orders cost?\") and then design to them. Second, both are measured <em>end to end</em>. RTO is not \"how long the restore API call takes\"; it is detection time, plus the decision to declare a disaster, plus restore, plus DNS or configuration cutover, plus application reconnection and cache warm-up, plus validation. Teams that quote a 30-minute RTO based on a restore benchmark and then take four hours in a real event have measured the wrong thing.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The toolbox and what each tool actually protects against</h4>\n<div style=\"overflow-x: auto; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Multi-AZ: what it really is, per engine</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">For RDS engines (MySQL, PostgreSQL, MariaDB, Oracle, SQL Server) a classic <strong>Multi-AZ instance deployment</strong> maintains a standby in a second AZ with <strong>synchronous physical replication at the storage layer</strong> — a commit is not acknowledged until it is durable on both volumes. The standby is not readable and serves no traffic; you are paying for insurance, not capacity. Failover is DNS-based: the instance endpoint CNAME is repointed at the standby, typically in <strong>60 to 120 seconds</strong>. Because the mechanism is DNS, your application's JVM or connection pool DNS caching matters enormously — a Java process with <code>networkaddress.cache.ttl</code> set to <code>-1</code> will keep resolving the dead IP forever and turn a 90-second failover into an outage until restart.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">The newer <strong>Multi-AZ DB cluster</strong> (MySQL and PostgreSQL) gives you one writer plus <strong>two readable standbys</strong> across three AZs, uses semi-synchronous commit that acknowledges when a quorum of one standby confirms, and typically fails over in <strong>under 35 seconds</strong>. It costs more than a Multi-AZ instance but the standbys are usable for reads, which changes the cost conversation.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Aurora is architecturally different.</strong> The storage volume is a distributed, shared, log-structured service that keeps six copies of every 10 GB segment across three AZs, with a 4-of-6 write quorum and 3-of-6 read quorum. Compute nodes are stateless relative to that volume. Losing an AZ loses two copies and the cluster keeps writing. Failover promotes an existing reader in about 30 seconds; with zero readers, Aurora must launch a new writer, which is materially slower. This is why \"Aurora with a single instance\" is a common and expensive mistake: your storage is highly durable, but your <em>availability</em> depends on having at least one reader to promote.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Critically, none of this protects data. A synchronous replica of a <code>DROP TABLE</code> is a synchronously dropped table.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Cross-Region replicas and Aurora Global Database</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">A <strong>cross-Region read replica</strong> for RDS uses asynchronous, engine-native replication (binlog for MySQL/MariaDB, physical streaming or logical for PostgreSQL) across the AWS backbone. Its RPO is exactly its replication lag, which you must monitor with the <code>ReplicaLag</code> CloudWatch metric and alarm on. In a Region-loss event you call <code>promote-read-replica</code>, which makes it a standalone writable instance — and permanently breaks the replication link. That promotion is irreversible: there is no \"demote back\", so failback later means establishing replication in the opposite direction or doing a full rebuild.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Two traps candidates miss. First, <strong>the data-loss window is real</strong>: writes committed on the primary but not yet shipped are gone. If the primary is lost abruptly, you cannot even enumerate what you lost without an independent audit log. Second, <strong>a cross-Region replica is not a backup</strong>: it has its own backup retention configuration, and by default a replica may not have automated backups enabled the way you assume.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Aurora Global Database</strong> replicates at the storage layer using dedicated infrastructure, targeting sub-second cross-Region lag with typical <strong>RPO around one second</strong> and negligible impact on primary write latency (replication is offloaded from the database engine). It supports up to five secondary Regions, each with up to 16 readers. There are two failover flavors: <strong>managed planned failover</strong>, which is for drills and planned Region moves and is designed to be RPO-zero because it coordinates with a healthy primary, and <strong>managed unplanned failover</strong> (\"switchover\" versus \"failover\" in the API), which promotes a secondary when the primary Region is unreachable and accepts the small RPO. Aurora Global also supports <strong>write forwarding</strong> so secondary-Region readers can transparently forward writes to the primary, which simplifies application logic at the cost of cross-Region write latency.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">The copies that survive a bad actor</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Automated backups with PITR</strong> are your primary defense against logical damage. RDS takes a daily snapshot and continuously ships transaction logs to S3, letting you restore to any second within the retention window (1-35 days) down to roughly a <strong>five-minute</strong> granularity of the latest restorable time. The restore <em>always creates a new DB instance</em> — it never rewinds in place — so recovery involves a new endpoint and a cutover. Aurora PITR works the same way at the cluster level.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Automated backups live in the same Region and the same account as the instance, and — this is the part that bites people — <strong>deleting the DB instance deletes its automated backups</strong> unless you retained them explicitly. That is why mature programs add <strong>manual snapshots copied cross-Region and cross-account</strong>. The cross-account copy is the one that survives a compromised account: an attacker with administrative credentials in the production account can delete instances, snapshots, and backups, but cannot reach a vault in a separate account whose trust policy allows only inbound copies. Cross-Region snapshot copies of an encrypted instance require re-encryption with a KMS key in the destination Region, so you must pre-create and reference a customer managed key there.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\">Finally, the cheap controls: <strong>deletion protection</strong> on every production instance (an API call to delete fails until someone deliberately modifies the instance to turn it off, which is both a speed bump and a CloudTrail signal), and <strong>a final snapshot on deletion</strong>. Pair these with an SCP that denies <code>rds:DeleteDBInstance</code> and <code>rds:DeleteDBClusterSnapshot</code> outside a break-glass role.</p>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">DR patterns by tier</h4>\n<div class=\"code\"><div class=\"code-head\"><span>sql</span><button class=\"copy\" type=\"button\">Copy</button></div><pre><code class=\"hljs\">              RPO          RTO           Relative cost      What <i class=k>is</i> <i class=k>running</i> <i class=k>in</i> DR Region\n<i class=c>----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------</i>\nBackup <i class=o>&amp;</i>      hours        hours<i class=o>-</i>days    <i class=o>~</i><i class=n>5</i><i class=o>%</i>  (storage)     Nothing. Restore quarterly from the DR account into a scratch environment and run application smoke tests, not just \"the instance came up\".</li>\n<li><strong>Measure restore time against your actual data volume.</strong> Restore time grows with size, and for RDS the restored volume <em>lazy-loads</em> from S3, so the instance is available before it is fast — the first queries hit cold blocks. If your RTO includes \"performing acceptably\", budget for the hydration period.</li>\n<li><strong>DNS and routing cutover.</strong> Put your application's database host behind a Route 53 record or a name in Secrets Manager / Parameter Store that you control, never a raw RDS endpoint in a config file. Keep TTLs short (30-60s) on records used for DR.</li>\n<li><strong>Application reconnection.</strong> Pools must detect a dead connection quickly, retry with backoff and jitter, and honor the DNS TTL. Test with a real forced failover (<code>reboot-db-instance --force-failover</code>) in staging and in production during a maintenance window.</li>\n<li><strong>Failback.</strong> The step everyone forgets. After promotion, the old Region is stale. Plan whether you rebuild it as a replica of the new primary and switch back during a quiet window, or simply stay put and make the DR Region the new primary.</li>\n<li><strong>Game days.</strong> Kill an AZ in a test environment. Revoke a KMS key grant. Practice a restore with the on-call who has never done one.</li>\n</ul>\n<h4 style=\"margin-top: 1.5rem; font-size: 1.25rem; font-weight: 600\">Interview scenario: design HA + DR for a payments ledger</h4>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><em>Constraints:</em> 4 TB PostgreSQL ledger, 12,000 write TPS at peak. Business states RPO of 1 minute and RTO of 15 minutes for a Region-level event, zero tolerance for silent data loss, and a budget that will not fund a fully duplicated active/active stack. Regulatory requirement to retain restorable copies for 7 years.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step 1 — separate the problems.</strong> HA inside the Region: Aurora PostgreSQL cluster with a writer plus two readers spread across three AZs. Storage is already 6-way replicated; two readers give both read capacity and a fast promotion target, so in-Region RTO is roughly 30 seconds with RPO zero. This handles instance and AZ loss without paging anyone.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step 2 — Region loss.</strong> RPO of 1 minute and RTO of 15 minutes rules out backup-and-restore (a 4 TB restore will not complete and warm in 15 minutes) and rules out relying on snapshot copies. Aurora Global Database with one secondary Region gives typical RPO around a second and a managed unplanned failover measured in about a minute. Run the secondary as <strong>pilot light</strong>: the global cluster's secondary with one small reader instance, application ASGs at zero desired capacity with baked AMIs and pre-provisioned target groups. That keeps cost near 20-25% of a duplicate stack while meeting RTO — scaling an ASG and warming an ALB is a few minutes, comfortably inside 15.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step 3 — the failure modes replication does not cover.</strong> Aurora Global would faithfully replicate a bad migration. So: backup retention at 35 days for PITR (the fast path for \"we shipped a bug at 14:03, rewind to 14:00\"), Aurora Backtrack is not available for PostgreSQL so PITR-to-a-new-cluster is the mechanism, and a nightly AWS Backup job copying cluster snapshots into a <strong>separate, locked-down backup account</strong> in a third Region with vault lock in compliance mode for the 7-year retention. That account is the ransomware and insider defense: production credentials cannot delete those copies.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step 4 — cutover mechanics.</strong> Application reads the writer host from Secrets Manager, replicated to the DR Region; Route 53 health-checked CNAME with a 30-second TTL fronts the API, not the database. Runbook: declare, trigger Aurora global failover, set ASG desired capacity, verify with a synthetic ledger write and a checksum of the last 1000 transactions, flip Route 53, then post-incident reconcile against the immutable audit log in S3 to enumerate anything inside the RPO window.</p>\n<p style=\"line-height: 1.75\"><strong>Step 5 — prove it.</strong> Quarterly managed <em>planned</em> failover (RPO zero) as a game day, monthly automated restore of the previous night's snapshot in the backup account with row-count and checksum validation, and a tracked metric of \"measured RTO in last drill\" reported to the business alongside the stated objective. If the measured number drifts above 15 minutes, that is a defect, not a surprise.</p>\n<div style=\"margin-top: 1rem; border-left: 4px solid #f97316; padding: 1rem; border-radius: 4px; font-size: 0.9rem\" data-box=\"1\"><strong>🎯 Interview Answer:</strong> \"HA and DR solve different problems.
